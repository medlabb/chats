 ## Dyslipidemia

**Dyslipidemia** refers to abnormal blood lipid levels, including abnormalities of cholesterol, triglycerides, LDL-C, or HDL-C. Higher LDL-C and lower HDL-C are associated with increased coronary heart disease risk. (1)

### Common secondary causes

Before starting lipid-lowering therapy, consider secondary causes because correcting the underlying condition may improve the lipid abnormality. History and physical examination are often sufficient for initial assessment. (2)

Examples include:

- **Hypothyroidism:** increased total cholesterol/LDL-C  
- **Diabetes mellitus:** increased triglycerides and total cholesterol  
- **Alcohol use:** increased triglycerides and HDL-C  
- **Obesity:** increased triglycerides and decreased HDL-C  
- **Chronic kidney disease:** increased total cholesterol and triglycerides  
- **Nephrotic syndrome:** increased total cholesterol  
- **Certain medications**, including diuretics, some β-blockers, corticosteroids, and oral contraceptives  
- **Sedentary lifestyle:** decreased HDL-C  

These associations are summarized in **eTable 33–1**. (2)

### Clinical presentation

Most patients have **no specific symptoms or signs**, and dyslipidemia is usually detected through laboratory testing. (3)

Severe abnormalities may cause:

- **Eruptive xanthomas** with triglycerides above 1000 mg/dL  
- **Tendinous xanthomas** with markedly elevated LDL-C, often suggesting an inherited lipid disorder  
- **Lipemia retinalis** with triglycerides above 2000 mg/dL  

These findings are illustrated in **Figure 33–1** and **eFigure 33–2**. (3)

### Genetic disorders

Important inherited conditions include:

- **Familial hypercholesterolemia:** markedly elevated LDL-C and premature atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease  
- **Familial chylomicronemia syndrome:** severe hypertriglyceridemia, recurrent pancreatitis, and hepatosplenomegaly  
- **Dysbetalipoproteinemia**, familial combined hyperlipidemia, and sitosterolemia (4)

Referral to a lipidologist or preventive cardiologist is recommended for known genetic lipid disorders, a striking family history of hyperlipidemia or premature atherosclerosis, or extremely abnormal LDL-C, triglyceride, lipoprotein(a), or HDL-C levels. (5)

### Management principles

- **Dietary therapy:** A Mediterranean diet emphasizing replacement of saturated fats with monounsaturated fats may lower LDL-C. Soluble fiber may reduce LDL-C by 5–10%, and plant stanols/sterols by about 10%. Dietary effects should be reassessed approximately 4–12 weeks after initiation. (6)
- **Risk reduction:** Sustained weight loss and increased physical activity can improve triglycerides and other cardiometabolic risk factors. (7)
- **Statins:** Statin therapy reduces cardiovascular events, with benefit generally proportional to the achieved LDL-C reduction. (1)
- **PCSK9 inhibitors:** Evolocumab and alirocumab can reduce LDL-C by approximately 50–60% and are generally used for high-risk patients when statins are insufficient or not tolerated. (1)
