##### Treatment

Treatment involves surgery and antibiotics. Early surgical decompression and drainage improve the long-term outlook and should be considered when MRI shows evidence of cord compression or when any neurologic deficit is progressing. With the advent of MRI, however, spinal epidural abscess is diagnosed increasingly before it has compressed the spinal cord and at a time when treatment with intravenous antibiotics alone is successful. [Nafcillin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660408) or [vancomycin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660261) is administered to cover staphylococcal or streptococcal infection, and a third- or fourth-generation cephalosporin such as [ceftazidime](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659207) or [cefepime](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660650), respectively, to cover gram-negative infections; other agents are added or substituted based on the clinical context and results of Gram stain of excised material. The results of culture of the necrotic material that makes up the abscess may subsequently alter the antibiotic regimen. The antibiotic dosages are those used to treat bacterial meningitis, as given in [Chapter 4](/content.aspx?legacysectionid=greenberg11_ch4), Confusional States. Intravenous antibiotics are usually continued for 4-6 weeks, but treatment for 6-8 weeks is required in the presence of vertebral osteomyelitis or when the response to treatment has been slow.

Follow-up MRI is obtained after ~4 weeks when the patient is improving but at any time if deterioration is occurring.


##### Prognosis

Uncontrolled sepsis may result in a fatal outcome. Delayed diagnosis or treatment and suboptimal management may lead to irreversible paraparesis or paraplegia, which occurs in up to 20% of cases, depending on the series. The most important prognostic indicator is the patient’s clinical status before decompressive surgery; the more severe the preoperative deficit, the less recovery is to be expected. Delayed diagnosis is usually reflected by a more severe deficit and thus a poorer prognosis.


#### ACUTE TRANSVERSE MYELITIS

This syndrome results from a variety of infectious (bacterial, viral, fungal, parasitic) and noninfectious inflammatory disorders (multiple sclerosis, neuromyelitis optica, acute disseminated encephalomyelitis, systemic autoimmune diseases, idiopathic) that produce anatomic and functional disruption of the spinal cord. Children and young adults are affected most often. Clinical findings include bilateral sensory, motor, and autonomic deficits in the limbs and trunk; a discrete sensory level corresponding to the site of inflammation in the spinal cord; a course of hours to days; an inflammatory CSF profile (pleocytosis and/or increased IgG index; accompanying low CSF glucose suggests an infective cause); and an MRI showing an intrinsic spinal cord lesion that usually enhances with gadolinium administration. Clinically, some cases may have an asymmetric or even unilateral presentation, and a sensory level is sometimes hard to appreciate.

The MRI is particularly helpful in distinguishing these possibilities. Compressive lesions of the spinal cord, such as by tumor, hemorrhage, or spinal epidural abscess, can be seen. Lesions with a vascular distribution or in a watershed area suggest an ischemic stroke. An arteriovenous fistula and venous congestion suggest a vascular malformation, while T-2 hyperintensities in the region of spinal degenerative changes (eg, disc herniation, spondylolisthesis, or osteophytic lesions) suggest a spondylotic myelopathy.

Treatment of acute transverse myelopathy is with glucocorticoids, typically [methylprednisolone](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660280) (1 g intravenously daily for 3-5 days), although their benefit has not been rigorously established. Plasma exchange, intravenous immunoglobulins, or [cyclophosphamide](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659897) may be useful in glucocorticoid-unresponsive patients, but their utility remains to be established. Patients tend to improve over several months, but may have residual deficits, and mortality rates >30% have been reported. Recurrences may occur, depending on the underlying cause.


#### ACUTE FLACCID MYELITIS

Acute flaccid limb weakness and areflexia develop over a few hours or days in patients with evidence on imaging of involvement of spinal cord gray matter. Any or all of the limbs may be affected, but involvement of the upper limbs is most common. Sensory deficits are present occasionally; cranial nerve (especially facial nerve) palsies and mental changes may occur. Electrodiagnostic studies confirm the presence of anterior horn cell disease, typically without sensory involvement. The CSF shows a mild pleocytosis (>5 white cells/mm<sup>3</sup>) and slightly elevated protein content. MRI shows primarily nonenhancing, longitudinally extensive (≥1 segment) hyperintensities on T2 and fluid-attenuated inversion recovery series in the gray matter of the spinal cord, with perilesional edema; hyperintensities may also occur in the dorsal brainstem. With time, the signal abnormalities become more defined in the anterior horn cell region.


 
The disorder occurs predominantly in children and is often preceded by a mild febrile illness with respiratory or gastrointestinal symptoms, and sometimes with neck stiffness, headache, and limb pain.

Acute attacks are treated with intravenous [methylprednisolone](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660280) (1 g daily) for 5 days, followed by an oral [prednisone](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660281) taper (1 mg/kg/d for 1 week, with rapid reduction over the ensuing 1-2 weeks). If the response is poor, plasmapheresis is undertaken. Treatment with intravenous immunoglobulins is generally unhelpful. Intravenously administered [eculizumab](/drug.aspx?gbosId=658995), a complement inhibitor that is an FDA-approved therapy for NMO-IgG positive cases, has reduced the annualized relapse rate from 35% to 2% compared to placebo. Prior vaccination against meningococcal infection is necessary. [Inebilizumab](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659499), a humanized anti-CD19 antibody that depletes B cells, has also reduced the relapse rate but is not yet approved by the FDA. #### ACUTE DISSEMINATED ENCEPHALOMYELITIS


##### Pathogenesis

Neurologic symptoms and signs develop over a few days in association with a nonspecific viral infection, after immunization, or without obvious antecedents, when it then may represent the initial manifestation of multiple sclerosis. Pathologically, perivascular areas of demyelination are scattered throughout the brain and spinal cord, with an associated inflammatory reaction. Lesions are all of similar age, and the brain appears swollen.
