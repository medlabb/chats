### DISTURBANCES OF BEHAVIOR

#### Aggression, Hostility, Impulsiveness, and Violence

The spectrum of aggressive emotions and behaviors is characterized by heightened vigilance in response to a sense of threat and enhanced readiness to attack. Physiologic tone may be geared for a fight. Assertiveness, the adaptive aspect of these emotions, includes sensing that something needs to be done and feeling willing and competent to take constructive action. The manner and extent to which aggressive emotions can be expressed varies from society to society and from situation to situation. These emotions are among the most carefully regulated because of their potential destructiveness. Acts of aggression are on a continuum, beginning with irritability, progressing to verbal threats and intimidation, and extending to physical bullying and assault to homicide, sometimes including acts of calculated violence and sadism.

Irritability is an unpleasant feeling state characterized by inner unease. In contrast to anger, irritability does not lessen after an outburst. Often, others are more aware of an individual’s irritability than is the person themself. It is diagnostically nonspecific, seen in a variety of physiologic states—psychotic, anxiety, and mood disorders—and as a lifelong temperamental quality. Hunger, sleepiness, sexual frustration, and pain are among the physiologic triggers commonly associated with irritability.

Individual differences in the tendency toward experiencing and expressing anger and violence are biologic, developmental, and cultural in origin. Some infants are irritable from birth. Subtle early birth injuries and brain anoxia may increase the susceptibility of some people to be violent. Furthermore, studies of EEG patterns in violent people show increased abnormalities, especially in those with repeated violence and violence with little or no obvious motive. Soft neurologic signs are also seen in violent criminals. Biochemically, low cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) 5-HIAA has been associated with a variety of impulsive behaviors, such as violent crimes, recurrent fire setting, and violent suicide attempts.

Consistent with the hypothesis that an inverse relationship exists between central serotonergic system function and impulsive, aggressive behavior, a few double-blind, placebo-controlled studies in patients with cluster B personality disorders have demonstrated that enhancing central serotonergic function by using SSRIs reduces irritability and impulsive aggressive behavior.

A 32-year-old man came for psychiatric treatment after a breakup with his fiancée of 5 years related to his anger outbursts, in which he would curse, yell, slap, and throw things at her. Once, during a rage attack, he slashed the tires of his fiancée’s car. Another time, he impulsively took an overdose of pills that he found at home. He responded to treatment with an SSRI, which improved his mood lability and anger outbursts.

#### Anger

When anger begins surfacing during interactions with medical or mental health staff, it is useful to acknowledge this expressed emotion with a supportive approach instead of attempting to talk the patient out of any feelings they have. It is useful to pay attention to the interactions with family members, who usually feel uncomfortable with expressed negative emotions, and try to redirect the patient to avoid talking about them. Some family members may express their own anger, fueling the patient’s emotions.

Although nursing staff can handle and provide opportunities for the patient’s expression and abreaction of angry feelings, it is sometimes advisable that a psychotherapist talks with the patient about their feelings and meets with the patient regularly, even for a short period. On the surface, patients’ anger is directed outward toward causes of the accident, missing work, being in pain, being hospitalized, and perceived inconsiderate treatment by hospital staff. Patients often avoid expressing anger toward the hospital staff as they depend on them. The ultimate object of the anger may be “self.” Patients blame themselves for being in the situation in which they were burned whether or not there is validity to this idea. This is not easy to accept, and the patient may displace their anger toward themselves or toward family members. They may push away their family and even their health care team by utilizing acting out behavior, projection, and projective identification. A differential diagnosis in terms of potential causes of mood changes or mood disorders needs to be considered and pharmacologic interventions implemented when needed. Patients are really angry at themselves for either their role in the burn injury (real or imaginary), their lack of ability to avoid the situation, losses they “caused,” for not recovering fast enough, for needing pain medication, for not tolerating the hospitalization, and so forth. As these feelings are unconscious and unacceptable to the patient, they are displaced onto external objects, sometimes in excess.

Interventions for agitation should be conceptualized as hierarchal, beginning with the least restrictive and least intrusive interventions and only using the most restrictive interventions when all other treatment approaches have been exhausted. The core clinical strategy for managing agitation is the use of interpersonal strategies that emphasize verbal deescalation interventional techniques. It is important for the clinician to reduce the patient’s anxiety and fear by maintaining a humane and respectful disposition. A patient who is treated with honesty, dignity, and respect is likely to respond with reciprocity if there is retained reality testing. Affect management is central to any effective aggression management technique. It involves acknowledging the patient’s affect, validating it when appropriate, and encouraging the patient to talk about their feelings. Attempting to use logic and rational thought with a patient who is effectually dysregulated may lead to a further increase in agitation. An emotionally distraught patient requires an active response from the clinician. The use of active listening techniques, such as paraphrasing to the patient a brief encapsulated form of their statements, helps convey that the clinician is attempting to understand what the patient is experiencing. It is important to be honest and unambiguous. Verbal deescalation techniques require time and are often sufficient to reduce agitation and eliminate the risk of violence. Offering food or drink may also help facilitate a therapeutic alliance and reduce a patient’s agitation. Management of aggressive patients may be compromised when the clinician inadvertently evokes feelings of fear, anger, or rejection. This can generate thoughts of retaliation aimed toward the clinician. If identified, the clinician should stop the interview and ask another staff member to continue with the therapeutic intervention.

Patients with a history of documented or witnessed assaultive behaviors should be provided a significant amount of space by all clinical providers. A general rule of thumb is to keep at minimum two quick steps or at least two-legs lengths distance from the patient. Personal space can be visualized as an oval zone extending 4 to 6 ft all around. If the patient is standing, then the clinician should stand. If the patient is sitting, then the clinician should also sit down, preferably at the same level as the patient. The clinician should never stand over the patient during the interview as this may further escalate patients and places the clinician in a compromised position for potential harm. If the patient is pacing, then the clinician can walk with the patient but at a much slower pace. The clinician should take a posture that makes them appear small and thus less threatening. This can be done by holding the hands at waist level with palms up and open. This posture ensures the patient, especially if paranoid, does not misperceive the intentions of the clinician and ensures that there are no syringes or other devices of perceived harm in the practitioner’s hands. Assuming the “Thinker” stance (one forearm crosses the chest, and the opposite elbow rests on it with the index finger on the cheek or chin) is also an effective technique. In this position, the patient’s content can be validated by affect but also ensure the practitioner is ready for any unexpected behaviors by the patient. Some patients may not respond or only partially respond to verbal intervention and will require psychotropic medication to aid in behavioral management.

Aggressive acts can be reactive (in response to a trigger or perceived insult) or instrumental (planned with intent to harm or to use aggression to get something). Reactive aggression is unplanned, unprofitable, and poorly controlled. Reactive aggression may be triggered by events that appear benign to adults, like a school staff putting a hand on the child’s shoulder to calm them, but which feel intrusive or threatening to the child due to their background and history. Children with autism, ID, and learning disabilities may become aggressive when frustrated because they lack the verbal skills to solve the problem and express themselves, or because they do not know any other way to ask for what they want. On the other hand, instrumental aggression is planned, profitable, and self-controlled. It often represents a reaction to narcissistic injury or stems from a belief that aggressive behavior is an appropriate means to gain desired end or exert power.

Management. Once the initial agitation is addressed, the clinician’s job is to determine whether the child can be safely discharged. Risk for future violence depends in part on the nature of the violence, and on the presence or absence of underlying psychiatric illness. Evidence of planned or premeditated aggression, homicidal intent, weapons involvement, or a history of severe or escalating violence, all confer an increased risk. Risk may be influenced by parental disciplinary practices, child-rearing style, and history of substance use or criminality. Risk may also be elevated by social factors such as association with gangs or delinquent peers.

Management must be tailored based on the nature of the aggressive behavior, whether it stems from an underlying psychiatric illness, and the level of risk. Youth with a range of psychiatric illness can be prone to reactive aggression, and the treatment for each must be targeted to the illness or symptoms present ([Table 35.4–4](#tt35-4-4)). Instrumental aggression is less common in mentally ill youth, but can be seen in conduct disorder and substance use.

Psychosocial interventions and behavioral strategies are central and should be considered first-line treatment. When aggression is reactive, the treatment involves identifying triggers, teaching skills to cope with those triggers without aggression, and addressing the core symptoms that predispose the youth to aggressive behaviors. Children with impulsive aggression benefit from parent–child skills training programs and the effective parental use of verbal warnings, prompting, “time outs,” and other behavioral interventions. Treatment of underlying conditions (such as ADHD, autism, or PTSD) is also important.

*Mindfulness exposure* is often part of interventions having both relaxation and an attention-focusing component. *Practicing deeper breathing*, a mindfulness platform, is sometimes called “watching the breath” or diaphragmatic breathing. The following guidelines are useful:

1. Sitting in a straight-back chair or lying down on a firm surface.

2. Closed eyes may be preferred.

3. Focus on breathing with attention to the abdomen/belly with a hand on it.

4. Relax the abdominal area and breathe slowly.

5. Slowly inhale through the nose for about a count of approximately 4 to 6.

6. Notice the abdomen rise; a hand on the stomach helps your awareness of this.

7. Try to hold the breath for about a count of 6 to 7.

8. Slowly exhale through the mouth or nose for a count slightly longer than your inhalation, for example, a count of 8.

To restate the breathing technique, in starting the breathing exercise, allot about 2 to 5 minutes to establish a calm breathing baseline that may or may not be maintained. While seated and comfortable, erect posture with the spine vertically aligned, take a deep breath through the nose sensing, and noticing its course to expand the chest via the rise in the upper abdomen and diaphragm. A count of 4 to 6 is right. Putting a hand on the upper waist/belly helps center attention to this upward rise. Hold the breath in for a count of approximately 6 to 7, then gently exhale through the mouth or the nose, whichever is more comfortable in the beginning. The precise timing of in-breaths, holding, and out-breaths varies. In-breaths are shortest, out-breaths are most extended, and time holding the breath is in between these. Doing this breathing practice for 5 minutes once or more times per day is helpful. Physiologically, arterial baroreceptors are stimulated and modulate blood pressure. Also, the vagus nerve afferent stimulation to the frontal cortex increases parasympathetic tone—greater tranquility.

Given the fundamental platform of breathing, a summary of the essence of this *Learned Mindfulness* model founded on emotional intelligence, mindful awareness, and self-observation follows. Starting with a basic familiarity of how one’s sensations emerge, one can be taught to label them as feelings. Grasping, releasing, contracting, and expanding attend these. Breathing affords a model of such fundamental dynamics. For example, releasing the breath follows the pause between inhalation and exhalation. This act establishes a sense of muting and not impulsively reacting that transfers to grasping, holding, and releasing objects arising in the mind. The emerging realization of gracefully letting go can progress further in the refinement of emotional processing: perception, conception, and performance. This emotional intelligence practice correlates the mind’s “objects,” for example, sensations, feelings, and thoughts, with their mindful management.

When aggression is instrumental, treatment involves setting clear consequences, often in collaboration with parents and law enforcement, while promoting prosocial behavior with positive reinforcements. Youth with instrumental aggression often benefit from interventions such as multisystemic therapy (MST), which addresses family relationships, parent–child communication, parenting skills, school involvement, and peer activities.

If medication is necessary, it should comprise only one aspect of a comprehensive treatment approach and should be used to address the core conditions and symptoms that predispose the youth to aggressive behaviors. While the use of emergency medication may at times be required (as discussed above), the use of PRN medication is not considered standard outpatient treatment for aggression in youth, and should be avoided. If severe aggressive behavior persists despite adequate medication trials to target the underlying psychiatric condition, antipsychotic and/or mood-stabilizing medication may be considered.

Determining whether inpatient hospitalization is indicated depends both on immediacy of risk for further aggression, and on whether the risk for aggression is due to mental illness and will be helped by hospitalization. There are youths who are engaging in criminal behavior not due to psychiatric illness, and for them, inpatient hospitalization may not be helpful; in such cases, clinicians should collaborate with the parents and law enforcement to identify other supports to contain the youth’s behavior. If the child’s aggression stems from abuse at home or if the family cannot safely supervise the youth, a referral to child protective services may be necessary.

If the aggressive behavior is rooted in psychiatric illness yet there is no imminent risk of harm or significant change in the youth’s mental status and the youth’s family can contain the behavior, the youth may be safely discharged with outpatient treatment. Prior to discharge, clinicians should engage the youth in creating a safety plan. Similar to safety plans for suicidality (see above), safety plans for aggression should identify triggers and warning signs for aggressive behaviors, coping skills to safely manage situations that may lead to aggression, and supports. Clinicians should also help families establish a crisis or emergency plan and provide 24-hour crisis hotlines in case the youth’s aggressive behavior worsens. Active family engagement is critical to the successful management of youth aggressive behavior. Families of aggressive children may by struggling with stigma or feelings of overwhelm, guilt, and shame. Moreover, their child’s behavior may have compromised their ability to obtain community support. Providing referrals for support groups, parent advocates, and educational resources will enhance the family’s support and facilitate their ability to engage in their child’s treatment.

The pathologic childhood triad of bed-wetting past the age of 6 years, setting fires, and torturing animals has been associated with subsequent violent behavior in adults. Interpersonally, studies show that violence-prone individuals require more personal space around their physical person than do others. Violent individuals feel threatened when approached too closely, particularly from the rear.

Psychological and social contributions are also strong. Violence in families breeds violence, and battered children often grow up to be battering adults. Cultural norms for the expression of violence differ considerably. In some socioeconomic and ethnic groups, violent gangs organize the energies of many adolescent youth. For some, violent behavior is an adolescent socialization pattern necessary to prove one’s manhood or womanhood. Like other social organizations, violent gangs have detailed rules that inhibit and govern the expression of violence. Some unpredictable and unsocialized violent people are too violent to be contained even in gangs.

Aggressive and violent behavior is diagnostically nonspecific. Violence in schizophrenia may occur as a consequence of paranoid delusions, in response to command auditory hallucinations, or secondary to passivity experiences. Manic patients and those in mixed states may be violent, often in response to minimal provocation. Violent behavior commonly occurs in patients with antisocial and borderline personalities (in the latter often self-directed as well as other-directed). Violent behavior may occur in epilepsy, although rarely during true ictal periods; in frontal lobe syndromes as a “release phenomenon;” and in association with abused substances, particularly disinhibiting sedatives, such as alcohol, or stimulants, such as amphetamines and cocaine, which increase irritability, aggressiveness, and paranoia.

Impulsive violence may be provoked by a number of stimuli and situations. Alcohol is perhaps the most common disinhibitor of violence. Intrafamilial violence, the most common setting for homicide, is frequently related to alcohol intoxication. In episodic dyscontrol and intermittent explosive disorder, violent behaviors typically erupt after a person has ingested alcohol, a phenomenon known as pathologic intoxication. In these often ferocious outbursts, the individual may confront or provoke any potential target for violence, including total strangers and police, but significant others, spouses, and parents are frequent victims. Patients with episodic dyscontrol commonly have histories of violent sexual behavior, including rape and, often while intoxicated, speeding and reckless driving, sometimes chasing down, stopping, and attacking other motorists who they believe “get in their way.”
