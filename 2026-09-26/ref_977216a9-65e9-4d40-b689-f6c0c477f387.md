# Chapter 68: Primary and Secondary Hemophagocytic Lymphohistiocytosis

## INTRODUCTION

Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) is a highly life-threatening condition of massive, uncontrolled inflammation (a hyperinflammation). It exists in a primary (genetic) form and a secondary (acquired) form, and is often triggered by infections, malignancies, and autoimmune diseases. HLH triggered by autoimmune conditions is also referred to as macrophage activation syndrome (MAS or MAS-HLH). While the underlying factors are biologically very different, the clinical picture of all forms of HLH is quite similar, with a sepsis-like inflammation often leading to multiple organ failure and death if not treated promptly and appropriately (convergent phenotype). Primary HLH mostly affects children, whereas secondary HLH is far more common in adults. The current chapter will present primary and secondary HLH separately. ## PRIMARY HEMOPHAGOCYTIC LYMPHOHISTIOCYTOSIS

Primary HLH comprises Mendelian-inherited conditions causing HLH. The most common form, by far, is familial HLH (FHL), which is associated with lymphocyte cytotoxicity defects.

Other forms of primary HLH associated with lymphocyte cytotoxicity defects are Griscelli syndrome type 2 (associated with hypopigmentation, silver-colored hair, and immune defects due to mutation in *RAB27A*, a *RAS* gene family member), Chédiak-Higashi syndrome, and X-linked lymphoproliferative syndrome (XLP) type 1. Another group of primary HLHs are associated with abnormalities of inflammasome activation, inborn errors of immunity, and inborn errors of metabolism.

### FAMILIAL HEMOPHAGOCYTIC LYMPHOHISTIOCYTOSIS

If not treated, FHL is rapidly fatal, with a median survival of 1–2 months. The incidence of FHL has been estimated to be 1 in 50,000 live births. Together with severe combined immunodeficiency, it is the most common fatal inherited human immunodeficiency. Because its inheritance is autosomal recessive, it is more common in regions where consanguinity is frequent; it is equally common in males and females. The median age at onset is between 3 and 6 months. It may be diagnosed at birth or even in utero, whereas less severe forms may result in onset during adolescence and adulthood.

#### Clinical and Laboratory Features

A typical presentation is a fever, often in an infant, with a sepsis-like condition associated with cytopenias, in particular thrombocytopenia, and hepatosplenomegaly. If blood cultures are negative and signs of malignancy are absent, HLH should be considered and assessed for promptly.

##### HEMATOLOGIC AND BONE MARROW FINDINGS

Thrombocytopenia is frequent, and the platelet levels may be extremely low. Anemia and neutropenia are also common, and bicytopenia (i.e., at least two of these three lineages affected) is a diagnostic criterion.

Hemophagocytosis is not always present, particularly in the early phase of the disease, and hence, serial examinations over time may be helpful. However, hemophagocytosis is not specific for HLH. Over time, the bone marrow may become hypocellular.

##### LIVER INVOLVEMENT AND COAGULOPATHY

Ferritin and transaminase levels are almost always elevated. Ferritin, an acute-phase reactant (i.e., a marker of inflammation), can serve as a rapid and easily available screening test for HLH, although it is not specific for the disease. Icterus, with a predominantly conjugated hyperbilirubinemia, and elevated γ-glutamyl transferase levels are also common. HLH may resemble acute liver failure, and in histopathologic specimens, an image similar to chronic persistent hepatitis may be found. Disseminated intravascular coagulation, severe acute bleeding, and low levels of [fibrinogen](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659057) are frequent.

##### CENTRAL NERVOUS SYSTEM INVOLVEMENT

About one a third of children with FHL have neurologic symptoms at diagnosis, which may be severe, and around half have abnormal cerebrospinal fluid with elevated cell and/or protein content. Common symptoms include seizures, decreased consciousness, and signs of meningismus, as well as irritability and hypotonia in neonates and infants. Cranial nerve palsies and ataxia may also occur. Isolated neurologic symptoms may be the first presentation of FHL, particularly in older children and adolescents. Magnetic resonance imaging findings are nonspecific.

##### OTHER CLINICAL AND LABORATORY FINDINGS



Petechia, purpura, and a nonspecific transient maculopapular rash are frequent. Lymph node enlargement develops in about half of the children.

### DIAGNOSIS

Secondary HLH, despite being life-threatening and treatable, is markedly underdiagnosed. Awareness of the diagnosis is, therefore, essential. As in primary HLH, elevated ferritin levels may serve as a rapid and readily available screening test.

In contrast to primary HLH, no exact or specific diagnostic test is available for secondary HLH. For diagnostic assistance, combinations of clinical and laboratory criteria are often used, such as the HLH-2004 criteria presented above and the HScore. Although the diagnosis of HLH typically is based on fulfilment of five HLH-2004 criteria, many centers find four fulfilled criteria sufficient to establish the diagnosis in secondary HLH. The HScore is a weighted scoring system based on nine variables: fever, organomegaly, known underlying immunosuppression, number of cytopenias, ferritin, triglycerides, [fibrinogen](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659057), aspartate aminotransferase, and hemophagocytosis. Both HLH-2004 criteria and HScore have good diagnostic accuracy when evaluated in critically ill adults.

A note of caution: The underlying disease associated with secondary HLH itself can sometimes result in several HLH-2004 criteria being fulfilled, which may complicate diagnostic reasoning.

### MALIGNANCY-ASSOCIATED HEMOPHAGOCYTIC LYMPHOHISTIOCYTOSIS

Malignancy-associated HLH (mal-HLH) is likely underdiagnosed; it is reported to be almost as common as infection-associated HLH in adults, but it is rare in children. It is most frequent in young adult males, affecting about 2.5% of their hematologic malignancies. Mal-HLH is the form of secondary HLH with the worst prognosis, with about 20–30% 2-year survival, mainly owing to poor survival associated with the underlying malignancies. One-month survival, mainly representing the HLH episode, has been reported to be around 70%.

Mal-HLH comes in two forms: “malignancy-triggered HLH,” when HLH is diagnosed at the time of cancer diagnosis before initiation of cancer therapy, and HLH that occurs during chemotherapy. The latter often has an infectious trigger and may develop years after initiation of chemotherapy.

Lymphomas are found in about half of all cases, followed by leukemias (30%) and other hematologic malignancies (10%), whereas underlying solid tumors are less frequent (10%). The triggering malignancy may be very difficult to identify. If one does not find the cause of a secondary HLH, searching repeatedly for an underlying (occult) malignancy is prudent. Repeated bone marrow examinations, biopsies, and various radiologic examinations may be required.

A soluble IL-2 receptor alpha/ferritin ratio >2.0 has been reported to have a positive predictive value of 85% that a patient with HLH has lymphoma-associated HLH, whereas a low ratio is more common in patients with non–mal-HLH.

### PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

The cause of secondary HLH is multifactorial, but it often has a dominating underlying cause. According to “the threshold model,” the factors triggering and unbalancing the immune system accumulate, eventually reaching a threshold at which inflammation becomes uncontrolled and fulminant HLH develops **([Fig. 68-2](#hpim22_ch68fg2))**. Diverse pathways and etiologies can thus result in the same common end stage of HLH. Although secondary HLH is not an inherited disorder, whole-genome sequencing has revealed variably pathogenic monoallelic HLH-associated genetic variants in some patients. As in primary HLH, inflammatory cytokines play a crucial role in the development of symptoms and signs in secondary HLH. The cytokine pattern is different in the various forms of secondary HLH, which can be used diagnostically.

#### Lymphocyte Cytotoxicity

In contrast to familial HLH, which is characterized by inherited defects in cytotoxic cells but normal numbers of cytotoxic cells, the number of circulating NK cells and cytotoxic T cells is often reduced in secondary HLH. As a consequence, the overall lymphocyte cytotoxic function can be deficient, as in familial HLH.
