### BUSPIRONE

Buspirone (Buspar), an azapirone derivative and a serotonin-1A receptor partial agonist, is a nonbenzodiazepine anxiolytic that is more efficacious than a placebo in the treatment of GAD. It is not indicated in the treatment of other anxiety disorders. Although buspirone and benzodiazepines have comparable efficacy in patients with GAD who persist with treatment, several studies have found that patients are more likely to discontinue buspirone than a benzodiazepine for reasons other than side effects. It has been suggested that the higher rate of discontinuation of buspirone compared with a benzodiazepine may be due to buspirone’s delayed onset of action.

Although buspirone is an efficacious treatment for GAD, it has a limited role in the treatment of generalized anxiety in late life. First, it is not a substitute for antidepressant medication in the treatment of generalized anxiety that is comorbid with, or secondary to, major depression. Second, because buspirone has a delayed onset of action, it is not suitable as an acute adjunctive treatment for generalized anxiety symptoms associated with major depression. Third, there is evidence that buspirone may have diminished efficacy in persons who have previously been treated with a benzodiazepine. Therefore, its usefulness in the management of older adult patients with chronic GAD, many of whom have been treated with benzodiazepines, is unclear. Finally, the twice-a-day to three-times-a-day dosing schedule of buspirone increases the risk of poor adherence to this medication.

Unlike benzodiazepines, buspirone does not cause sedation, psychomotor impairment, cognitive impairment, or depressed respiratory drive. This side-effect profile suggests that it would be preferable to a benzodiazepine in the ongoing management of generalized anxiety in nondepressed patients who have chronic obstructive airway disease, sleep apnea, or a neurologic disorder. However, select antidepressants may also be effective for anxiety and well tolerated in these patients.

The usual starting dose of buspirone in older patients is 5 mg twice a day, followed by dose increments of 5 mg every 4 to 5 days to reach a target dose of 20 to 30 mg/day, given in divided doses. If a person fails to show significant improvement after 4 to 6 weeks at this target dose, the dose can be further gradually increased to a maximum of 45 mg/day, as tolerated. The main side effects of buspirone are dizziness, headache, and nausea. Gradual dose titration is essential in order to minimize the risk of these effects.

Sometimes a physician elects to switch a patient from long-term benzodiazepine therapy to buspirone. Because buspirone has a delayed onset of action and does not suppress benzodiazepine withdrawal symptoms, a patient should not be abruptly switched from a benzodiazepine to buspirone. Instead, the patient should take a therapeutic dose of buspirone for 2 to 4 weeks before the benzodiazepine is gradually tapered. Despite this approach, some patients are less satisfied with the anxiolytic effect of buspirone compared with that of a benzodiazepine.
