### [ASPIRIN](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660322)

The most widely used antiplatelet agent worldwide is [aspirin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660322). As a cheap and effective antiplatelet drug, [aspirin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660322) serves as the foundation of most antiplatelet strategies.

#### Mechanism of Action

[Aspirin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660322) produces its antithrombotic effect by irreversibly acetylating and inhibiting platelet cyclooxygenase (COX)-1 **([Fig. 123-3](#hpim22_ch123fg3))**, a critical enzyme in the biosynthesis of thromboxane A<sub>2</sub>. At high doses (~1 g/d), [aspirin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660322) also inhibits COX-2, an inducible COX isoform found in endothelial cells and inflammatory cells. In endothelial cells, COX-2 initiates the synthesis of prostacyclin, a potent vasodilator and inhibitor of platelet aggregation.

#### Indications

[Aspirin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660322) is widely used for secondary prevention of cardiovascular events in patients with established coronary artery, cerebral artery, or peripheral artery disease. Compared with placebo in this setting, [aspirin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660322) produces a 25% reduction in the risk of cardiovascular death, MI, or stroke. [Aspirin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660322) is no longer used routinely for primary prevention because recent studies suggest that the risk of gastrointestinal and intracerebral hemorrhage outweigh the benefits. Consequently, [aspirin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660322) is only recommended for primary cardiac prevention if the baseline cardiovascular risk is at least 1% per year over 10 years and patients are at low risk for bleeding.

#### Dosages

[Aspirin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660322) is usually administered at doses of 75–325 mg once daily. Higher doses of [aspirin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660322) are not more effective than lower [aspirin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660322) doses, and some analyses suggest reduced efficacy with higher doses. Because the side effects of [aspirin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660322) are dose-related, daily [aspirin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660322) doses of 75–100 mg are recommended for most indications. When rapid platelet inhibition is required, an initial [aspirin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660322) dose of at least 160 mg should be given.

#### Side Effects

The most common side effects are gastrointestinal and range from dyspepsia to erosive gastritis or peptic ulcers with bleeding and perforation. These side effects are dose related. Use of enteric-coated or buffered [aspirin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660322) in place of plain [aspirin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660322) does not eliminate gastrointestinal side effects. The overall risk of major bleeding with [aspirin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660322) is 1–3% per year. The risk of bleeding is increased two- to threefold when [aspirin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660322) is given in conjunction with other antiplatelet drugs, such as [clopidogrel](/drug.aspx?gbosId=658818) or [ticagrelor](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659122), or with anticoagulants, such as [warfarin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660008). When dual or triple therapy is prescribed, low-dose [aspirin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660322) should be given (75–100 mg daily). Eradication of *Helicobacter pylori* infection and administration of proton pump inhibitors may reduce the risk of aspirin-induced upper gastrointestinal bleeding in patients with peptic ulcer disease.

## GENERAL GUIDELINES FOR UTILIZATION OF ANTIPLATELET THERAPY

1. [Aspirin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660322) remains the most common antiplatelet agent utilized in a wide range of indications for therapies, as well as for primary and secondary prevention. Through its inactivation of cyclooxygenase enzymes, [aspirin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660322) mediates antithrombotic effects and leads to platelet inactivation (it also can mediate anti-inflammatory effects through similar mechanisms, although therapeutic uses based on these effects are outside of the scope of this chapter). [Aspirin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660322) use can be complemented or, in some situations, substituted by other antiplatelet agents. The typical dose utilized for cardiovascular indications is 75–100 mg daily; in the United States, this is typically 81 mg. In cases of acute myocardial infarction or acute coronary syndrome, [aspirin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660322) is typically loaded with a dose of 325 mg to achieve more rapid platelet inhibition.

2. [Adenosine](/drug.aspx?gbosId=658912) diphosphate (ADP) receptor inhibitors (also known as P2Y<sub>12</sub> receptor inhibitors) are the next most common group of antiplatelet medications and include [clopidogrel](/drug.aspx?gbosId=658818), [ticagrelor](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659122), [prasugrel](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659056), [cangrelor](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659218), and ticlopidine. Of these, [clopidogrel](/drug.aspx?gbosId=658818), [ticagrelor](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659122), and [prasugrel](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659056) are the typically used oral agents and are the agents most often combined with [aspirin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660322) for dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) regimens. [Ticagrelor](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659122) and [prasugrel](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659056) have shown greater efficacy compared to [clopidogrel](/drug.aspx?gbosId=658818). Ticlopidine is rarely used in contemporary practice due to potential side effect of bone marrow suppression. [Clopidogrel](/drug.aspx?gbosId=658818), [ticagrelor](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659122), [prasugrel](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659056), and ticlopidine are oral agents, whereas [cangrelor](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659218) is administered intravenously. [Cangrelor](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659218) can be utilized in situations requiring bridging antiplatelet therapy due to its rapid inhibition of platelet function and the rapid return of platelet function 1 h after discontinuation. It can also be utilized in patients unable to take or absorb the enteral ADP receptor inhibitors. In the setting of percutaneous coronary interventions (PCI), a loading dose for the ADP receptor inhibitors [clopidogrel](/drug.aspx?gbosId=658818), [ticagrelor](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659122), and [prasugrel](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659056) is given to induce more rapid platelet inhibition. The agent is then continued at a daily maintenance dose.

3. Glycoprotein (GP) IIb/IIIa inhibitors (including abciximab, [eptifibatide](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660667), and [tirofiban](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660687)) are IV medications that are typically used as adjunctive agents to [aspirin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660322) and/or ADP receptor inhibitors, typically in patients with ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) or non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI) who are undergoing PCI to prevent acute thrombotic complications. The studies evaluating GP IIb/IIIa inhibitors have largely been conducted prior to routine use of ADP receptor inhibitors, and thus, contemporary practice has shifted away from routine GP IIb/IIIa inhibitor use. Current practice is to utilize these agents in cases where higher-risk features are present, including significant coronary thrombus burden; evidence of significant distal embolization, often identified by slow or no-reflow phenomena; or evidence of coronary dissection. These agents are infused during the time of the procedure and up to 18 h afterward.
