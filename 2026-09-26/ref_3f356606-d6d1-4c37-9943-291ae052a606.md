## 12.12 Schizophrenia: Pharmacologic Treatment

Nearly every patient with schizophrenia will benefit from pharmacologic treatment but to varying degrees. Drugs to treat psychosis (aka “antipsychotics”), currently all still reducing postsynaptic dopamine receptor activity, are the mainstay of pharmacologic treatment, being effective for reducing the impact of psychotic symptoms such as hallucinations, delusions, and disorganized thoughts and behavior, as well as related symptoms of agitation and aggression. In many patients, these symptoms can be completely eliminated or reduced to only a mild level. Medications can also decrease the likelihood that symptoms will recur. Nevertheless, negative symptoms and cognitive dysfunction are inadequately treated with currently available medications.

Controlling positive psychotic symptoms is often insufficient for improving the community functioning and the quality of life of patients with schizophrenia. There is also an increased awareness of the serious health problems of patients with schizophrenia and the possible contributing role of some medications. These observations have led to principles of drug treatment that emphasize the necessary, but not always sufficient, role of drugs for psychosis in helping patients achieves their personal goals.

### EFFECTIVENESS OF DRUGS FOR PSYCHOSIS

A large body of evidence supports the effectiveness of dopamine receptor-blocking drugs. Many studies were carried out in the 1960s, addressing skepticism that these agents truly improved psychosis. The 1995 Schizophrenia Patient Outcomes Research Team (PORT) found that about 70% of patients treated with a drug for psychosis achieved relative remission or substantial improvement in contrast to 25% of patients treated with placebo. Recent meta-analyses of high-quality randomized controlled, parallel-design studies confirmed that dopamine receptor-blocking drugs are significantly superior to placebo for total symptomatology and positive symptoms.

The field of schizophrenia practice and research needs to understand some obvious areas. First, effective diagnostic and treatment algorithms for the early detection and treatment of psychotic young adults need to be established. Effective medications are necessary, even when optimized across each individual person, but they are by no means sufficient. Psychotic illness needs broad, diverse, and comprehensive treatment, especially at its inception or when it threatens to break through. This kind of comprehensive treatment extends beyond antipsychotic medication to include cognitive remediation approaches, psychosocial treatments, behavioral therapies, family training, and community involvement. The newest study in the treatment of early psychosis, the Recovery After an Initial Schizophrenia Episode (RAISE) study, found that implementing all of these treatments early on in disease manifestations generates better outcomes sooner in young people. Treating a psychotic illness such as schizophrenia “piecemeal” generates only partial gains and falls short of the success that comes with a comprehensive plan allowing potentiated gains.

It would certainly be useful if more were known about the mechanism of action of the effective drugs used for psychosis, all of which are dopamine receptor antagonists. What actually do these antidopaminergic drugs do in the brain that quiet the storms of psychotic symptoms? This would be an approach to discovering disease pathophysiology. One of the single issues that affect psychotic illnesses most adversely is the lack of disease understanding. What the field needs is a simple, direct, and concrete presentation of the mechanism(s) of psychosis to combat stigma, direct treatment development, and on which to base behavioral and cognitive treatment approaches.

### FIRST EPISODE SCHIZOPHRENIA

The treatment of patients with early-phase illness differs from that of patients with chronic schizophrenia. First-episode patients are generally more treatment responsive, require lower drug doses (approximately 50%), and are more sensitive to side effects. They often have a difficult time accepting their illness and are at high risk for nonadherence, relapse, psychosocial deterioration, and suicidality. Although medication for psychosis is the cornerstone of management, multidisciplinary interventions, such as in coordinated specialty care, focusing on sustained engagement, relapse prevention, physical health, and functional recovery, are paramount.

## 12.13 Psychiatric Rehabilitation

Approximately 5% of adults in the United States experience disability due to a serious psychiatric illness such as schizophrenia. Psychiatric rehabilitation denotes a wide range of interventions to help people with disabilities due to mental illness improve their functioning, quality of life, and recovery by enhancing the opportunities, skills, and supports they need to succeed in regular adult roles and in the environments of their choice. Regular adult roles include living independently, attending school, being employed in a competitive job, having positive relationships with family, having friends, pursuing leisure activities, and having intimate relationships. Psychiatric rehabilitation emphasizes relative independence, self-management of illness, self-efficacy, and community integration, including avoidance of dependence on professionals and segregated settings.

Psychiatric rehabilitation has become a fundamental component of the treatment for schizophrenia, consistent with current knowledge regarding the etiology, pathophysiology, manifestations, treatment, and course of the disorder. Current evidence, reviewed in other chapters, indicates that schizophrenia is almost certainly a heterogeneous set of disorders causally related to genetic factors, prenatal and perinatal insults, and postnatal stress. Although the definitive neurobiologic substrate of the disorder remains uncertain, many patients manifest mild neurodevelopmental abnormalities related to attention, cognition, social function, and motor function in childhood, long before the onset of overt psychotic illness. Research on schizophrenia shows that many but not all patients exhibit abnormalities of neural structure, circuitry, and function. Clinically, the disorder is characterized by positive symptoms of psychosis, such as hallucinations and delusions; negative symptoms, such as lack of motivation and poverty of speech; cognitive deficits, such as problems with memory and executive functioning; and psychosocial difficulties, such as poor social role performance, chronic unemployment, high rates of substance abuse, and increased risk for homelessness, victimization, and other problems. Long-term follow-up studies have shown over many years that about 10% to 15% of schizophrenia patients recover completely, and most others improve significantly (Bleuler termed the usual course “healing with scarring”), but some continue to experience symptoms, cognitive difficulties, and psychosocial problems for decades.

Antipsychotic medications often reduce positive symptoms of psychosis, but they have limited impact on negative symptoms, cognitive impairment, and psychosocial functioning. For the great majority of patients, medications do not restore premorbid levels of functioning and do not produce normative role performance. Some 20% or more of schizophrenia patients have psychotic symptoms that do not respond to antipsychotic medications, and many experience only a partial symptomatic response. Further, medications do not reverse the consequences of stigma, impaired learning, failure to master adult developmental tasks, and social withdrawal. Therefore, maximizing functional recovery through psychiatric rehabilitation is critical for most patients.
