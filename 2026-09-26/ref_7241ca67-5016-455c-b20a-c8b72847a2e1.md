### TREATMENT OF ANXIETY DISORDERS

Both cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) and antidepressant medications are effective for treating youth anxiety disorders (e.g., GAD and SAD). Expert reviews of the literature and meta-analyses repeatedly affirm the evidence base for both interventions, whether administered alone or in combination.

The Child/Adolescent Anxiety Multimodal Study (CAMS) remains the largest double-blind, placebo-controlled trial for child and adolescent anxiety disorders. The CAMS compared the relative efficacy of CBT, sertraline, combination therapy (CBT plus sertraline), and placebo for n = 488 youth with GAD, separation anxiety disorder, and/or SAD. All active treatments were superior to placebo, and combination therapy led to significantly higher response rates than CBT or sertraline (81% vs. 60% vs. 55%, respectively). Remission rates (loss of principal diagnosis, loss of all anxiety diagnoses) ranged from 20% to 68% and followed the same patterns as response rates based on treatment condition. Factors associated with better response and remission rates in the CAMS included the absence of comorbid internalizing disorders, absence of SAD at baseline, lower baseline anxiety symptom severity, lower caregiver strain, nonminority status, and younger age. A recent paper using CAMS data goes further to suggest that youth with mild–moderate anxiety benefit most from CBT monotherapy, whereas those with more severe illnesses may be best served by beginning with combined treatment.

Beyond acute and short-term follow-up of anxious youth, there has been considerable interest in understanding how these youngsters fare over time, particularly when they have had the benefit of evidence-based treatment. To address this question, youth who participated in the CAMS trial were followed as part of a prospective longitudinal study as they entered early adulthood (Child/Adolescent Anxiety Multimodal Treatment Extended Long-term Study; CAMELS). On average, youth were recontacted approximately 6 years following their CAMS treatment and followed for another 4 years. Findings from CAMELS suggest that roughly 20% of youth treated in CAMS remained consistently remitted over time as they entered early adulthood. Roughly half exhibited a fluctuating course marked by intermittent relapse. The remaining 30% were chronically ill, with anxiety disorder evident at every time point. These findings speak to the persistent nature of anxiety and the need to consider models of care that integrate chronic disease approaches and use surveillance and booster sessions to maintain wellness.

#### Psychotherapy

Over 50 years of evidence support the effectiveness of exposure-based CBT for youth anxiety. Although there is variability across treatment protocols, they are generally linked by their focus on psychoeducation, emotion identification, somatic management techniques, cognitive restructuring, exposure tasks, and relapse prevention. Traditional individual child treatment typically involves 10 to 20 weekly treatment sessions, and numerous studies support the use of additional formats, including group, family, brief intervention, and telehealth administration. In this section, we provide a brief synopsis of the evidence base for CBT before shifting to focus on newer trends in psychosocial treatment research for anxiety.

### TREATMENT

Anxiety disorders in older adults are frequently undetected and untreated (e.g., one study found that only 36% of patients with late-life GAD received any treatment). Older adults are significantly less likely than younger adults to receive treatment for anxiety and even less likely to seek treatment. When treatment takes place, it usually involves benzodiazepines or other anxiolytic medications rather than antidepressants or psychotherapy. Treatment response rates tend to be worse in older adults compared to younger adults. Several factors may be associated with worse response (e.g., worse cognitive function), but there are also changes in pharmacokinetics (reduced glomerular filtration and hepatic metabolization), which may also increase both side effects and effectiveness. Longitudinal studies have shown recurrence and chronicity rates up to 39% to 52% after 3 to 6 years. For late-life GAD, persistent worry is a significant component of relapse. Older adults tend to prefer psychotherapeutic approaches, though meta-analyses have shown that they are less effective than pharmacotherapies.

#### Pharmacotherapy

Research on the treatment of anxiety in older adults is limited. Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) and serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) are the first-line treatment for late-life anxiety disorders with similar side-effect profiles in younger adults and are relatively well tolerated. Recent evidence suggests that SSRI pharmacotherapy is superior to psychotherapy as an acute treatment for geriatric anxiety. Data from controlled trials support the use of escitalopram (Lexapro), duloxetine (Cymbalta), pregabalin (Lyrica), venlafaxine XR (Effexor), citalopram (Celexa), and sertraline (Zoloft) for late-life GAD. In a randomized trial, pregabalin was associated with a 2-point greater reduction in overall anxiety, a difference statistically significant although clinically trivial. In general, there are specific potential risks that should be monitored in older adults, including the risk of falls, gastrointestinal bleed, bone loss, hyponatremia, and monitoring of blood pressure in SNRIs (especially venlafaxine). Another study also found that Buspirone was effective and well tolerated in late-life GAD. The antipsychotic Quetiapine has demonstrated efficacy and tolerability both as a monotherapy and as adjunctive therapy to SSRIs, but one study reported multiple side effects, including drowsiness, dry mouth, dizziness, headache, and nausea. Mirtazapine is a popular choice for its effects on sleep and appetite, but the evidence of its efficacy is limited and inconsistent. Tricyclic antidepressants or monoamine oxidase inhibitors (MAOI) may be efficacious; however, they should be considered only for treatment-resistant patients due to their side-effect profiles and safety concerns.

Data are even more sparse with respect to the treatment of other anxiety disorders, but evidence from controlled trials and open-label studies suggests the efficacy of sertraline, citalopram, and escitalopram, as well as of imipramine (Tofranil) and alprazolam (Xanax), for panic disorder in older adults. The SSRIs citalopram, escitalopram, and sertraline may be particularly effective treatments for anxiety in older adults based on their low propensity for drug interactions and short half-lives. Fluvoxamine (Luvox) has demonstrated efficacy in an open-label trial, including older patients with GAD, panic disorder, or OCD.

#### Psychotherapy

Some evidence suggests that SSRI medications may be more effective than psychotherapy as an acute treatment for late-life anxiety disorders. Research, mostly with older GAD patients, provides support for the effectiveness of cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) relative to a waiting list and active control conditions such as supportive therapy and attention placebo. One study has demonstrated the superiority of CBT to supportive psychotherapy in a well-characterized sample of older adults with anxiety disorders (52% with panic disorder), and all participants in this investigation were taking medications but had failed to respond prior to receiving psychotherapy. Response to CBT for GAD in older adults may be compromised by memory impairment, executive dysfunction, or failure to complete at-home practice. A recent study has demonstrated that the sequence of antidepressant medication augmented with CBT in older adults with GAD leads to worry reduction in the short term. This study also found that both maintenance medication and CBT could prevent relapse compared with a placebo, providing two strategies for improving outcomes in GAD in older adults.

For older individuals who refuse to take medications or for whom they are contraindicated, relaxation training may be the most effective component of a behavioral treatment for GAD or distressing anxiety symptoms that do not meet the criteria for a specific anxiety disorder. Recommending a good relaxation tape with breathing or meditation exercises, progressive muscle relaxation, or imagery may be helpful; if, after a few weeks of practice, the patient does not report sufficient gains, it may be worthwhile to reopen a discussion about medications. One study showed that for older patients, relaxation therapy might be the most efficacious aspect of CBT. While older adults can learn and effectively use reappraisal, this may be more challenging in a population with diminishing cognitive abilities. Mindfulness-based stress reduction has also been shown to reduce anxiety symptoms in older adults, even showing preliminary efficacy in individuals with cognitive dysfunction. Finally, acceptance and commitment therapy has shown improvement in worry severity in late-life GAD.

For anxiety disorders other than GAD, some evidence supports the use of CBT for panic disorder in older adults, although results from a study that included a substantial proportion of older panic disorder patients suggest the superiority of sertraline over CBT. There are no published data beyond case reports documenting the behavioral treatment of specific phobias or OCD, all of which use exposure-based protocols, and no data on the treatment of older adults with agoraphobia without panic disorder or social anxiety disorder (social phobia). Case reports of PTSD treatment in older adults have described gains associated with psychodynamic and supportive approaches, CBT, eye movement desensitization and reprocessing life review, and exposure therapy. A pilot study of prolonged exposure therapy in older veterans with PTSD showed a significant decrease in their symptoms of PTSD.

Consumers, particularly those with mood and anxiety disorders, are increasingly turning to CAM to either self-treat or augment their more conventional medications. Aerobic exercise has some data for utility in anxiety conditions and, given its myriad health benefits, should be routinely recommended. Clinical Management. Because SSRIs may initially exacerbate symptoms of anxiety, older adults should be started on a low dose ([Table 55.3d–3](#tt55-3d-3)). If the patient is tolerating the medication, the dose should be increased at least to the minimum target dose within 4 weeks. The classification “anxiety disorder due to a general medical condition” is meant to describe cases in which the symptoms are a direct physiologic consequence of the illness. Anxiety is ubiquitous and can be found with many of the conditions that cause other psychiatric disorders. Anxiety in cancer can be caused by different medical conditions, including pulmonary embolism, hypoxia, hypoglycemia, and cardiac disorders. Hormone-secreting neoplasms and paraneoplastic syndromes can also cause anxiety ([Table 27.8–4](#tt27-8-4)).

Anxiety due to substance withdrawal is recognized and treated by most clinicians. Nicotine withdrawal is less commonly recognized. Many patients stop smoking abruptly on receiving their diagnosis and may experience severe anxiety symptoms, sometimes associated with dissociative symptoms and bizarre behaviors. Behavioral smoking cessation programs are an indispensable adjunct and are commonly offered to family members as well, taking advantage of what is known as a window of opportunity when defenses are shaken and motivation is high.

Substance-induced anxiety is a common cause of anxiety in cancer patients. They include bronchodilators, frequently used in lung cancer patients in whom they compound the anxiety caused by air hunger, corticosteroids, the mainstay of many protocols; anti-inflammatory cytokines, which are being increasingly used for treatment, and antipsychotics, including some of the ones that are used as antiemetics, such as metoclopramide (Reglan). Akathisia is often mistaken for anxiety by patients and staff; thyroxine, psychostimulants, sympathomimetic agents, serotonergic agents, and antihistamines may produce symptoms of anxiety.

Treatment. Treatment aims include reducing the patient’s overall level of emotional distress as well as reducing specific target symptoms that may impair social or occupational function. Treatment of anxiety in cancer patients depends on the etiology and the timing of the onset of symptoms. In some situations, anxiety symptoms can represent disorders that started before the onset of a medical illness. In that case, the treatment approach should be directed to the premorbid condition. In other instances, anxiety can be the consequence of a medical condition or its treatment. In such cases, the treatment should target the underlying condition.

Current or past delirium can contribute to anxiety in cancer patients. Delirious patients can present with early symptoms of restlessness, irritability, altered sleep patterns, and impaired concentration. Patients who have been delirious may have persistent worry related to their memory of this experience or their behavior during episodes of delirium.
