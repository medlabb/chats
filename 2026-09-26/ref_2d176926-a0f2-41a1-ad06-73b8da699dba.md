### UNCOMPLICATED BEREAVEMENT

#### Definition

DSM-5-TR states that *UB* (V62.82) may be used when the focus of clinical attention is a normal reaction to the death of a loved one. As part of their reaction to such a loss, some grieving individuals present with symptoms such as feelings of sadness and associated symptoms such as insomnia, poor appetite, and weight loss. The bereaved individual typically regards the depressed mood as “normal,” although the individual may seek professional help for relief of associated symptoms such as insomnia or anorexia.

#### Diagnosis and Clinical Features

Questionnaires have been used for decades to screen for symptoms of grief. The Texas Revised Inventory of Grief (TRIG) assesses typical symptoms of UB, such as acceptance of loss, crying, and intrusive thoughts. The Inventory of Complicated Grief (ICG) assesses indicators of complicated bereavement, such as anger, disbelief, and hallucinations. Bereavement is also called “grief” and “mourning.” After the loss of a loved one, the bereaved individual may experience sadness, loneliness, and perhaps anger. In some circumstances—for example, involving long-term caregivers—the process of bereavement may begin even before death occurs, which is called anticipatory grief. Cultural expectations may influence how a person experiences grief. In some cultures, a death in the family is manifested in a quiet and private manner; in other cultures, there may be loud, intense displays of emotion.

In contrast to the relational problem of UB, complicated or complex grief makes it harder for the bereaved person to adapt to the loss of the loved one. These individuals may ruminate over the timing or manner of the death. They may put excessive energy into avoiding reminders of the loss. They may question whether their own experience of grief is too much or too little. In complicated grief, these symptoms persist longer than 6 months following the death of the loved one.

#### Differential Diagnosis and Comorbidity

The DSM-5-TR discusses how to distinguish bereavement or grief from a major depressive episode (MDE). In UB, the predominant affect is feelings of emptiness and loss, while in an MDE, it is persistent depressed mood and the inability to anticipate happiness or pleasure. The dysphoria in UB is likely to decrease in intensity over days to weeks and occurs in waves, which tend to be associated with thoughts or reminders of the deceased. The depressed mood of an MDE is more persistent and not tied to specific thoughts or preoccupations. The pain of UB may be accompanied by positive emotions and humor that are uncharacteristic of the pervasive unhappiness and misery characteristic of an MDE. If a bereaved individual thinks about death and dying, such thoughts are generally focused on the deceased and possibly about “joining” the deceased, whereas, in an MDE, such thoughts are focused on ending one’s own life because of feeling worthless, undeserving of life, or unable to cope with the pain of depression.

Bereaved persons with depressive symptomatology that do not meet the criteria for major depression may benefit from brief focused psychotherapy. Interpersonal therapy lends itself well to this purpose since it addresses issues related to loss, depression, and change of roles.

Although antidepressant drugs can be effective in bereaved older adults with depressive symptoms, clear guidelines are not available. If major depression develops during the bereavement period, antidepressant treatment should be considered.

Complicated grief—a persistent complex bereavement disorder characterized by prolonged acute grief, feeling that life has no meaning, avoidance behaviors, and dysregulated emotions—has been identified in DSM-5 as a condition in need of further study. Complicated grief treatment (CGT) has been developed as a 16-week treatment with components of prolonged exposure, interpersonal therapy (IPT), and motivational interviewing and has been found to be more efficacious in symptom improvement than IPT in older adults. It appears that antidepressants may enhance the response to CGT but that antidepressants alone may be ineffective.

#### Treatment of Psychotic Depression

For the treatment of psychotic depression, the recommended treatment includes combinations of antidepressants (SSRIs or venlafaxine XR) and atypical neuroleptics (except clozapine), although there are limited clinical trials for older adults. In one multicenter 12-week double-blind, randomized controlled trial (STOP-PD) and a follow-up study (STOP-PD II), combination pharmacotherapy with olanzapine and sertraline was associated with higher remission rates during the trial period compared to olanzapine plus placebo in patients. ECT treatment is another option, especially if combination drug treatment fails or is poorly tolerated.

#### Treatment of Depression in Dementia

In older adults with major depression and mild-to-moderate dementia, the historical treatment of choice was a combination of an antidepressant with psychosocial intervention, although there are limited data to support the use of antidepressant medication. Indeed, in a large, well-powered study, not only were antidepressants (i.e., sertraline and mirtazapine) found ineffective in depressed demented patients but they also were associated with more adverse effects than placebo. A review article in 2017 found that evidence does not support the use of pharmacologic treatment of depression in patients with dementia. Further, a more recent meta-analysis in 2021 found that nonpharmacologic interventions were more efficacious than drug interventions in reducing depressive symptoms in older adults with depression symptoms and comorbid dementia without an MDD diagnosis. These findings suggest that psychosocial interventions such as PATH and caregiver-focused treatment may be better first-line treatment options for this population.

Some depressive states are normal and common reactions to major unwelcome and undesirable life events. Normal bereavement best exemplifies this. In bereavement after major losses, such as the death of a parent, spouse, or child, people experience sadness, pining, and yearning but do not ordinarily have the feelings of guilt, unworthiness, and self-reproach that characterize depressive disorders. Feelings of helplessness and hopelessness may be temporarily present in bereavement, but they ordinarily pass with time. In uncomplicated cases, the process of bereavement takes 3 to 6 months in the acute phase and up to 1 year for complete resolution. Bereaved people are more likely to feel physically ill and seek general health care than at other times, and older widowers are more likely to die than age-matched nonbereaved controls. Pathologic or prolonged grief reactions, such as bereavements that last more than 1 year, may be seen when the surviving spouse was excessively dependent on the deceased and is unable to obtain emotional and practical (e.g., financial) support elsewhere or when the survivor is unable to grieve fully because of markedly ambivalent feelings toward the deceased. Distinguishing between pathologic or prolonged grief and grief triggering a depressive episode may not always be possible.

#### Anxiety Disorders

Anxiety symptoms, including panic attacks, morbid fears, and obsessions, are common during depressive disorders, and depression is a common complication of anxiety states. Systematic British studies have shown that early-morning awakening, psychomotor retardation, self-reproach, hopelessness, and suicidal ideation are the most robust clinical markers of depression in that differential diagnosis. On follow-up of depressed patients, the manifestations tend to remit, whereas those with anxiety states continue to exhibit marked tension, phobias, panic attacks, vasomotor instability, feelings of unreality, and perceptual distortions, as well as hypochondriacal ideas. A predominance of such anxiety features antedating the present illness suggests the diagnosis of an anxiety disorder. Because anxiety disorders rarely first appear after 40 years of age, a late appearance of marked anxiety features strongly favors the diagnosis of melancholia. The clinical picture is often one of morbid groundless anxiety with somatization, hypochondriasis, and agitation. Superior response to ECT further supports the depressive nature of the illness. Periodic monosymptomatic phobic and obsessional states exist which we can regard as affective equivalents based on a family history of mood disorders and their response to thymoleptic agents. Recent data from a large clinical series suggest that 15% of cases have hypomanic symptoms; these patients likely have bipolar II disorder. Social phobias often usher in adolescent depression, even a bipolar II disorder; the latter is particularly likely when pairing the phobia with alcohol abuse.
