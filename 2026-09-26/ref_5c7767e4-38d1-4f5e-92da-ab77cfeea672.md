### DEFINITIONS

OCD is characterized by the presence of recurrent obsessions or compulsions that are distressing, time consuming, or debilitating. Obsessions are defined as recurrent, persistent, and intrusive thoughts, ideas, impulses, or mental images that are experienced by the individual as distressing and as a product of one’s own mind (to differentiate them from thought insertion). These thoughts or ideas frequently generate (or are associated with) feelings of anxiety, tension, disgust, or aversion. [Table 51.1–1](#tt51-1-1) lists common obsessional fears. Compulsions are purposeful, repetitive behaviors or mental acts that typically function to neutralize, prevent, or reduce anxiety. [Table 51.1–1](#tt51-1-1) also listed common compulsive behaviors. Often, compulsions are performed in response to an obsessive thought or according to some other rigidly applied rules. Although compulsions function to mitigate distress or anxiety or to prevent some dreaded event, they are clearly excessive or are not realistically connected with the triggering stimulus (e.g., tapping three times to prevent illness after someone in the room coughs). While obsessions and compulsions are often ego dystonic and are often recognized by children as senseless or exaggerated, such insight is not required for the diagnosis of OCD in childhood. Otherwise, symptoms of OCD in childhood resemble those seen in adults.

### DIAGNOSIS AND CLINICAL FEATURES

Presenting problems among children and adolescents with OCD generally resemble the adult presentation. However, compared to adults, children are more likely to deny impairment due to obsessions and compulsions. Children may also be somewhat less likely to consider their OCD symptoms excessive, perhaps due to lower levels of cognitive awareness. Children do not need to recognize their symptoms as senseless or unrealistic for diagnosis to be assigned; the level of insight varies considerably related to a child’s developmental level. Unlike most adults, children might not report symptoms to be ego dystonic. However, children are typically willing to discuss distress from family conflict arising from their rituals (e.g., chronically running late, excessive need for reassurance, time burden on parents, disruption of siblings functioning) or interference in schoolwork resulting in dropping grades. Overall, most children are quite open in describing their symptoms and seek assistance in better managing the illness.

Although comorbidity is the rule rather than the exception in childhood OCD, the content of the obsessions and compulsions cannot be better explained by a co-occurring Axis I disorder. Examples include self-deprecating ruminations associated with depression, obsessive concern with appearance in body dysmorphic disorder, hair pulling in trichotillomania, and fears of harm befalling a loved one in separation anxiety disorder. Similarly, the symptoms also are required not to be substance induced or due to the influence of a medical condition. The range of obsessions and compulsions experienced in childhood is similar to that of adults with OCD, although children are more likely to engage in compulsive reassurance seeking and involve family members in their rituals. Most children with OCD have both obsessions and compulsions; pure obsessional illness is rare in this age group. In fact, most childhood cases of OCD involve multiple obsessions and compulsions (mean number of four current compulsions and four current obsessions in one report). Compulsions in the absence of anxiety-related or otherwise distress-inducing obsessions may occur in (mostly younger) children, who often describe their rituals as being performed in response to an irresistible urge or an otherwise vague sensation (the “just-right” phenomena). The absence of reported obsessions in these cases may be due to underdeveloped awareness or ability to articulate these thoughts or result of a misdiagnosed tic disorder. Common compulsions involve cleaning/washing rituals, checking, repeating behaviors (touching, tapping, rubbing), counting, ordering/arranging, avoidance, doubt/indecision, confessing, seeking reassurance, and slowness. The content and type of obsessive and compulsive symptoms may change over time. OCD is also stress sensitive, and many children experience acute symptom exacerbations during times of psychosocial stress or change (e.g., attending a new school, moving to a new home, death of a family member).

Similar to adult OCD, family members are commonly drawn into the child’s OCD symptoms through the provision of reassurance to the child, modification of family routines as a result of the child’s symptoms, or even actual participation in the child’s compulsive rituals. Such accommodation can negatively impact the family environment and lead to increased levels of family conflict and negative emotional reactions toward the affected child. Accommodation has also been associated with increased feelings of depression and anxiety on the part of the accommodating family member (typically mothers).

Given the typically strange and senseless nature of many OCD symptoms, many children and adolescents will attempt to camouflage their rituals. It is not uncommon for children with OCD to be able to inhibit their rituals for short periods of time to avoid detection (e.g., in class). Not infrequently, teachers, peers, and even parents remain unaware of the child’s OCD for months or years, only becoming aware of the problem after the child can no longer control the symptoms or becomes too overwhelmed to cope.

### HISTORY

Early examples of childhood-onset OCD, consisting of case descriptions, date back to the late 19th century. In 1895, Sigmund Freud described an 11-year-old child with obsessions. Subsequently, the French physician Pierre Janet provided the first description consistent with OCD in childhood in his 1903 report on a 5-year-old boy. In the first half of the 20th century, descriptions of clinical samples and generalizations emerged. In 1942, L. Berman extracted clinical information on 62 children with possible obsessive-compulsive neurosis out of a case registry of 3,050 cases. In 1955, Louise Despert described more than 60 cases of children with “obsessive-compulsive neurosis,” noting a male preponderance, children’s recognition that their symptoms are abnormal and unwanted, and the tendency to attempt to conceal their symptoms from treatment professionals and others. Leo Kanner’s (1962) textbook of Child Psychiatry contained descriptions of childhood OCD and the consequences of social isolation and family accommodation.

In 1965, Lewis Judd provided descriptive criteria for the diagnosis of OCD in children that closely resembles the current diagnostic criteria, including the requirement of significant impairment in functioning. Reports as recent as 1973 implicated parental withdrawal of attention as a critical causal factor of childhood OCD. However, by the 1980s, the notion that OCD was a neurobiologic disorder with possible childhood onset became more commonplace. Further, findings from the Epidemiologic Catchment Area (ECA) study indicating most adults with OCD report an onset of the disorder during adolescence prompted increased awareness of the disorder in childhood as well as research in this area. Programmatic work in the 1980s under the direction of Judith Rapoport and her group at the National Institute of Mental Health provided the basis for the first systematic studies of epidemiology, phenomenology, and treatment of childhood- and adolescent-onset OCD as well as fundamentally dismissing any notion that the emergence of OCD in youth was unusual or unexpected. The Pediatric Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder Treatment Study (POTS) emerged as the plenary multimodal treatment trial in 2004.

### DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS

#### Developmentally Normative Rituals and Superstitions

Benign habits, transitory superstitions, or mildly excessive thoughts are not uncommon during childhood. In certain phases of early childhood, rituals or superstitions may be utilized by a child to manage new or stressful situations (first day of preschool, separating from a parent) or achieve a sense of proficiency. For example, a child may require a particular bedtime ritual or demand to wear a particular item of clothing. These normative rituals usually fade by the time of entry into formal schooling and are rarely associated with distress, significant interference with activities, or overlap in content with typical obsessions or compulsions. The absence of functional impairment differentiates mild, age-appropriate superstitions and habits from obsessive-compulsive symptoms.

#### Other Anxiety Disorders

Despite the presence of preoccupation and worry about issues related to their fears, children with other anxiety disorders typically do not engage in compulsive behaviors to reduce their anxiety. The content of the worry can also be useful in differentiating syndromes. Pediatric patients with generalized anxiety disorder (GAD) present with heightened anxiety relating to their health and safety, the safety of others, and their future. The content of these symptoms, although exaggerated, is grounded in everyday matters (finances, health, grades, safety) as opposed to the unusual or even bizarre content of obsessive worries. Behavioral analogs to compulsions are rare besides the requests for reassurance from others to reduce anxiety. Patients with social anxiety have fears of negative evaluation and extreme scrutiny by others. Again, fears are, by definition, excessive but typically not bizarre. Rituals are rare, although avoidance of feared situations is common. Similarly, the content of fears expressed by children with separation anxiety disorder is, by definition, confined to themes of separation or fears of harm to self or loved ones. Besides avoidance of separation, other behavioral features or rituals are absent from the presentation.

#### Tic Disorders, Including Tourette Syndrome

Children and adolescents with tic disorders typically display phonic or motor tics that occur frequently and may appear behaviorally similar to compulsive actions, particularly more complex tics. Tics are differentiated from compulsions as usually being avolitional and not preceded by precipitating obsessional thoughts. However, some patients describe their tics as partially volitional in that tic expression is triggered by, and leads to relief from, an uncomfortable premonitory sensation, a functional relationship identical to that between obsessions and compulsions. The content of tics is unlikely to be as purposeful as common compulsions (e.g., washing, arranging), although complex tics may also involve repetition, for example, tapping or touching something a specific number of times. It is noteworthy that there is substantial overlap between tic disorders and OCD in childhood; symptoms of each are not uncommon, and both diagnoses may be warranted.

### COURSE AND PROGNOSIS

OCD, with onset in childhood, is a chronic condition characterized by substantial morbidity. Follow-up studies suggest that 43% to 68% of youth diagnosed with OCD continue to meet diagnostic criteria for the disorder up to 14 years after identification. A recent meta-analysis found that the remission rate may be as high as 62% (52% to 72% within the 95% confidence interval), with shorter duration of income being linked to better outcomes. Worse outcomes at follow-up were predicted by poor initial treatment response, a lifetime history of tic disorder, and a parental Axis I psychiatric disorder. The literature lacks long-term controlled treatment studies, and most follow-up data are from extant treatment trials following only a few months posttreatment. Further, this literature is complicated by multiple confounding variables, including treatment type, comorbidity, age or age of onset, and access to follow-up care. In sum, despite promising treatments for youth with OCD, long-term data are scarce, and there is considerable room for improvement with existing interventions. Early intervention appears to be critically important.

Even with remission following treatment, children with a history of OCD or subclinical OCD symptoms should continue to be monitored for relapse in symptoms. Likewise, relatives (siblings) of patients with OCD should be monitored closely and considered for treatment with the emergence of subclinical symptoms, even in the absence of more severe or impairing symptoms.
