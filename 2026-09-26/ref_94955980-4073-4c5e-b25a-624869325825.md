<small>LITHIUM.</small>

Lithium has a narrow therapeutic index. Consequently, blood levels of lithium must be monitored to achieve therapeutic dosing and avoid toxicity. For acute mania, target serum levels of lithium are in the range of 0.8 to 1.2 mEq/L. Optimum maintenance serum levels may vary from patient to patient, with the goal of achieving a balance between clinical efficacy and tolerability. Many clinicians aim for maintenance levels between 0.6 to 0.8 mEq/L, especially if patients complain of side effects at levels of 0.8 mEq/L or higher. Side effects are dose dependent. Symptoms of toxicity may begin to manifest with serum levels of greater than 1.2 mEq/L and are common with levels greater than 1.4 mEq/L. Older or seriously medically ill patients may show signs of toxicity with levels less than 1.2 mEq/L. With high levels (e.g., >2 mEq/L), consultation with a nephrologist or internist should occur. In patients with high serum lithium levels, dialysis is preferable to reduce lithium levels because prolonged exposure increases the likelihood of irreversible cerebral damage. Symptoms of toxicity include tremors, sedation, and confusion. At higher levels, delirium, seizures, coma, and death may occur.

Steady-state serum lithium levels are achieved after five half-lives, and lithium levels typically reach steady-state 5 days after a change in dosage in patients with normal renal function. Levels are obtained as close to 12 hours after the last dose as possible and are usually measured using samples obtained in the morning before administration of the morning dose (trough level). In cases of suspected lithium toxicity, levels are obtained sooner.

Maintenance levels are checked every 3 to 6 months and sometimes more frequently in patients with compromised renal function, general debilitation, or signs of toxicity. Close monitoring is required for patients who take medications that may alter lithium levels, such as diuretics or certain nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs).

Baseline pretreatment studies and periodic follow-up tests for lithium should include monitoring of renal and thyroid function. Renal function is monitored using BUN, serum Cr, and a urinalysis. Because lithium commonly affects thyroid function, a baseline screening assessment of thyroid function (TSH) is obtained. These tests are repeated once or twice during the first 6 months of treatment and subsequently every 6 months to every year during ongoing treatment. Clinicians also should obtain thyroid studies in patients taking lithium who develop fatigue or asthenia, cold intolerance, or changes in the skin, voice, or weight (symptoms of impaired thyroid function). Many clinicians measure calcium levels at baseline and every 6 months to 1 year, given the association of lithium treatment with parathyroid dysfunction and hypercalcemia. Also, because lithium commonly produces a mild leukocytosis, a pretreatment WBC is often obtained.

ECGs are often obtained before initiation of treatment with lithium if there are risk factors for coronary artery disease. An ECG is obtained in cases of lithium toxicity or overdose.

In treating patients with eating disorders, it may be reasonable to obtain ECG as patients commonly have low potassium levels that may result in abnormal ECG recordings. As the serum potassium drops below normal, T waves become flattened (or inverted), and U waves may appear.

### POLYPHARMACY AND ADMINISTRATION OF LITHIUM

The medical workup before beginning lithium therapy includes complete blood count and blood chemistry, including renal function and thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) and calcium, and physical and neurologic examination within the last 6 months, including electrocardiogram (EKG) in patients over 60.

Lithium monotherapy is rare today for several reasons. First, in the acute hospitalized manic patient, it is often difficult to get cooperation for blood level testing, and nutrition and hydration may be unstable in uncooperative patients. Lithium is appropriate only when hydration is stable and blood testing can be routinely achieved. Second, most patients receive antipsychotic treatment as their first-line treatment to achieve rapid behavioral control, and lithium is sometimes added as a polypharmacy treatment in order to allow discharge to the community as soon as possible. However, a patient should not be started on lithium treatment in the acute hospital phase if he has not had exposure to lithium in the past and commits realistically to using it prophylactically in the future. The danger of a patient being discharged on a dopamine antagonist plus lithium who then stops his lithium after discharge is great because the sudden discontinuation of lithium can cause a rebound relapse. Third, lithium monotherapy is also rare nowadays in prophylaxis because many more patients with a clinical diagnosis of psychotic bipolar disorder require the continuation of dopamine-serotonin antagonists with their lithium. If side effects of lithium develop, it is easy nowadays to replace it with a dopamine-serotonin antagonist, not requiring blood testing and without danger of fatal toxicity with the medicolegal responsibility that goes along with prescribing lithium without blood monitoring.

Lithium does not seem to have significant pharmacokinetic or drug–drug interactions with other psychoactive compounds and can be used in combination. Because many patients respond only partially to lithium (or other mood stabilizers as well), it is often clinical practice to add another antimanic compound or mood stabilizer rather than stopping lithium when some, even partial, response has been observed. This leads to polypharmacy. In the early post–World War II days of modern clinical psychopharmacology, Lasagna was greatly influential in American medicine in promoting monotherapy as the only way to achieve true knowledge in medical pharmacology but also the only way to avoid impossibly complex interactions in individual patient care. This has proved increasingly difficult to maintain in clinical practice. It is estimated today that 80% of patients taking lithium are taking other major psychoactive drugs as well, usually dopamine blockers but antiepileptic mood stabilizers as well. Initially and especially in academic centers and in textbooks, this practice was considered embarrassing, rarely talked about, and even more rarely studied. The FDA demands monotherapy in psychopharmacologic trials so that lithium must be compared to a placebo (with perhaps a third group such as valproate or quetiapine). This standard has been stricter, perhaps unfairly so, with psychiatry than with other disciplines. In the neurology of epilepsy, for instance, it is common practice for the FDA to allow add-on trials of one antiepileptic agent to another current ongoing treatment in partially responsive epilepsy patients. Robert Post and his group have given considerable intellectual backing to the reality of rational polypharmacy in psychopharmacology in general and in bipolar disorder in particular. Since lithium, the anticonvulsant mood stabilizers, and the dopamine-blocking mood stabilizers seem to have no obvious common denominator biochemically, it makes intuitive sense that their therapeutic modes of action might be at least additive and perhaps synergistic with each other. The goal will be for each patient to have an accurate record of what drug combinations he responded to best over his own history of illness as the best predictor so far of what he might respond to in the future.
