## Answer

 ## **Opioid Use Disorder (OUD): Summary**

### **Definition and clinical concept**
Opioids are naturally occurring or synthetic psychoactive compounds used medically for pain relief but also misused as prescription medications, heroin, or synthetic opioids such as fentanyl. OUD is regarded as a **chronic, relapsing disorder** involving multiple brain circuits and is treatable with pharmacologic and psychosocial interventions. (1)

OUD generally involves **compulsive and prolonged opioid self-administration**, without a legitimate medical purpose or at doses exceeding medical need, despite significant opioid-related problems. The disorder reflects impaired control over opioid use and includes cognitive, behavioral, and physiologic features. (2)

### **DSM-5 diagnosis**
DSM-5 combines the former categories of opioid abuse and dependence into one disorder occurring on a **mild-to-severe continuum**. Diagnosis requires **at least 2 of 11 symptoms within a 12-month period**. The criteria fall into four domains: **impaired control, social impairment, risky use, and pharmacologic changes**. (3; 2)

The 11 generic substance-use criteria include:

1. Using opioids in larger amounts or for longer than intended  
2. Persistent desire or unsuccessful efforts to cut down  
3. Spending substantial time obtaining, using, or recovering from opioids  
4. Craving or a strong urge to use  
5. Failure to fulfill major work, home, or school obligations  
6. Continued use despite social or interpersonal problems  
7. Giving up or reducing important activities  
8. Recurrent use in physically hazardous situations  
9. Continued use despite known physical or psychological problems  
10. Tolerance  
11. Withdrawal (4)

### **Severity and specifiers**
- **Mild OUD:** 2–3 symptoms  
- **Moderate OUD:** 4–5 symptoms  
- **Severe OUD:** 6 or more symptoms  

DSM-5 also specifies:
- **Early remission:** no criteria met for more than 3 months but less than 12 months  
- **Sustained remission:** no criteria met for more than 12 months  
- Whether the person is **on maintenance therapy**  
- Whether the person is in a **controlled environment** where opioid access is restricted (2)

Physical dependence—tolerance or withdrawal—is **neither necessary nor sufficient** for diagnosing OUD. The term “use disorder” also helps distinguish the disorder from normal physiologic dependence and avoids the potentially stigmatizing or ambiguous term “addiction.” (3)

### **Related opioid conditions**
DSM-5 distinguishes OUD from:
- **Opioid intoxication**
- **Opioid withdrawal**
- **Other opioid-induced disorders**
- **Unspecified opioid-related disorder** (2)

Opioid intoxication may involve euphoria followed by apathy, dysphoria, psychomotor agitation or retardation, impaired judgment, constricted pupils, drowsiness or coma, slurred speech, and impaired attention or memory. (5)

Severe overdose is characterized by **coma, pinpoint but reactive pupils, and respiratory depression or apnea**. Fentanyl overdose may also cause respiratory muscle rigidity and laryngospasm, described as “wooden chest” syndrome. Death may result from hypoxic–ischemic injury or cardiac arrhythmia. (5)

### **Epidemiology and public health importance**
The opioid crisis initially expanded with increased prescription opioid use during the 1990s and later shifted toward heroin and synthetic opioids, particularly fentanyl. Opioid-related overdoses have caused substantial mortality, and opioid use disorders disproportionately affect people with psychiatric disorders. (6)

The text reports that approximately **1.5% of the global population in 2019** had nonmedical use of opioid-derived compounds. In the United States, OUD has affected people across age, socioeconomic, and occupational groups. (1)

In 2019, U.S. estimates included approximately **745,000 people reporting past-year heroin use** and **438,000 people meeting criteria for OUD**. The chapter also notes that opioid overdose deaths occurred in three waves: prescription opioids, heroin, and subsequently high-potency synthetic opioids. This is illustrated in **Figure 11.9–2**. (7; 8)

### **Course and prognosis**
The course is variable. Some individuals use opioids intermittently without developing OUD, but approximately one-third of recreational users may eventually develop moderate-to-severe OUD; there is no reliable way to predict progression in an individual first-time user. Co-occurring psychiatric or substance-use disorders and intravenous heroin use are major risk factors. (9)

OUD most commonly follows a **chronic and recurrent course**. Use may progress from intermittent use to daily use, increasing frequency, tolerance, physiologic dependence, and opioid-related problems. Multiple daily doses of short-acting opioids may be used to prevent or relieve early withdrawal symptoms. (9)

Relapse commonly follows self-detoxification or voluntary abstinence, particularly during stress, depression, interpersonal conflict, or major life crises. After relapse, opioid use, tolerance, dependence, and related problems may escalate rapidly. Common reasons for seeking abstinence include occupational, family, financial, and legal problems; exhaustion from maintaining daily use; withdrawal; severe depression; or legal pressure. (9)

The selected references state that OUD is amenable to **pharmacologic and psychosocial interventions** and that treatment can help individuals initiate and maintain long-term abstinence.
