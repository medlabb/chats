### DEFINITION

Adolescence is the period of maturation between childhood and adulthood heralded by the physiologic signs and surging hormones of puberty. It is a time when peer relationships deepen, autonomy grows, and intellectual pursuits intensify and a sense of social belonging is sought. Adolescence is a time of accelerated social and psychosexual exploration, culminating in an integrated concept of “self.” Adolescents can be viewed as neurobiologic “works in progress,” often consumed by academic, interpersonal, and emotional challenges, and exploring new territories using their talents, and experimenting with social identities.

## 53.8 The Treatment of Adolescents

The treatment of adolescents must begin with a definition and developmental understanding of adolescence. Although most developmental theorists view adolescence as roughly coinciding with the onset of puberty, it is important to note that the criteria for both the onset and the conclusion of adolescent development are complex and multidetermined. Biologic, psychological, social, cognitive, and cultural paradigms, all play a role in the conceptualization of what exactly constitutes adolescent development.

From a biologic perspective, adolescence begins with the onset of puberty. It is important to note that puberty is not limited to the development of secondary sexual characteristics. In addition, neurodevelopment research suggests that during puberty, the brain undergoes a number of important changes. These changes are most likely necessary for the emerging adolescent to move away from what Piaget characterized as a relatively rule-bound and concrete understanding of the world toward an increasing capacity for abstract thought. Neuron migration, changes in myelination, pruning of gray matter, and increases in white matter, all lead to changes in frontal lobe activity and have been implicated as playing major roles in long-observed cognitive changes. However, there is also growing evidence for incomplete frontal lobe myelination leading to decreased capacities for complicated problem-solving activities in the presence of hotly affective environments. Thus, despite substantial development of brain regions responsible for executive function, the relatively poor myelination of these regions explains some of the classically observed lapses in judgment and some of the impulsivity that characterizes adolescent behavior. In this fashion, Piaget’s observations have been validated and also challenged by a growing understanding of adolescent neurobiology. During the ages of 14 to 26, cerebral cells die, a process known as apoptosis. Also, during this time period, myelination of neurons from lower centers, such as the amygdala, cingulate gyrus, hippocampus to the cortex, and the orbitofrontal cortex, takes place. This important neurobiologic task helps the developing adolescent move from their behavior being driven by impulse and emotion, to behavior which is based more on complex, rational decision making, reflection, and problem-solving processes.

Psychologically, adolescence has typically been viewed as a period of separation and individuation coupled with an increasing desire to form a coherent and consistent sense of self. These tasks, roughly characterized as identity formation by theorists such as Erik Erikson, Anna Freud, and Peter Blos, are central to the experience of the adolescent as they continue along the developmental trajectory. According to Erikson’s epigenetic model, adolescence is determined by the quest for identity formation at the risk of role diffusion. The onset of important advances such as the development of sexual and romantic attractions, the formation of political and ethical opinions, and the willingness to differ in opinion from both peers and adult caregivers are all key elements of these psychological changes.

Adolescence is a time to search for ideals and social values that can be carried into adulthood. Erickson used the term moratorium to describe the interim period between the concrete thinking of childhood and a more evolved complex abstract thinking and ethical development. He defined identity crisis as a normative task of adolescence in which adolescents pursue alternative behaviors and styles and then successfully mold these different experiences into a solid identity. A failure to do so results in identity diffusion, or role confusion, in which there is a lack of cohesive or confident sense of identity. Successful development creates the primary psychological virtue of fidelity, characterized by the ability to relate to others and form genuine relationships—setting the stage for the next developmental phase, intimacy versus isolation. Adolescence is the time to bond with peers, experiment with new beliefs and styles, fall in love for the first time, figure out who one wants to be and where one fits in, and explore creative ideas for future plans. The literature leads to the conclusion that the majority of adolescents go through this developmental process with basic optimism and good self-esteem, maintain good peer relationships, and sustain basically harmonious relationships with their families.

### WHAT IS TYPICAL IN ADOLESCENCE?

The conceptualization of adolescence as a continual struggle, with its defining feature of intense psychic pain and severe turmoil, was a historical notion. The hypothesis that most adolescents experience enduring severe psychological distress, intense depressive symptoms, severe mood lability, and a disruption of their usual conduct was not borne out by epidemiologic studies. Although certain psychiatric disorders, such as major depression, schizophrenia, and bipolar disorders, emerge with greater frequency in adolescents than in younger children, the majority of adolescents are free of psychiatric disorders. For up to approximately 75% of youth, adolescence is a period of successful adaptation to physical, cognitive, and emotional changes, largely continuous with their previous functioning. Thus, the majority of adolescents display reasonable autonomy in decision making, increasing independence in completing academic work, and successful peer relationships without significant alienation from their families or the emergence of conduct-disordered behaviors. Psychological maladjustment, self-loathing, disturbance of conduct, substance abuse, affective disorders, and other impairing psychiatric disorders emerge in approximately 20% of the adolescent population. Adolescent adjustment is, in general, continuous with previous psychological function; thus, psychologically disturbed children are at greater risk for psychiatric disorders during adolescence. Adolescents with psychiatric disorders are at increased risk for greater conflicts with families and for feeling alienated from their families. Although up to 60% of adolescents endorse occasional distress or a psychiatric symptom, this group of adolescents functions well-academically and with peers and describes themselves as generally satisfied with their lives. Studies conducted since the 1980s indicate that most adolescents endorse more acceptance than rejection of their parents’ values and lifestyles, and few report feelings of isolation or alienation from their families.

The developmentalist Erik Erikson characterized the normative task of adolescence as identity versus role confusion. In other words, adolescents, faced with the task of applying childhood talents, strengths, and experiences to their evolving physical and emotional selves, must be able successfully to change “who they are” so as to build a more mature identity. The integration of past experiences with current changes takes place in what Erikson termed ego identity. Adolescents express various aspects of their psychological selves by becoming fans of heroes or other well-known musical or political idols. Some adolescents appear consumed by their identification with a particular idol, whereas others are more moderate in their expression. Adolescents who feel accepted by a peer group are less likely to become consumed by adoration of an idol. Adolescents who are socially isolated, feel socially rejected, and become overly identified with an idol to the exclusion of all other activities are at higher risk for serious emotional problems and require psychiatric evaluation.

In addition, all adolescent behavior is influenced by personal traits, such as temperament, and by the environment, including relationships with peers, siblings, and parents and the community in which the teenager lives. Consequently, there is a great deal of heterogeneity in adolescent behavior. To further complicate matters, developmental advances are never linear. Progress is punctuated by periods of regression to earlier stages. It takes considerable clinical acumen or a great deal of familiarity with a particular teenager to understand what is normal for one adolescent or pathologic for another.

Some researchers feel that viewing adolescence as a tumultuous period creates a setting in which adolescent tribulations and “out-of-control” behavior are viewed as normative and therefore not deserving of clinical attention. Researchers such as Offer have instead suggested that whereas most adolescents are in fact well adjusted and not psychologically troubled, the tendency to view adolescence as “normally difficult” potentially risks missing the population of youth who are legitimately suffering. In fact, research suggests that approximately 20% of the adolescent population in Western settings is seriously in need of clinical intervention. Some of this attention is needed for managing particularly charged crisis points in normal development for youth at risk, whereas other attention is required to treat the onset of many psychiatric disorders that achieve greater prevalence during adolescence. Among these are mood and anxiety disorders, schizophrenia, eating disorders, and substance use disorders. The suicide rate increases tremendously throughout adolescence: Data from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) show that the suicide is the second leading cause of death in youth aged 10 to 24 years. In addition, data from the Youth Risk Behavior Survey (YRBS) published by the CDC show that 18.8% of teens “seriously considered” suicide during the 12-month period during 2019. Prevalence estimates were highest among females (24.1%); White non-Hispanic students (19.1%); students who reported having sex with persons of the same sex or with both sexes (54.2%); and students who identified as lesbian, gay, or bisexual (46.8%).

Although the overall rates of suicide had decreased compared to the rates in 2018, there is growing concern about the increase in the rates of suicide among Black adolescents. Thus, the psychiatric treatment of adolescents has significant public health repercussions.

Much of these challenges are driven by the paradoxical lack of careful thought among adolescents, who nevertheless clearly have the capacity to embrace rational and abstract problem-solving approaches. In short, often the overall judgment and risk assessment of adolescent thinking is markedly different from that of adults. For example, whereas adults would rarely accept a challenge to drive recklessly on the freeway, such a scenario is not unheard of in late adolescent populations in spite of their growing capacity for sound judgment and abstract reasoning. Some researchers have suggested that the affectively charged environment of the adolescent leads to an increased willingness to take risks, sometimes called a “hot cognition” in the cognitive developmental literature. Again, this is best understood in the setting of the incomplete neurodevelopment outlined earlier. Thus, whereas adolescents might appear adultlike in their thinking during calmer moments, affectively volatile environments can pose a particular risk to their well-being. These observations serve to accentuate the need to view adolescent behavior as potentially in need of clinical attention.

### EFFECTIVE ADOLESCENT THERAPY

The treatment of adolescent patients is quite different from the treatment of latency-age or adult patients. Adolescents can seem both extremely childish and surprisingly mature. In addition, the same adolescent population may undergo rapid and unnerving developmental changes in a relatively short amount of time. These issues substantially complicate the clinician’s task. Forming an effective alliance with an adolescent patient requires special attention to already important clinical considerations such as confidentiality and safety. For example, the patient who admits to using illegal drugs may not want their parents to know but may acknowledge a desire for help in understanding this growing drug problem. Although patient safety is always the most important clinical consideration, clinicians must view each situation on a case-by-case basis. Often, adolescents come to their treaters for guidance on issues that they are not able to discuss at length with their parents. A friendly, honest, flexible, and available clinician is absolutely essential for the adolescent to feel comfortable in coming forward with important and pressing issues.

Clinicians also need to be curious about their adolescent patients’ worlds. Adolescents will express themselves wonderfully in displacement, finding voices in the music they listen to, in the television they watch, and in the stories and movies they embrace. Although it is neither reasonable nor possible for a clinician to stay abreast of the pantheon of popular culture, inviting adolescent patients to discuss aspects of their culture that excite them is a valuable means of building trust and of gaining insight.

### SPECIAL POPULATIONS

#### Children and Adolescents

Poor Mental Health. Mental ill-health is among the leading causes of disability in young persons aged 5 to 14 years, ranking in second place in Europe and the Americas. The prevalence of mental health disorders among children aged 3 to 17 years in the United States is estimated to be 7.1% for anxiety, 7.4% for behavioral/conduct problems, and 3.2% for depression.

The results of the Saving and Empowering Young Lives in Europe (SEYLE) study showed that in a usual school class of teenagers, approximately 10% of young people display high-risk behaviors like excessive alcohol and illegal drug use, heavy smoking, truancy, etc. Thirty percent display unhealthy lifestyles like poor sleep and diet, physical inactivity, and excessive Internet/media use. Both of those groups have high levels of depression, anxiety, and suicidal behaviors. Approximately 12.5% of adolescents required qualified psychiatric and psychological help. However, experiences from the SEYLE study in Europe and the Columbia University TeenScreen program in the United States show that there is a fear of stigma when screening for mental health problems in schools, and both parents and adolescents show little trust in the mental health care systems. Therefore, the process of destigmatization of screening for mental disorders and increasing help-seeking behaviors is an important public health issue. SEYLE results show that younger adolescents are more likely to adhere to rules, procedures, and recommendations with higher attendance rates in the health care system for girls and for pupils victimized by peers. Suicidal behaviors also appear to be an important predictor of help-seeking and a predictor of referral to mental health care.

Risk for Suicide. A meta-analysis calculated a pooled suicide rate of 6.04 per 100,000 in 15 to 19 years adolescents, which is far higher than the rate found in 10- to 14-year-olds (0.93 per 100,000). Suicidal thoughts and behaviors at this age are also common, with a lifetime prevalence of about 30% for suicidal ideation and 4% to 10% for suicide attempts. For most adults, it is difficult to acknowledge the child’s despair and suicidality. Caretakers, as well as parents, can deny serious childhood diagnoses due to frustrations and feelings of guilt when having difficulties handling a suicidal, depressed child or a suicidal child with serious conduct difficulties. Suicidal behavior in children and adolescents often occurs in the context of a poor family environment characterized by low perceived support, parent-child conflicts, and a lack of family cohesion. Parental suicide and psychopathology, particularly affective disorders and alcohol use, also increase the suicide risk in offspring. Suicidal children and adolescents have poor self-esteem and poor personal identity, are often truant, not seldom bullied, and have poor school grades, which leads to a sense of inadequacy. Impulsive-aggressive traits and impaired decision-making have also been described. Issues of gender identity are well recognized as risk factors for adolescent suicides. The presence of a mental health condition is associated with a 10 times higher risk of suicide death in young people, especially in cases of affective disorder, disruptive/conduct disorders, and substance use disorders. Attempted suicide is an important risk factor for future suicide.
