## Answer

 ## **Tuberculosis (TB)**

**Tuberculosis** is caused by bacteria in the *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* complex, most commonly *M. tuberculosis*. It primarily affects the lungs but can involve almost any organ. If properly treated, drug-susceptible TB is curable in most cases; untreated disease may be fatal. Transmission usually occurs through airborne droplet nuclei from people with infectious pulmonary TB. (1)

### **Clinical features**
Common symptoms include:

- Persistent cough, initially often nonproductive, later with sputum
- Fever, night sweats, weight loss, anorexia, malaise, and weakness
- Hemoptysis, which may occasionally be massive
- Pleuritic chest pain or dyspnea in more extensive disease

Absence of fever does not exclude TB. (2)

Postprimary or adult-type TB commonly affects the apical and posterior upper-lobe segments and may produce cavitary disease. Chest radiographs or CT scans may demonstrate infiltrates, cavities, or miliary nodules. (3)

### **Extrapulmonary TB**
Common sites include the lymph nodes, pleura, genitourinary tract, bones and joints, meninges, peritoneum, and pericardium. Lymph-node TB typically presents as painless cervical or supraclavicular swelling. (4)

Tuberculous meningitis may present with headache, fever, malaise, altered mental status, neck rigidity, cranial-nerve abnormalities, hydrocephalus, and intracranial hypertension. Lumbar puncture is central to diagnosis; cerebrospinal-fluid culture remains the diagnostic gold standard, while Xpert MTB/RIF is a preferred initial diagnostic test. (5)

### **Diagnosis**
Diagnosis may involve:

- History and physical examination
- Chest imaging
- Tuberculin skin testing or interferon-γ release assays
- Acid-fast bacillus staining
- Mycobacterial culture
- Molecular diagnostic testing

Latent TB infection and active TB disease require different assessments and treatment approaches. (6)

### **Latent TB infection**
Regimens described in the selected reference include:

- **Isoniazid plus rifapentine** weekly for 3 months
- **Rifampin** daily for 4 months
- **Isoniazid plus rifampin** daily for 3 months
- **Isoniazid** for 6–9 months

Shorter rifamycin-based regimens are generally preferred over prolonged isoniazid monotherapy because of effectiveness, safety, and tolerability. The 3-month weekly isoniazid–rifapentine regimen is described as the regimen of choice for many adults and children older than 2 years, but it is not recommended during pregnancy. (6)

### **Prevention**
Prevention includes rapid diagnosis and isolation of infectious cases, appropriate treatment until patients become noninfectious—usually 2–4 weeks after starting proper treatment—and preventive treatment for people with TB infection who are at high risk of active disease. BCG vaccination is an additional preventive strategy. (7)
