The three interacting dimensions that lead people to have psychosis are as follows:



1. Experiencing Reality: There are changes in how they are experiencing “reality”—for example, hallucinations, delusions, paranoia, or spiritual experiences as well as changes in how they process reality, like making idiosyncratic connections or relying on irrational “primary process” thinking.

2. Self-Identity: There are changes in their internal processing and self-identity—for example, how they hold themselves together, balance rational and irrational portions, self-observation, or create their own stories. It can also include “anhedonia,” the inability to feel joy or pleasure in life.

3. Relationships: There are changes in how they relate to other people—for example, disconnected, missing “normal” social signals, hard to relate to, socially anxious, avoidant, or even paranoid.

![images](images/ff12-15-1.jpg)

FIGURE 12.15–1. The Psychosis Triangle: Interacting dimensions of psychosis. None of these dimensions falling apart on its own creates psychosis. All three of them must be seriously impaired for someone to become psychotic. Any of the three can act as the initial weak point, putting its neighboring dimensions at risk. Once two dimensions are compromised, the third dimension in the triangle is under severe strain, and if it also falls, psychosis is the result—or it could be a stronghold to rebuild the other two dimensions, leading to recovery. The “prodromal” period is usually a time when self-identity and relationships are deteriorating, making the person vulnerable to distortions in reality.

In contrast with the medical model that only focuses on the Experiencing Reality dimension, often sacrificing the other two dimensions along the way, the recovery model is open to all three dimensions.

Using all three dimensions allows us to integrate and appreciate not just biologic factors but also personal meaning, such as hope creation, role modeling, identity formation, and social factors, like structural racism, mentoring, and productive social integration.

One of our authors, Chacku Mathai, is a thoughtful peer advocate who effectively integrates all three dimensions (adapting to the unusual way he experiences reality including hearing voices, some of which are more trustworthy than others, intersecting personal identities, and navigating relationships within a racial, immigrant context). In this section, he models synthesizing his unique personal journey and experiences with the overarching social and professional contexts that envelop all of us.

Understanding the experiences of people diagnosed with schizophrenia and/or other psychotic disorders, especially experiences of recovery, is an incredible challenge due to the wide heterogeneity of experience, even among those with the same diagnosis. To add to the challenge, the history of psychiatry, psychiatric diagnosis, and the evolving characterization and naming of mental illness, psychosis, and schizophrenia are inextricably linked to the social and political forces, such as racism, that marginalized and oppressed certain groups throughout our history.
