### PREGNANCY

SSRIs are the most frequently used class of medications to treat depression among women of childbearing age. It is therefore not surprising that they are the most commonly used psychotropic drugs during pregnancy. Except for paroxetine, the SSRIs are considered relatively safe to take during pregnancy when deemed necessary for the treatment of the mother.

Women who are pregnant or planning a pregnancy and are taking SSRIs should consult with their physician about whether to continue taking their medication. As with all medications that treat serious disorders, such as those treated by SSRIs, the potential benefits and risks of SSRI treatment need to be weighed against the potential risks of not treating, given that pregnancy does not protect against the serious disorders these drugs treat.

A prospective study giving a choice to continue or stop medication for depression during pregnancy showed a higher rate of relapse following medication discontinuation, and women resuming their antidepressant regimen did not do as well as those who did not interrupt treatment.

Although studies have shown conflicting results, depression during pregnancy has been associated with a greater risk of obstetrical complications, premature delivery, pre-eclampsia, and a lower likelihood of breastfeeding initiation. Newborns of these women may be at an increased risk of low birth weight, and irritability, and may exhibit lower activity and interactive behaviors. Peripartum depression may also impact the maternal-fetal attachment process. A recent meta-analysis found a small-to-moderate association between antenatal anxiety and/or depression in mothers and performing worse offspring in social-emotional, cognitive, language, and adaptive behavior development. Interestingly, these findings were not limited to infancy but extended to childhood and adolescence.

Studies that assessed the risks associated with taking SSRIs during pregnancy have yielded conflicting results. This reflects the difficulty of studying this topic, as conducting randomized controlled trials on this matter poses ethical problems. Hence, the evidence is mostly derived from observational studies, and residual confounding is very likely. Among the possible risks identified, meta-analyses found an increased risk of preterm birth associated with the use of medications for depression (not limited to SSRIs) during pregnancy. The possibility of an association between SSRI use during pregnancy and autism has also been suggested but remains controversial.

A Centers for Disease Control and Prevention epidemiologic study on the causes of major birth defects among 9,622 case infants and 4,092 controls looked at the use of fluoxetine, sertraline, paroxetine, and citalopram during early pregnancy. It found that these drugs as a group did not significantly increase the risk for most birth defects, including congenital heart defects. The study did find an association between SSRI use and a slightly increased risk for three specific birth defects: anencephaly (adjusted odds ratio [OR] = 2.4; 95% CI 1.1–5.1), craniosynostosis (adjusted OR 2.5; 95% CI 1.5–4.0), and omphalocele (adjusted OR = 2.8; 95% CI, 1.3–5.7). Nevertheless, the authors emphasized that the absolute risks for these birth defects were small.

<small>SEROTONIN REUPTAKE INHIBITORS (SRIS = SSRIS AND SNRIS).</small>



All SRIs are generally considered compatible with pregnancy. Some studies, largely those with inappropriate control groups, have demonstrated a statistically significant, though arguably not clinically relevant, relationship between SRI use and congenital teratogenicity. There was significant concern about the association of paroxetine with cardiac septal defects, resulting in the FDA revising its label to category D in 2005. However, subsequent meta-analyses failed to reproduce the relationship, challenging the conclusion that paroxetine is a cardiac teratogen. More recently, several large, methodologically rigorous studies have demonstrated no significant relationship between cardiac malformations and SRI use in the first trimester. The possible link between late pregnancy (after 20 weeks of gestation) exposure to SRIs and persistent pulmonary hypertension of the newborn (PPHN) also created public alarm and FDA label changes warning of risk in 2006, which was then rescinded in 2011. An increasing number of studies and meta-analyses draw contrasting conclusions and do not adequately control for confounders such as maternal depression severity, preterm birth, and mode of delivery. Current information, therefore, does not support SRI discontinuation due to PPHN risk.

Spontaneous abortion rates were initially estimated to be higher among women taking antidepressants. Significant limitations of this early research include lack of control for psychiatric status; lack of control for a history of prior miscarriage; and the possibility that therapeutic abortions were misrepresented as miscarriages due to stigma. Several analyses in a large cohort found no increased risk attributable to SRIs when controlling for depression status. Additionally, SRI use was not associated with an increased risk of stillbirth, neonatal death, or postneonatal death in a second well-controlled, large study.

Antidepressant exposure in late pregnancy has been significantly associated with lower gestational age and lower APGAR scores at 1 and 5 minutes, even with proper controls for psychiatric status. SRI exposure was associated with lower infant birth weight only when maternal psychiatric status was unaccounted for. Importantly, these effects are quite low and of questionable clinical significance. A 1.2- to 2-fold increased risk of preterm delivery, defined as birth prior to 37 weeks of gestation, was associated with in utero SRI exposure in a meta-analysis that controlled for illness exposure; however other characteristics of women who maintain meds through pregnancy could confer residual confounding. Depression has also been associated with preterm birth, but additional research is needed.

A neonatal adaptation syndrome affects approximately 30% of late pregnancy, SRI-exposed newborns, and substantially more preterm infants are affected. Features of poor neonatal adaptation include respiratory difficulty, hypoglycemia, hypertonia, jitteriness, vomiting, seizures, and sleep disturbances. The syndrome appears to be time-limited, with the greatest incidence in the first 48 hours postbirth but potentially extending to 2 weeks postbirth. While one study provided evidence for similar rates of neonatal signs in the absence of SRI exposure 2 weeks prior to delivery, thus implicating maternal or other neonatal factors in this syndrome, another study found increased adaptation syndrome risk even after control for maternal characteristics. Long-term sequelae of this syndrome have not been adequately studied; one study found continued differences in response to visual and auditory stimuli in SRI-exposed babies compared to unexposed at 30 days postdelivery.

Concerns have been raised about the use of paroxetine during pregnancy and cardiovascular malformations in the newborn. In a study using Swedish national registry data, the risk of a cardiac defect was about 2% in infants exposed to paroxetine during pregnancy versus 1% among all registry infants. Another study using a U.S. insurance claims database compared the risk of congenital cardiac malformations in infants of women who received paroxetine versus other medications for depression in the first trimester. The results showed a trend toward increased risk with paroxetine (OR = 1.5; 95% CI 0.8–2.9). Most of the cardiac defects observed in these studies were atrial or ventricular septal defects, conditions in which the wall between the right and left sides of the heart is not completely developed. In general, septal defects are among the most common types of congenital malformations. They range from those that are symptomatic and may require surgery to those that are asymptomatic and may resolve on their own. It is generally recommended to avoid paroxetine during the first trimester of pregnancy or in women who plan to become pregnant in the near future.

Neonates exposed to SSRIs in the third trimester may develop poor neonatal adaptation syndrome (PNAS). This refers to short-term neurobehavioral effects in newborns. Symptoms such as poor muscle tone, trouble sleeping, shaking, weak or excessive crying, irritability, hypoglycemia, or seizures can be seen. It has been reported to affect up to 30% of exposed neonates and may represent discontinuation syndrome. The symptoms appear shortly after birth and may last up to two weeks. A meta-analysis found an increased risk of PNAS among neonates exposed to a medication for depression in utero (OR = 5.07; 95% CI, 3.25–7.90; P <.0001).

Another possible pregnancy-related side effect in offspring of SSRI-treated mothers is persistent pulmonary hypertension of the newborn (PPHN), a condition associated with substantial infant mortality and morbidity. A large cohort study examined the risk of PPHN associated with exposure to an SSRI in the 90 days before delivery versus no exposure. A total of 102,179 women had been exposed to SSRIs late in pregnancy. The authors found that 322 SSRI-exposed newborns (31.5; 95% CI, 28.3–35.2 per 10,000 births) were diagnosed with PPHN, compared to 7,630 newborns not exposed to medication for depression (20.8; 95% CI, 20.4–21.3 per 10,000 births). The unadjusted OR was 1.51 (95% CI, 1.35–1.69), and the OR became statistically nonsignificant when restricting to women diagnosed with depression and adjusting for confounders (OR = 1.10; 95% CI, 0.94–1.29). This data suggests that the risk of PPHN in newborns exposed to SSRIs late in pregnancy is low and may even be lower than previously thought.
