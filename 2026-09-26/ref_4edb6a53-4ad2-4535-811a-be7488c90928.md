### INSOMNIA DISORDER

Insomnia is one of the most common medical and sleep complaints, and it is frequently comorbid with most medical and psychiatric disorders. The diagnosis requires three main features: persistent sleep difficulty, adequate sleep opportunity, and associated daytime impairment. For insomnia to be considered chronic, the symptoms must occur at least three times per week for at least 3 months. Patients may complain of difficulty falling and/or staying asleep or early morning wakening. All other etiologies (e.g., primary sleep disorder, comorbid medical or psychiatric conditions, medications) must be ruled out before making this diagnosis.

#### Treatment of Insomnia

Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) and pharmacotherapy are the main treatment options for chronic insomnia. A mounting body of research has found that in older adults, the risk of sleep medication side effects (including impaired balance, dizziness, grogginess, and confusion) and the potential risk of long-term effects on cognition is significant. In contrast, CBT-I is safe, the effects are long lasting, and it is endorsed as a first-line treatment for chronic insomnia by multiple societies and guideline panels.

Behavioral Treatments. CBT-I has been shown to have excellent short- and long-term effectiveness in both younger and older adults. This multicomponent approach addresses common thoughts and maladaptive behaviors that interfere with sleep. A typical program consists of 6 to 10 weekly sessions administered by a specially trained clinician. Behavioral components include establishing a strict bedtime/wake time, reduction in time in bed, and sleep hygiene. Cognitive restructuring involves changing unrealistic beliefs or irrational fears about sleep and/or the loss of sleep. Finally, patients are educated about sleep behaviors that promote sleep and those practices that may be interfering with their sleep (Tables 55.3c–1 and 55.3c–2).

The use of CBT-I has been limited by a lack of trained clinicians and the requirement of 6 to 10 sessions for therapy. An abbreviated form of CBT-I called brief behavioral therapy for insomnia (BBTI) was created to overcome these impediments. A course of BBTI consists of four sessions, three of which last 30 minutes or less, some by phone, and can be administered by nurses within the primary care setting. BBTI is exclusively focused on behavior with the core components of stimulus control and sleep restriction. Several randomized trials have shown BBTI to be effective in older adults.

#### Diagnostic Workup of Insomnia



Diagnostic workup of insomnia may include thyroid function tests, glycosylated hemoglobin level, complete blood count, serum iron studies, ferritin level, liver function tests, renal functions tests, and other tests that may be indicated depending on the clinical presentations and comorbid conditions.

Sleep diaries are a cost-effective and reliable way to assess the sleep–wake cycle in individuals with insomnia. The most widely used self-evaluating questionnaires are the Insomnia Severity Index (ISI) and Epworth Sleepiness Scale. Polysomnography is not routinely recommended for the initial assessment of individuals with insomnia but is used in the assessment of patients suspected of suffering from other sleep disorders (such as sleep apnea, periodic limb movement disorder) associated with insomnia, and individuals with chronic insomnia disorders that fail to respond to treatment.

#### Treating Insomnia

In general, nonpharmacologic interventions are the first line for insomnia treatment. However, the use of pharmacologic agents is frequently requested by patients due to their faster effect, and therefore the clinician should be aware of the risk of dependency and try to shorten the duration of drug use, and ensure the focus of treatment is not removed from nonpharmacologic therapy. If nonpharmacologic therapy is successful, then the patient should start to gradually discontinue drug treatment at the first opportunity.

Nonpharmacologic Treatment With Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Insomnia (CBTi). This treatment modality combines behavioral and cognitive techniques to overcome dysfunctional sleep behaviors and misperceptions, and distorted, disruptive thoughts about sleep. The therapist begins with a careful clinical interview to assess the insomnia’s etiology, chronicity, severity, associations, and comorbid conditions. A treatment plan is then designed using relevant and appropriate cognitive and behavioral techniques. These may include universal sleep hygiene, stimulus control therapy, sleep restriction therapy, relaxation therapies and biofeedback, cognitive therapy, and occasionally, paradoxical intention. With recent advances in technology and telehealth, various forms of CBTi can be delivered with no need for in-person visits.

Studies repeatedly show significant, sustained improvement in sleep symptoms with CBTi. Improvements include reduced wakefulness duration, decreased number of awakenings, and shorter latency to sleep onset. Short-term benefits are similar to that of medication, but CBTi tends to have lasting benefits, even 36 months after treatment. With the cessation of the medication, insomnia frequently returns and is sometimes accompanied by rebound insomnia. CBTi has not been shown to produce any adverse effects. There are no established “best practice” guidelines for the length or quantity of sessions. CBTi, however, is not without limitations. Most data do not compare the efficacy of the individual components (described below) of CBTi. However, sleep hygiene education alone does not appear to improve sleep. Intuitively, the multicomponent approach addresses many of the variables contributing to insomnia. Other limitations of CBTi include lack or unavailability of trained specialists, cost of sessions, and noncoverage by insurance carriers.

<small>ACCEPTANCE AND COMMITMENT THERAPY (ACT).</small>

Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) includes six processes or skills and is another modality for the management of insomnia. These processes include Acceptance, Cognitive Defusion, Being Present, Self as Context, Values, and Committed Action. In fact, after receiving these skills, the patient increases psychological flexibility. Psychological flexibility is the ability to behave adaptively and in accordance with values despite having painful experiences. ACT has promising effects on insomnia, anxiety, and depression management.

<small>PARADOXICAL INTENTION.</small>

This is a cognitive technique with conflicting evidence regarding its efficacy. In clinical practice, compliance is often a barrier, but it does work for a limited number of patients. The theory is that performance anxiety interferes with sleep onset. So when the patient tries to stay awake for as long as possible, rather than fall asleep, performance anxiety will be reduced, and sleep latency will improve.

Pharmacologic Treatment. For many years, BZDs were the most commonly prescribed sedative hypnotic medications for treating insomnia. BZRAs have replaced BZDs as the standard for sedative hypnotic medications used to treat insomnia. BZRAs include zolpidem, zaleplon, and eszopiclone. Subsequent development of a low-dose antihistamine (doxepin), melatonin receptor agonists (ramelteon and tasimelteon), and orexin antagonists (suvorexant and lemborexant) have more recently augmented the armamentarium of medications approved for treating insomnia. However, unapproved off-label (and not recommended) use of sedating antidepressants is common. Low-dose atypical antipsychotics (olanzapine, risperidone, and quetiapine) are effective in the treatment of paradoxical insomnia in patients who manifest with delusional features and do not respond to psychoeducation. These agents may also be helpful in patients with other subtypes of insomnia who have not responded to other sedative hypnotic agents. It seems they can be used to treat insomnia; however, the risk of adverse side effects such as metabolic syndrome should not be overlooked. Additionally, various over-the-counter (OTC) sleep aids are available. Nonprescription formulas include sedating antihistamines, protein precursors, and other substances. L-tryptophan was popular and readily available at health food stores until an outbreak of eosinophilia led to it being pulled off the shelves. Melatonin is the leader among self-administered food additives believed by some to alleviate sleeplessness. Essentially, low-dose melatonin can improve sleep when used to align or re-entrain the circadian rhythm with the desired sleep–wake schedule.

#### Insomnia

Insomnia is common in the general pediatric population and is certainly more common in youth with psychiatric disorders due to the disorders themselves as well as a sequela of the medications prescribed to treat the primary disorder. Pharmacologic treatment may be indicated if children do not show significant improvement following behavioral intervention. However, excluding other primary causes of dyssomnia (e.g., obstructive sleep apnea, iron deficiency anemia) is critical prior to pursuing pharmacotherapy, as pharmacotherapy could mask an underlying disorder.

Pharmacotherapy for Insomnia. There are no FDA-approved sleep agents for children. After aggressive sleep hygiene and education, controlled trials have demonstrated improved sleep latency outcomes with melatonin. Data regarding other agents used chronically are largely derived from expert opinion or case series, including benzodiazepines, clonidine, mirtazapine, imipramine, trazodone, and, less frequently, sedating atypical antipsychotics.

The primary regulator of the sleep–wake cycle is melatonin, an endogenous hormone produced by the pineal gland in response to changes in retinal light perception. Exogenous melatonin supplementation may be the preferred initial pharmacotherapy for sleep-onset insomnia due to its chronobiotic properties. In clinical studies of children and adolescents, both immediate- and extended-release melatonin reduced sleep onset latency and increased total sleep duration in pediatric patients, although the increase in total duration of sleep is greater with extended-release preparations. Additionally, melatonin may have a particularly important role in circadian rhythm sleep disorders. In this regard, low-dose melatonin (0.5 mg), when timed relative to the endogenous dim light melatonin onset, is more effective in shifting the sleep phase than higher doses, suggesting that timing may have a greater impact than dosage. Data regarding melatonin administration suggest that the optimal timing for melatonin administration is 4 to 6 hours prior to a child’s preferred bedtime, and doses of 0.5 to 1 mg have been effective when given in this window. Variation across studies has contributed to a lack of consensus regarding pediatric melatonin dosing. For example, 0.05 mg/kg may be a minimal effective dose when given 1 to 2 hours prior to bedtime. Finally, studies of selective GABAergic modulators and benzodiazepines have not produced positive results in prospective trials of youth with insomnia.
