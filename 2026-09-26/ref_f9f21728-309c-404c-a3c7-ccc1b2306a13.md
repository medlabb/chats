While NMS is one of the few life-threatening psychotropic-induced complications, its infrequent occurrence and severe nature make controlled studies impossible. The best available data supports the following strategies:

1. Prevention: Antipsychotics should be used cautiously in patients with dehydration, catatonic symptoms, basal ganglionic disease, or a history of NMS or MH.

2. Early identification: Patients with early signs should have their antipsychotic immediately discontinued, supportive measures initiated, and careful reintroduction of these agents considered only if absolutely necessary.

3. Treatment: The most important step is early recognition and prompt withdrawal of the offending agent. All cases require supportive interventions, which may include IV hydration, temperature control, and, in more severe cases, ventilator support or dialysis. In advanced cases, admission to an intensive care unit is required, with a fatal outcome occurring in up to 20% of cases. A variety of other treatments may also help, including the use of dopamine agonists (e.g., bromocriptine, amantadine); muscle relaxants (e.g., benzodiazepines, dantrolene); and, in selected patients, electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) (Table 33.2c–4). There has been some limited success reported with psychotropic agents such as bupropion (Wellbutrin), levodopa (Sinemet), carbamazepine (Tegretol), and apomorphine (Apokyn).

4. In patients who require an antipsychotic for their ongoing disorder after an episode of NMS, several steps may reduce the chance of recurrence. These include waiting at least 2 weeks after symptoms resolve before reintroduction is attempted, resolving prior risk factors (e.g., dehydration; avoiding concomitant lithium), and considering alternative therapies when appropriate (e.g., mood stabilizers; ECT). If rechallenge with an antipsychotic is necessary, choosing an alternate agent, starting with low doses and titrating slowly, can also reduce the risk of recurrence.

<small>CLOZAPINE-ASSOCIATED MYOCARDITIS OR CARDIOMYOPATHY.</small>

Infrequently, clozapine treatment may precipitate myocarditis or cardiomyopathy. If patients taking clozapine develop new cardiac or respiratory symptoms concerning for myocarditis or cardiomyopathy, a medical evaluation should ensue with the assistance of specialists. Medical and laboratory assessment may include ECG, evaluation of cardiac biomarkers such as troponin, and echocardiography. Echocardiography is the visualization of cardiac anatomy by the use of computer-transformed echoes of ultrasound.

Psychiatry was consulted to evaluate a 36-year-old woman with schizophrenia who had been started on clozapine approximately 1 month ago and had been admitted to medicine 1 day ago with fever, tachycardia, and dyspnea. The primary team was concerned about NMS due to her autonomic symptoms. In addition to holding the clozapine, the psychiatrist recommended a cardiology evaluation due to the possibility that the patient’s symptoms represented clozapine-induced myocarditis. The patient had nonspecific ECG changes, elevated cardiac troponins, and left ventricular dysfunction on echocardiogram; myocarditis was diagnosed.

<small>NEUROLEPTIC MALIGNANT SYNDROME.</small>

Neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS) is a rare, potentially fatal consequence of neuroleptic administration. The syndrome consists of altered mental status, generalized rigidity (often severe), hyperthermia, and autonomic instability. Sustained muscle contraction, as well as impaired central mechanisms of thermoregulation, result in peripheral heat generation and muscle breakdown. Muscle breakdown contributes to elevated levels of creatine kinase (CK). Leukocytosis and low serum iron concentration are common. Myoglobinuric acute renal failure may occur due to rhabdomyolysis. Liver enzymes may become elevated. Patients may die from hyperpyrexia, aspiration pneumonia, renal failure, hepatic failure, respiratory arrest, or cardiovascular collapse. Treatment includes discontinuation of the neuroleptic, hydration, and general supportive care. Administration of dantrolene (a muscle relaxant) or bromocriptine (a dopamine agonist) may be considered, along with other treatments that have anecdotal success, including amantadine, benzodiazepines, or ECT. Data is limited with regard to the efficacy of any of these specific therapies.
