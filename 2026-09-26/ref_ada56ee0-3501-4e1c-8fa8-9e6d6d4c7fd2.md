Reports of successful breastfeeding and healthy infants of well-informed and clinically stable mothers treated with lithium have shifted perceptions surrounding the use of lithium in lactating women. The mean concentration of lithium in breast milk is approximately half that of the maternal serum concentration, ranging from 20% to 70%. Lithium concentrations in the serum of breastfeeding infants were, on average, one-third of the level found in maternal serum, but with considerable variability, with infant-to-maternal serum concentration ratios ranging from 5% to 67%. However, given the large range in concentrations observed, laboratory monitoring of infant serum lithium, TSH, blood urea nitrogen, and creatinine immediately postpartum and up to 6 weeks of age is recommended, with ongoing monitoring every 2 to 3 months thereafter for the duration of breastfeeding. Long-term neurobehavioral assessments have found no difference in exposure relative to nonexposed siblings in the two studies. Recent literature suggests that lithium presents less risk to the breastfed infant than previously thought. Women who are will be well served by clinicians that engage them in personalized discussions surrounding the risks and benefits of breastfeeding while taking lithium.

<small>ANTIEPILEPTIC DRUGS (AEDS).</small>

Our understanding of the safety and risks associated with the use of antiepileptic drugs (AEDs) during pregnancy and breastfeeding is largely drawn from neurology literature. Convergent studies from numerous AED registries associate first-trimester use of carbamazepine and valproate with neural tube defects in 0.5% to 5% and major malformation in up to 16% of exposed offspring, with valproate conferring a greater risk than carbamazepine. Valproate was also associated with greater fetal death rates than other AEDs. This finding led to the recommendation that women taking AEDs take an increased dose of daily folate (4 mg/day), thereby mitigating some of the risk of neural tube defects. Other than neural tube defects, reported malformations include heart defects, urogenital defects, and oral clefts. Oxcarbazepine, while related to and like carbamazepine, has been little studied. Experimental animal studies indicate that risks are similar to those of carbamazepine, but human data are lacking.

Lamotrigine has gained attention as a therapy for bipolar depression in pregnant women, as registry data have demonstrated no difference in rates of neural tube and cardiac malformations compared to the general population. Initial studies of comparably small population size found a small increase in the incidence of oral clefts among infants of mothers taking lamotrigine. More recent, larger studies have found no increased risk of facial malformations. A case-control study in 2016 further supported the favorable profile of lamotrigine, showing no difference in orofacial cleft or cleft palate rates among infants exposed and unexposed during pregnancy. Of note, lamotrigine carries FDA approval for the treatment of bipolar depression. However, it has not demonstrated efficacy in managing acute mania. It should also be remembered that estrogen has a major effect on glucuronidation, thus substantially increasing clearance of lamotrigine across pregnancy and likely necessitating higher doses. While there are, regrettably, no clear guidelines for psychiatry on prophylactic dose changes of lamotrigine in pregnancy, clinicians can be guided by the guidelines for neurology.

It is also crucial to consider the timing of making decisions about medication use in pregnancy. Ideally, pregnancy should be considered when prescribing medications for any reproductive-aged woman, regardless of her plans (as nearly 50% of pregnancies are unplanned). Ideally, a thorough conversation of the risks of untreated illness versus the risks of medication use should be conducted with each woman well before a planned pregnancy to allow time to observe the effects of any suggested modifications. Because limiting exposure to both medication and illness is the goal, women taking small doses of numerous medications might be able to pare down to fewer medications at larger doses if planning a pregnancy. Similarly, women taking a newer drug with less evidence for compatibility with pregnancy, and a history that does not include lack of response or tolerance to older drugs, might trial a drug with more evidence if planning a pregnancy. But if the same woman were to present already pregnant, the calculation is different; switching to another drug is another exposure for the fetus, and unknown efficacy for the mother may mean even more exposures down the line. Timing within the pregnancy itself also matters. While few drugs are associated with congenital teratogenicity, for those that are, it is important to remember when the period of organ formation occurs; if a pregnancy has already advanced beyond that point at presentation, there is no utility to stopping the drug (and possibly considerable potential for harm).

Finally, it is crucial to take into account pregnancy changes to metabolism and volume of distribution and their effects on serum concentration of drugs. Many clinicians are tempted to reduce medication doses in pregnancy (on the theory that this will pose less risk to the fetus). In fact, there is no evidence to support this practice, and we know that serum concentrations of many drugs will be 40% to 50% lower at the end of pregnancy—meaning that many women will require higher rather than lower doses of drugs to maintain their mental health. There are unfortunately as yet no guidelines to inform prophylactic dose changes in pregnancy, but clinicians would be wise to monitor symptoms carefully and react to relapses quickly. For those drugs that have commercially available tests for blood level (such as lithium, lamotrigine, and the TCAs), incorporating frequent levels across pregnancy can inform decisions about dose changes.

With the exception of the risk of rash, lamotrigine is generally well tolerated. Although transient difficulties with headache may be encountered during the initiation phase, other difficulties such as sedation, neurocognitive adverse effects, sexual problems, or weight gain are not typically seen.

The most common lamotrigine adverse effects during dose escalation (added to standard therapy) phase in adult bipolar disorder patients were headache, rash, dizziness, diarrhea, dream abnormality, and pruritus. In the lamotrigine monotherapy phase in maintenance treatment studies in adult bipolar disorder patients, the most common adverse effects were nausea, insomnia, somnolence, back pain, fatigue, rash, and rhinitis, and the adverse effects most commonly leading to discontinuation were rash and manic/hypomanic/mixed mood symptoms.

In late 2008, the FDA announced a class warning regarding increased risk of suicidality (suicidal behavior or ideation) in patients with epilepsy as well as psychiatric disorders for lamotrigine as well as 10 other anticonvulsants (carbamazepine, felbamate [Felbatol], gabapentin [Neurontin], levetiracetam [Keppra], oxcarbazepine [Trileptal], pregabalin [Lyrica], tiagabine [Gabitril], topiramate [Topamax], valproate, and zonisamide [Zonegran]). In the FDA’s analysis, anticonvulsants compared to placebo yielded approximately twice the risk of suicidality (0.43% vs. 0.22%). The relative risk for suicidality was higher in the patients with epilepsy compared to psychiatric disorders, suggesting that the risk in psychiatric patients was quite small. Acute oral overdoses of lamotrigine up to 15 g have caused central nervous system and cardiac adverse effects, and some have been fatal.

Lamotrigine was classified as pregnancy category C. Limited initial data suggested the possibility of a modest association between lamotrigine and cleft lip or palate, but this has not been replicated in later studies. Specifically, the North American Antiepileptic Drug Registry found a higher risk of oral cleft, which was not found in other pregnancy registries. Lamotrigine is excreted in breast milk, considered “unsafe” for lactation, and thus is contraindicated with breastfeeding.

Rare cases of lamotrigine-associated adverse events have been reported, including thrombocytosis and rash. Maternal levels should be monitored, and infant levels can be monitored when there is a concern for toxicity in the infant, possibly suggested by difficulty breathing, rash, poor feeding, or drowsiness. There have been no reports of adverse events clearly attributable to valproate; however, thrombocytopenia was reported in one case of a breastfed child whose mother was taking valproate, and hepatotoxicity remains a theoretical risk. While carbamazepine is generally well tolerated by the breastfed infant, some infants have demonstrated transient mild hepatic toxicity, poor feeding, and sedation associated with carbamazepine exposure. Infants should be monitored for jaundice, poor weight gain, and drowsiness. If there is a concern for elevated levels of carbamazepine, infant serum liver enzymes, blood counts, and a carbamazepine level can be assessed. When AED exposure is limited to breast milk, there is no evidence for adverse impact on IQ, verbal and nonverbal memory, or executive function by age 6.

In summary, while all antiepileptics are considered compatible with lactation, there are significant concerns about the use of carbamazepine and especially valproate in pregnancy, and they should be discontinued if possible. If used, folic acid supplementation (4 mg daily) is essential. A wise course of action is to prescribe these drugs in reproductive-aged women only when all other possible treatments have failed, thus answering in advance the question of whether it will be possible to discontinue. More information on these drugs is needed, and pregnancy registries have been established both in the United States (North American Antiepileptic Drug (AED) Pregnancy Registry: 1-888-AED-AED4 (1-888-233-2334) [http://www.massgeneral.org/aed/](http://www.massgeneral.org/aed/)) and in Europe (EURAP Registry (International Registry of Antiepileptic Drugs and Pregnancy): [http://www.eurapinternational.org/registry/index.php](http://www.eurapinternational.org/registry/index.php)).
