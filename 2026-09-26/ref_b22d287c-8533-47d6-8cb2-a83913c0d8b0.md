Severe iron deficiency anemia is characterized by small, pale erythrocytes ([Figure 23.1](30_chapter_23.xhtml#c23f1)) and RBC indices that indicate a low mean corpuscular volume (MCV) and low mean corpuscular Hb concentration. Additional laboratory studies usually demonstrate decreased serum iron levels, an increased total iron-binding capacity, a decrease in serum ferritin levels, and transferrin saturation (serum iron/total iron-binding capacity) less than 18%. In practice and owing to the high prevalence of iron deficiency among pregnant women with anemia, these studies are often reserved for patients unresponsive to presumptive treatment of iron deficiency anemia. In evaluating the patient with anemia, a recent dietary history is obviously important, especially if **pica** (consumption of non-nutrient substances such as starch, ice, or dirt) exists. Such dietary compulsions may contribute to iron deficiency by decreasing the amount of nutritious food and iron consumed.

Treatment of iron deficiency anemia generally requires an additional 60 to 180 mg of elemental iron per day, along with folate to maximize RBC production, in addition to the iron in the prenatal vitamin/mineral preparation. Oral supplementation is used in the first trimester and beyond as it is safe, inexpensive, and effective if tolerated and taken as prescribed. Iron absorption is facilitated by ingestion between meals or at bedtime on an empty stomach. More recently, it has been recognized that higher doses or multiple doses per day may increase gastrointestinal side effects and increase the risk for nonadherence without increasing absorption. Once-daily or every other day dosing is sufficient. Oral and currently available preparations of parenteral iron are both safe and effective in replacing iron stores. Parenteral iron may be considered after the first trimester for those women who cannot tolerate oral preparations, those who do not respond to oral iron, or for those with severe iron deficiency later in pregnancy. The response to therapy is first seen as an increase in the reticulocyte count peaking 7 to 10 days after starting iron therapy. The Hb level should increase by at least 1 g/dL within 2 to 3 weeks of starting therapy. Because of the plasma expansion associated with pregnancy, the Hct may not increase significantly but, rather, stabilizes or increases only slightly.

#### Macrocytic Anemia

Macrocytic anemias are much less common than iron deficiency anemia and are associated with RBC indices that indicate an increased MCV, typically greater than 100 fL. Folate deficiency is the most common cause of macrocytic anemia in pregnancy and is associated with diets lacking legumes, fresh leafy vegetables, or animal proteins. Folate supplementation before and during pregnancy has additional benefits beyond prevention of anemia. Adequate intake of folic acid (folate) has been found to reduce the risk of neural tube defects (NTDs) in the fetus. The first occurrence of NTDs may be reduced by as much as 36% if women of reproductive age consume 0.4 mg of folate daily both before conception and during the first trimester of pregnancy. The Recommended Dietary Allowance for folate for pregnant women is 0.6 mg. Folate deficiency is especially likely in multiple gestations or when patients are taking antiseizure medications. Women with a history of a prior NTD-affected pregnancy or who are being treated with antiseizure drugs may reduce the risk of NTDs by more than 80% with a daily intake of 4 mg of folate in the months in which conception is attempted and for the first trimester of pregnancy.
