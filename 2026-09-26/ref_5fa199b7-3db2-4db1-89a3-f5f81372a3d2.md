As in other medical conditions, screening for perinatal psychiatric disorders has caveats and must be responsibly implemented so that cases identified can be referred to treatment promptly. Careful planning for the choice of instrument, the setting, clinical cut-off scores, and the timing of administration must be appropriate to individual settings. Screening offers little hope for symptom improvement if referrals for treatment cannot be made. The assessment of perinatal disorders can be confounded by somatic complaints common to the postpartum period, such as sleep and eating disturbances, decreased sexual interest, weight gain, and fatigue. As a result, the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS), which de-emphasizes such symptoms, has become a common instrument utilized by obstetricians, family doctors, psychiatrists, and pediatricians. The ten-question scale is available in 70 languages, has been validated cross-culturally, and is easily completed by the patient or care provider in a few minutes. Sensitivity and specificity range from 70% to 88% and studies evaluating the accuracy of the EPDS demonstrate that it identifies ill individuals more effectively than a clinician’s assessment. EPDS includes a question about suicidal thoughts, which is especially important in this high-risk population. Moreover, a three-question mini-anxiety screener is embedded within the EPDS, making it possible to screen for both depressive and anxiety disorders at once. Antenatal presence of anxiety and depression is highly predictive of PPD and, therefore, important to consider in the decision-making process surrounding treatment during pregnancy.

#### Psychiatric Treatment Decision Making During Pregnancy and Postpartum

Structured Clinical Decision Making. Because of the temporal coincidence of mood disorders during women’s reproductive years, and because pregnancy is unplanned in 50% of cases, psychiatrists must routinely ask about contraception and family planning during pharmacologic management visits. The family planning period is ideal for a structured preconceptual counseling discussion regarding perinatal treatment with prospective parents. Decision making in the treatment of pregnant women should be structured to include patient education regarding the natural history of her psychiatric disorder, the identification and review of likely risks and benefits associated both with the disorder and available somatic treatments, and frank discussion of available treatment options. This decision-making process must be tailored to address the severity of the patient’s current illness and psychiatric history and the experiences, values, and concerns of the patient. Clinical decision making must balance the known and unknown risks of medication treatment with the known and unknown risks of untreated psychiatric illness.

Providers must engage patients in a comprehensive discussion of risks associated with treatment versus the considerable risks associated with an untreated mood disorder. Discussions supporting open communication with patients in a comprehensible format, respecting the patient’s concerns and value structure, are critical to optimizing treatment decisions and obtaining fully informed consent for treatment. Such conversations iteratively take place. Some women will desire a withdrawal of psychotropic medications under close supervision prior to pregnancy, with iterative decisions about medication resumption over time in the event of symptom recurrence or if there is a recommendation for postpartum prophylaxis. When pregnancy is discovered during a pharmacologic treatment course, an analysis of the risks associated with medication continuation and the risks associated with illness relapse must be carefully evaluated.

Fetal neurodevelopment is an active field of research. More recent neuroimaging studies demonstrate altered structure and function in infants of mothers with depression. Changes in connectivity between the amygdala and the prefrontal cortex, decreased hippocampal growth, less cortical thickening, and increased amygdala volume have been identified in women with prenatal depression.

The potential negative impact of psychiatric illness on maternal caregiving functions is well demonstrated. Mentally ill mothers have an increased likelihood of having intrusive or withdrawn interactional styles with their infants and may lack sensitivity and attunement to infant cues. These maternal behaviors have been associated with offspring vulnerability for poor self-regulation, low self-esteem, insecure attachments, behavioral problems, and motor and cognitive development delays. Ultimately, the complex multifactorial nature of the fetal environment, postbirth environment, and shared parental–infant genetic factors make unitary explanations of infant development overly simplistic. Yet, awareness of these distinct levels of infant developmental origins empowers newly pregnant women to optimize conditions for their offspring.

Nonsomatic Treatments for Perinatal Mood Disorders. Treatment options should be presented according to a hierarchy from least to most somatically invasive. Nonpharmacologic depression treatments, such as outpatient psychotherapy or mindfulness-based stress reduction, can be advantageous for mild to moderately depressed or anxious women by providing emotional support and instrumental skill development needed to facilitate coping through this life transition. Maintenance of ongoing clinic visits and psychiatric monitoring and optimizing general health and social behaviors are all important strategies that promote primary prevention or early intervention as needed, thus conferring the best prognosis and preventing the untoward consequences and refractory course that otherwise might develop.

Chronobiologic treatments were developed because depression results from a desynchronization of circadian rhythms. Treatment implications are to resynchronize the biologic rhythms of sleep and activity with those of other underlying circadian rhythms, such as melatonin, cortisol, thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), or prolactin. This end may be achieved by administering bright light (greater than 2,500 to 10,000 lx) or wake therapy (sleep deprivation) at critical times of the day. Although still in its experimental stages, the use of bright light or wake therapy in women with pregnancy or PPD has had promising results in pilot studies. Further studies in this area would be worthwhile, given the relatively short onset of action of these treatments and the avoidance of risks associated with pharmacologic interventions.

The benefits of social and material support that is not delivered in traditional clinics and promotes the organic routine of a mother caring for her children in her home cannot be understated for new mothers. Models for treatment delivery continue to change over the years. In the United Kingdom, Australia, and several European countries, home health care providers have had a major effect on improving recognition and management and reducing the adverse consequences of these disorders. In the United States and elsewhere internet-based intervention, health coaches by phone and support groups are increasing. For women with severe mental illness, mother-baby inpatient units (in Europe, Australia, and India) enable mothers to undergo the necessary treatment while breastfeeding and developing a relationship with their baby. In the United States, there are several mother–baby day hospitals and three Inpatient Perinatal Psychiatry Units, located at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill, Zucker Hillside Hospital in Queens, New York, and El Camino Health in Mountain View, California. However, none of these options allow the baby to stay with the mother.

<small>NONPHARMACOLOGIC TREATMENTS FOR POSTPARTUM DEPRESSION.</small>

Complementary and alternative medicine therapies suggest potential benefits as monotherapy or in conjunction with pharmacologic treatments. Psychosocial interventions for individuals and groups, delivered in-home, clinic, or by phone, compared to usual care, are associated with symptom reduction, reduced psychosocial stress, increased mental wellness, and prevention of PPD in an increasing number of studies. Promising modalities include brief interpersonal psychotherapy, CBT, mindfulness, computerized resources, in-home nurse visits, and peer-based telephone support. Light therapy and supplements may also provide relief. Five-week morning 7,000 lx bright light therapy was associated with a significant improvement in depressive symptoms in an RCT compared to red, dim light. A meta-analysis of 18 RCTs examining the use of omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) in treating perinatal depression found a small beneficial effect. The result was of statistical significance, but the response appeared to be so small and inconsistent that the authors advised against prescribing omega-3 PUFAs to prevent or treat mood episodes during pregnancy. However, it might be beneficial as an adjunct therapy in treating PPD. There is strong evidence for the effectiveness of cognitive-behavioral and interpersonal therapy in managing PPD. Research is too limited to draw conclusions surrounding the effectiveness of exercise, yoga, massage, acupuncture, light therapy, and supplements.

#### Postpartum Psychosis

The most severe perinatal mood disorder, PPP, is rare, affecting approximately 0.1% to 0.2% (1 to 2 in 1,000) of postpartum women. The disorder was recognized as early as 400 BC by Hippocrates. PPP typically presents within the first few weeks following delivery. Depersonalization, delirium, agitation and insomnia, and delusions are characteristic of the disorder. Affective symptoms—including depression, irritability, anxiety, mixed episodes, and, most often, mania—are also present, and are an important reminder that the syndrome is an affective psychosis. (A woman with an underlying primary psychotic illness who experiences an exacerbation in the postpartum is NOT the same as a woman with PPP, see below for nonaffective psychoses.) A clinical cohort study assessing the phenotype of PPP found abnormal thought content among 75% of cases, disorganization among 25%, disturbed consciousness in 10%, and catatonia in 5%. It is important to recognize that the clinical presentation of PPP can include waxing and waning of consciousness, similar to delirium; therefore, it is imperative to obtain collateral information about symptoms as a presentation at any moment may not reveal the full extent of symptoms.

Women with pre-existing bipolar disorder are at very high risk of PPP. In addition, women with new-onset PPP are eventually diagnosed with bipolar disorder in 50% to 80% of cases. PPP is a medical emergency because of the potential risk for infanticide or suicide, and therefore imminent referral to a psychiatrist, hospitalization, and initiation of lithium is typically indicated. PPP has been associated with a 70-fold increased risk of suicide in the first 12-month postpartum, with peak rates within a few days to a few weeks after discharge. Although being a mother is protective against suicide, this narrow time interval postpartum for very ill women constitutes an exception and warrants greater vigilance. Infanticide risk is also greatly increased in this patient population. In a classification of maternal filicides, 24% occurred within the context of acute maternal psychosis and another 56% due to altruistic beliefs about alleviating suffering in the infant, which was often accompanied by maternal suicide. If recognized early and treated aggressively, PPP can have a good prognosis. Lithium is the gold standard for managing the disorder, with 98% of patients in one longitudinal study responding to lithium treatment. Benzodiazepines and antipsychotics can be useful for symptomatic management but should not take the place of lithium.

#### Excretion of SSRIs in Breast Milk

SSRIs appear in breast milk to varying extents, and data from the literature differ in their findings regarding the relative dose being transmitted to the infant. Various reports about drug levels in milk are unreliable because of variations in milk collection procedures and because such medications tend to be lipophilic. Breast milk concentrations for some drugs may vary more than twofold between the watery fore milk and the fattier hind milk, whereas others may show little variation. Accordingly, clinical monitoring of maternal plasma or breast milk is not routinely indicated.

In some studies, concentrations are lowest for fluvoxamine and sertraline. Low levels of both sertraline and its metabolite are found in the sera of infants. The concentration of sertraline and desmethylsertraline in breast milk is highly variable and, on average, is of the same order of magnitude as their concentration in the blood plasma of the mother. As a result, more than half of breastfed babies receive less than 2 mg/day of sertraline and desmethylsertraline combined, and in most cases, these substances are undetectable in their blood. No changes occur in 5-HT uptake by the platelets of breastfed infants as measured by blood 5-HT levels before and after beginning sertraline treatment. Sertraline is more likely to be detected in an infant if the maternal daily dose is 100 mg or higher. Data on paroxetine are mixed, with some studies showing somewhat higher breast milk concentrations and others reporting paroxetine to have the lowest transfer into breast milk. According to a pooled analysis of available data, breastfed infants exposed to paroxetine or sertraline are least likely to demonstrate detectable or elevated plasma drug levels, whereas infants exposed to fluoxetine appear to be at higher risk of developing elevated levels. Most data show that the relative infant dose of citalopram is intermediate between those of paroxetine and fluoxetine. High doses of citalopram should also be used with caution. Escitalopram and its demethyl metabolite are present in breast milk, and unwanted effects in breastfed infant should be assessed.

If treatment with an SSRI is started during the postpartum period, fluoxetine should not be the first choice. Fluoxetine transfers to a greater extent, and its active metabolite, norfluoxetine, has a long half-life of 1 to 2 weeks and may accumulate in a breastfed infant.

Slow increases in the dose help manage side effects. Women who have recently given birth are often sensitive to medication side effects, possibly related to elevated anxiety, which is common early in the postpartum course. Treatment should be initiated at half of the recommended starting dose (e.g., 25 mg of sertraline per day or 10 mg of paroxetine per day) for 4 days, and small increments should increase doses (e.g., 25 mg of sertraline per week or 10 mg of paroxetine per week) as tolerated until full remission is achieved. If the patient responds to an initial trial of medication lasting 6 to 8 weeks, the same dose should be continued for a minimum of 6 months after a full remission has been achieved to prevent a relapse. If there is no improvement after 6 weeks of drug therapy or if the patient has a response but then has a relapse, a medication change (within-class or different class) is recommended. Drug doses may need to be increased in the third trimester during the period of increased hepatic metabolism and expansion of blood volume. Antidepressant trials are often initiated in primary care and obstetrician-gynecology offices, and consultation with psychiatrists can facilitate optimal dose titration to achieve remission, address treatment resistance, and treat psychiatric comorbidities. Long-term maintenance treatment to prevent recurrence should be considered for women who have had three or more episodes of severe depression.

<small>BREXANOLONE AND RELATED COMPOUNDS.</small>

The utility of compounds derived from progesterone in the treatment of PPD has gained momentum in recent years, culminating in FDA approval of a novel agent for the treatment of PPD, brexanolone, in March 2019. Brexanolone is structurally identical to allopregnanolone, an endogenous neuroactive steroid and a progesterone metabolite. Three randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled studies demonstrated significant improvement in depression following a 60-hour continuous infusion of brexanolone. While there is significant ongoing enthusiasm for brexanolone, and it is now available at sites across the country, it remains expensive and time prohibitive for many, costing $34,000 per infusion and requiring at least a 2.5-day inpatient admission, and many have noted the strong placebo response of the clinical trials. The current understanding of brexanolone’s mechanism of action is discussed in the Mechanisms section later in the text. A related oral compound, zuranolone, was granted FDA approval for the treatment of PPD in August 2023. A double-blind RCT including 151 women meeting criteria for PPD demonstrated significant improvement in depressive symptoms at day 3, with persistence to day 45, among those receiving zuranolone compared to the placebo group.

<small>SEROTONIN REUPTAKE INHIBITORS (SRIS = SSRIS AND SNRIS).</small>



All SRIs are generally considered compatible with pregnancy. Some studies, largely those with inappropriate control groups, have demonstrated a statistically significant, though arguably not clinically relevant, relationship between SRI use and congenital teratogenicity. There was significant concern about the association of paroxetine with cardiac septal defects, resulting in the FDA revising its label to category D in 2005. However, subsequent meta-analyses failed to reproduce the relationship, challenging the conclusion that paroxetine is a cardiac teratogen. More recently, several large, methodologically rigorous studies have demonstrated no significant relationship between cardiac malformations and SRI use in the first trimester. The possible link between late pregnancy (after 20 weeks of gestation) exposure to SRIs and persistent pulmonary hypertension of the newborn (PPHN) also created public alarm and FDA label changes warning of risk in 2006, which was then rescinded in 2011. An increasing number of studies and meta-analyses draw contrasting conclusions and do not adequately control for confounders such as maternal depression severity, preterm birth, and mode of delivery. Current information, therefore, does not support SRI discontinuation due to PPHN risk.

Spontaneous abortion rates were initially estimated to be higher among women taking antidepressants. Significant limitations of this early research include lack of control for psychiatric status; lack of control for a history of prior miscarriage; and the possibility that therapeutic abortions were misrepresented as miscarriages due to stigma. Several analyses in a large cohort found no increased risk attributable to SRIs when controlling for depression status. Additionally, SRI use was not associated with an increased risk of stillbirth, neonatal death, or postneonatal death in a second well-controlled, large study.

Antidepressant exposure in late pregnancy has been significantly associated with lower gestational age and lower APGAR scores at 1 and 5 minutes, even with proper controls for psychiatric status. SRI exposure was associated with lower infant birth weight only when maternal psychiatric status was unaccounted for. Importantly, these effects are quite low and of questionable clinical significance. A 1.2- to 2-fold increased risk of preterm delivery, defined as birth prior to 37 weeks of gestation, was associated with in utero SRI exposure in a meta-analysis that controlled for illness exposure; however other characteristics of women who maintain meds through pregnancy could confer residual confounding. Depression has also been associated with preterm birth, but additional research is needed.

A neonatal adaptation syndrome affects approximately 30% of late pregnancy, SRI-exposed newborns, and substantially more preterm infants are affected. Features of poor neonatal adaptation include respiratory difficulty, hypoglycemia, hypertonia, jitteriness, vomiting, seizures, and sleep disturbances. The syndrome appears to be time-limited, with the greatest incidence in the first 48 hours postbirth but potentially extending to 2 weeks postbirth. While one study provided evidence for similar rates of neonatal signs in the absence of SRI exposure 2 weeks prior to delivery, thus implicating maternal or other neonatal factors in this syndrome, another study found increased adaptation syndrome risk even after control for maternal characteristics. Long-term sequelae of this syndrome have not been adequately studied; one study found continued differences in response to visual and auditory stimuli in SRI-exposed babies compared to unexposed at 30 days postdelivery.

Concerns have been raised about the use of paroxetine during pregnancy and cardiovascular malformations in the newborn. In a study using Swedish national registry data, the risk of a cardiac defect was about 2% in infants exposed to paroxetine during pregnancy versus 1% among all registry infants. The results showed a trend toward increased risk with paroxetine (OR = 1.5; 95% CI 0.8–2.9). Most of the cardiac defects observed in these studies were atrial or ventricular septal defects, conditions in which the wall between the right and left sides of the heart is not completely developed. In general, septal defects are among the most common types of congenital malformations. They range from those that are symptomatic and may require surgery to those that are asymptomatic and may resolve on their own. It is generally recommended to avoid paroxetine during the first trimester of pregnancy or in women who plan to become pregnant in the near future.

Neonates exposed to SSRIs in the third trimester may develop poor neonatal adaptation syndrome (PNAS). This refers to short-term neurobehavioral effects in newborns. Symptoms such as poor muscle tone, trouble sleeping, shaking, weak or excessive crying, irritability, hypoglycemia, or seizures can be seen. It has been reported to affect up to 30% of exposed neonates and may represent discontinuation syndrome. The symptoms appear shortly after birth and may last up to two weeks. A meta-analysis found an increased risk of PNAS among neonates exposed to a medication for depression in utero (OR = 5.07; 95% CI, 3.25–7.90; P <.0001).

Another possible pregnancy-related side effect in offspring of SSRI-treated mothers is persistent pulmonary hypertension of the newborn (PPHN), a condition associated with substantial infant mortality and morbidity. A large cohort study examined the risk of PPHN associated with exposure to an SSRI in the 90 days before delivery versus no exposure. A total of 102,179 women had been exposed to SSRIs late in pregnancy. The authors found that 322 SSRI-exposed newborns (31.5; 95% CI, 28.3–35.2 per 10,000 births) were diagnosed with PPHN, compared to 7,630 newborns not exposed to medication for depression (20.8; 95% CI, 20.4–21.3 per 10,000 births). The unadjusted OR was 1.51 (95% CI, 1.35–1.69), and the OR became statistically nonsignificant when restricting to women diagnosed with depression and adjusting for confounders (OR = 1.10; 95% CI, 0.94–1.29). This data suggests that the risk of PPHN in newborns exposed to SSRIs late in pregnancy is low and may even be lower than previously thought.

<small>TRICYCLIC ANTIDEPRESSANTS.</small>

The data show no evidence of increased incidence of congenital malformations in fetuses exposed to TCAs, although evidence is less extensive than that for the SRIs, and most reported exposures have been to clomipramine. Increased likelihood of preterm birth and low birth weight at an OR of ∼1.5 mirrors the findings of SRI-exposed pregnancies, and like SRIs, there have also been reports of PPHN and cardiac septal defects related to TCA exposure. Secondary TCAs (i.e., nortriptyline, desipramine) may be preferred in pregnancy based upon fewer hypotensive and anticholinergic effects. A poor neonatal adaptation syndrome has been described with SRI exposure, and some evidence suggests it could be more severe and include anticholinergic symptoms of urinary retention and bowel obstruction. TCAs are not typically found in measurable amounts in nursing infants. Respiratory depression and sedation, the only adverse outcomes reported with any TCA, occurred in an infant whose mother took doxepin. Of the drugs in this class, nortriptyline has been studied the most as a treatment for breastfeeding women. Children exposed to TCAs both in utero and through breast milk have been followed through preschool and compared with children who were not exposed to these drugs, and no developmental problems were found. Due to increasing preferences for SRI treatment, fewer long-term studies are available following in utero TCA exposure.

<small>MONOAMINE OXIDASE INHIBITORS.</small>

The use of monoamine oxidase inhibitors (MAOIs) is less optimal than many other agents in pregnancy for several reasons. First, studies have found growth restriction in animals receiving doses more than the maximum human recommended dose. Second, pregnancy-induced hypertension is a common complication of pregnancy that often has an insidious onset; further exacerbation of that condition using MAOIs may lead to placental hypoperfusion and have serious fetal consequences. Third, if premature labor occurs, tocolysis with β-mimetics may be obstetrically indicated but not possible because of potential interaction with the MAOI. Fourth, anesthetic management in labor may be complicated by the relative contraindication of opioids in patients taking MAOIs. If pregnancy occurs while the patient takes MAOIs, a gradual cross-taper to a more evidence-based drug is the preferred strategy. Guidelines have been suggested for appropriate obstetrical anesthesia and analgesia for women treated with MAOIs.

<small>BUPROPION.</small>

Bupropion’s dopaminergic mechanism of action and role in smoking cessation gives it an important place in the treatment armamentarium, and it is preferred by many reproductive-aged women concerned about SRI-associated weight gain. Data on bupropion are growing but still scant compared to those available for SRIs. Some small studies raised concerns about an increased risk of ventricular septal defects and left outflow tract heart defects among exposed infants, but numerous recent studies have not supported these concerns. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis including more than 5 million pregnancies reported an odds ratio of 1.23 (95% CI 1.01 to 1.49) for the use of bupropion during the first trimester and occurrence of cardiac malformations in the offspring, a statistically significant but likely not clinically significant association. The challenge of adequately powering studies to look at rare heart defects remains, and the studies to date have not consistently controlled for important confounding effects of maternal illness. A recent review on the management of ADHD during pregnancy recommends bupropion as the preferred therapy and notes that the drug appears to be compatible with pregnancy. No associations have been found between bupropion use and late gestation effects, neonatal withdrawal/toxicity, or developmental (neurobehavioral) teratogenicity, but data are limited at this point. A few small studies have assessed the degree to which bupropion passes to breastmilk. One study, conducted on two breastfed infants of mothers currently taking bupropion, was unable to detect bupropion, and its active metabolite, hydroxybupropion, in the blood of either infant. Infant exposure has been measured at 0.14% of the standard adult dose of bupropion and is therefore very small.
