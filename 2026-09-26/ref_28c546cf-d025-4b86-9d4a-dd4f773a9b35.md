The high N and Low E combination represents the “Gloomy Pessimist” aspect of the Style of Well-Being. Such individuals, because of their high distress proneness and low positive affect or energy, face a dark and dreary life with few things that cheer them and many that cause anguish and distress. Stressful circumstances often lead them to feeling depressed. Even under normal circumstances, they often find life hard and joyless. These are individuals who feel no positive affect and believe that life holds little value or happiness for them. Such individuals may experience a number of existential crises, including a diminished sense of personal worth and value, a lack of personal meaning, and a belief that the future holds no solutions or opportunities for improvement. Further compounding their situation is the lack of a useful social support network to provide emotional care. These individuals are frequently loners who are detached from their environments and are prone to experience deep loneliness. These individuals should not be confused with schizoid personality disorder; such individuals may be aloof and detached from social interactions, but that is their preferred style. The high N and low E person are isolated and detached not because of preference, but rather because they are overwhelmed with feelings of inadequacy and emotional distress and are fearful of social criticism. They may also lack the social tools and skills necessary to effect any positive coping with their situation.

The high N and Low O combination defines the “Maladaptive” type of defense style. These individuals tend to use primitive and ineffective defenses such as repression, denial, and reaction formation. They prefer not to think about disturbing ideas, and they may refuse to acknowledge possible dangers (such as a serious illness). They lack insight into the distressing affects they experience, and because they cannot verbalize their feelings, they may be considered alexithymic. The low O may also create a more authoritarian orientation toward life, believing that there are clear rules and guidelines that define and determine appropriate intra- and interpersonal processes. Securely settled within their own acquired belief structures, such individuals may be unable to adjust or adapt their behaviors in ways that facilitate their engagement with others. Thus, they may easily put off others and develop feelings of alienation and isolation.

Style of anger control considers levels of N and Agreeableness (A). While Extraversion examines our interest in being with others and our level of engagement with them, A relates to the quality of our interactions with others, ranging from a very compassionate, caring, and altruistic orientation to a more antagonistic, selfish, and manipulative style. The presence of high N serves to attenuate the communal, bonding aspects of the highly agreeable person while exacerbating the hostile, confrontational aspects of the antagonistic individual. The style involving high levels of N with low levels of A is termed “Temperamental.” These individuals are easily angered and tend to express anger directly. They may fly into a rage over a minor irritant, and they can seethe with anger for long periods of time. They are deeply involved in themselves and take offense readily, and they often overlook the effects of their anger on others. They may be prone to physical aggression and verbal abuse. Because these individuals may possess feelings of personal inadequacy and have a tenuous hold on their life tasks, they are keenly sensitive to any criticism or negative evaluation of their behavior, seeing them as threats that may expose them as impostors or worse. They respond quickly and harshly to any accusations of personal worth and are willing to physically strike out against any external agency.

The fourth and final maladaptive style relates to the role of Conscientiousness in moderating the expression of negative affect influencing self-regulation, which is the impulse control style. High levels of C are associated with good self-control, including active processes of planning, organizing, and carrying out tasks. Low levels of C are related to a more “here and now” orientation, a desire to take the path of least resistance, and a goal to maximize hedonic pleasure. For those who are low on C, their manner of impulse control is labeled “Undercontrolled.” These individuals are often at the mercy of their own impulses. They find it difficult and distressing to resist any urge or desire, and they lack the self-control to hold their urges in check. As a result, they may act in ways that they know are not in their long-term best interests. They may be particularly susceptible to substance abuse and other health risk behaviors. These individuals are very hedonistically oriented, preferring immediate pleasure to long-range success. These individuals frequently end up disappointing others in their lives because of their inability to live up to their commitments and obligations. Further complicating their relationships with others is underlying selfishness to their strivings. Others are valued and useful primarily to the extent that they can provide for whatever physical and/or emotional needs they may have. Undercontrolled style people are not necessarily manipulative in their manner of relating to others so much as they are interested in fulfilling their personal algorithm of obtaining maximum benefit for minimum effort or investment.

Personality Traits and Personality Disorders. DSM-5 provides a general definition of a personality disorder that applies to all 10 of the specific disorders: “A personality disorder is an enduring pattern of inner experience and behavior that deviates markedly from the expectations of the individual’s culture, is pervasive and inflexible, has an onset in adolescence and early adulthood, is stable in time, and leads to distress or impairment.” (p. 645)

It is reasonable to ask whether these inflexible and maladaptive traits are the same as or different from those encountered in nonpsychiatric populations. Several recent studies on this question have concurred in finding strong and replicable links between scales measuring personality disorders and the five factors in normal and clinical populations.

The pathologic childhood triad of bed-wetting past the age of 6 years, setting fires, and torturing animals has been associated with subsequent violent behavior in adults. Interpersonally, studies show that violence-prone individuals require more personal space around their physical person than do others. Violent individuals feel threatened when approached too closely, particularly from the rear.

Psychological and social contributions are also strong. Violence in families breeds violence, and battered children often grow up to be battering adults. Cultural norms for the expression of violence differ considerably. In some socioeconomic and ethnic groups, violent gangs organize the energies of many adolescent youth. For some, violent behavior is an adolescent socialization pattern necessary to prove one’s manhood or womanhood. Like other social organizations, violent gangs have detailed rules that inhibit and govern the expression of violence. Some unpredictable and unsocialized violent people are too violent to be contained even in gangs.

Aggressive and violent behavior is diagnostically nonspecific. Violence in schizophrenia may occur as a consequence of paranoid delusions, in response to command auditory hallucinations, or secondary to passivity experiences. Manic patients and those in mixed states may be violent, often in response to minimal provocation. Violent behavior commonly occurs in patients with antisocial and borderline personalities (in the latter often self-directed as well as other-directed). Violent behavior may occur in epilepsy, although rarely during true ictal periods; in frontal lobe syndromes as a “release phenomenon;” and in association with abused substances, particularly disinhibiting sedatives, such as alcohol, or stimulants, such as amphetamines and cocaine, which increase irritability, aggressiveness, and paranoia.

Impulsive violence may be provoked by a number of stimuli and situations. Alcohol is perhaps the most common disinhibitor of violence. Intrafamilial violence, the most common setting for homicide, is frequently related to alcohol intoxication. In episodic dyscontrol and intermittent explosive disorder, violent behaviors typically erupt after a person has ingested alcohol, a phenomenon known as pathologic intoxication. In these often ferocious outbursts, the individual may confront or provoke any potential target for violence, including total strangers and police, but significant others, spouses, and parents are frequent victims. Patients with episodic dyscontrol commonly have histories of violent sexual behavior, including rape and, often while intoxicated, speeding and reckless driving, sometimes chasing down, stopping, and attacking other motorists who they believe “get in their way.”
