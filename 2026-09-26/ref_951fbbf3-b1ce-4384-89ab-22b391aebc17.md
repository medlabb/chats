In addition to the neuroendocrine factors described above, medications such as antidepressants, antiandrogens, and oral contraceptives can contribute to female sexual dysfunction. The most common psychological factors affecting female sexual function are depression, anxiety, distraction, negative body image, and trauma. In medically healthy women, problems with sexual arousal are often the result of insufficient sexual stimulation rather than organic causes. Vascular disease, associated with diabetes mellitus (DM) and atherosclerosis, as well as many of its treatments, can cause arousal problems in women by inhibiting smooth muscle relaxation and vasocongestion, necessary for the engorgement of the clitoris and vagina, and resulting lubrication. There is conflicting data, however, regarding the relationship between metabolic syndrome and HSDD.

In general terms, ED can be classified as psychogenic, organic, or mixed. Psychogenic factors such as performance anxiety and others mentioned previously can negatively impact erectile function. This type of ED tends to occur on a situational basis, has a sudden onset, and concurrently, men can experience normal erections with masturbation, at night or upon awakening. Organic processes can be vascular (e.g., arterial insufficiency, venous leakage), neurologic, endocrine (e.g., hypogonadism, hyperprolactinemia), substance induced, or due to a systemic illness. In these cases, ED tends to be more insidious in its onset, and erections are deficient in most contexts (masturbation, nocturnal).

Healthy vascular and neurologic functions are necessary for an erection to occur. There is a neurologically mediated increase in penile arterial inflow. Nitric oxide is released from the endothelium and parasympathetic nerve terminals, allowing relaxation of the cavernosal smooth muscle, vasocongestion, and engorgement of penile tissue, resulting in an erection. A vasculogenic etiology is the most common among organic etiologies of ED. Current evidence suggests a strong correlation between cardiovascular disease and ED, with a common pathophysiologic basis of endothelial dysfunction, inflammation, and low testosterone. ED is also associated with coronary heart disease, stroke, and all-cause mortality. The presence of vasculogenic ED in young men can be a particularly early indicator of future cardiovascular disease, and these patients should receive an appropriate cardiovascular workup for risk stratification.

Risk factors for ED can be examined in accordance with etiology. Some neurologic disorders (e.g., multiple sclerosis, stroke, temporal lobe epilepsy, Parkinson disease, Alzheimer disease, spinal cord injury) are associated with ED, and the risk of nerve injury leading to ED is very high in patients undergoing radical pelvic surgery. Endocrine risk factors include hypogonadism, hyperprolactinemia, and hypothyroidism. Some risk factors for vasculogenic ED are atherosclerosis, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, obesity, smoking, DM, and pelvic irradiation. Among men with DM, 50% to 75% develop ED; the prevalence of ED in diabetics is three times that seen in nondiabetics. Lower urinary tract symptoms have also been linked to the development of ED. The most common medications that can cause or contribute to ED are antiandrogens, antihypertensives (thiazide diuretics, beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers), antiarrhythmics (e.g., digoxin), and many psychotropic drugs (tricyclic antidepressants, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors [SSRIs], phenothiazines, and butyrophenones).

In a patient with chronic renal disease, sexual function may be diminished due to fatigue and malaise. Chronic renal disease may also lead to sexual dysfunction due to associated vascular disease, neuropathies, and endocrine abnormalities. For example, both free and total testosterone levels are lowered in chronic renal disease but may return to normal following transplantation. In patients with chronic renal disease, sexual dysfunction may also be related to abnormalities between the pituitary and the Leydig axis. Both vitamin E and erythropoietin have been reported to improve sexual function in men with renal failure.

Studies have found that cardiovascular disease, including myocardial infarction (MI), may physically and psychologically interfere with sexual functioning and negatively impact all three phases of the sexual response cycle in older women as well as older men. Following an MI, many patients have concerns about intercourse that include anxiety about the inability to perform or fear that one may die during coitus. Angina may contribute to fears of having an MI during sex and reduce libido. In addition, the anxiety and depressive disorders that commonly occur after an MI may themselves cause sexual dysfunction.

Sexual intercourse places roughly the same demands on the heart as a vigorous walk around a city block. There is great intersubject variability in blood pressure and heart rate increases during sexual intercourse. Generally, an individual who can walk vigorously or pedal 600 kpm per minute on a stationary bicycle without symptoms, increased blood pressure, or electrocardiogram changes can safely engage in sexual intimacy, including coitus. Sexual abstinence is recommended for 8 to 12 weeks after MI, although there is minimal evidence to justify this in uncomplicated patients. After this period of abstinence, gradual resumption of sexual activity is recommended, often beginning with sexual experiences other than sexual intercourse. Because superior positions may heighten cardiac demand, side-by-side or patient-inferior positions may be recommended when coitus is initially resumed. In selected patients, the use of beta blockers and sublingual nitrates prior to intercourse may reduce angina. Patients who benefit from the use of nitrates must be instructed not to use phosphodiesterase inhibitors.

Chronic pulmonary disease impacts sexual function in at least two ways: chronic arterial hypoxia is believed to cause testosterone reductions and associated diminished desire in both men and women, and shortness of breath may limit physical ability to engage in sexual activity.

Both insulin-dependent (Type I) and noninsulin-dependent (Type II) diabetes mellitus (DM) may contribute to sexual dysfunction in men and in women. ED has been reported in up to 50% of men with Type I DM and in up to 33% of men with Type II DM. As men living with DM age, the rate of ED may approach 70%, and comorbid smoking and alcohol abuse predict earlier onset of ED. Men living with DM are believed to develop ED secondary to neurologic or microvascular lesions, and, as a result, the presence of any of the “opathies” will be associated with ED. Sexual dysfunction in women with DM is often overlooked but may occur at a rate as high as 50%. Dyspareunia secondary to decreased vaginal lubrication and difficulty achieving orgasm are among the problems reported by women living with DM.

Acquired male erectile disorder has been reported in 10% to 20% of all men. Freud declared it common among his patients. Erectile disorder is the chief complaint of more than 50% of all men treated for sexual disorders. Lifelong male erectile disorder is rare; it occurs in about 1% of men under age 35. The incidence of erectile disorder increases with age. It has been reported variously as 2% to 8% of the young adult population. Alfred Kinsey reported that 75% of all men were impotent at age 80. There is a reported incidence of 40% to 50% in men between ages 60 and 70. All men over 40, Masters and Johnson claimed, have a fear of impotence, which the researchers believed reflected the masculine fear of loss of virility with advancing age. Male erectile disorder, however, is not universal in aging men; having an available sex partner is related to continuing potency, as is a history of consistent sexual activity and the absence of vascular, neurologic, or endocrine disease. Twenty percent of men fear erectile dysfunction prior to their first coitus; the reported incidence of actual erectile dysfunction during first coitus is 8%. As Stephen Levine has stated, the first coital encounter “is a horse race between excitement and anxiety.”

Male erectile disorder can be organic or psychological or a combination of both, but in young and middle-aged men, the cause is usually psychological. A good history is of primary importance in determining the cause of the dysfunction. If a man reports having spontaneous erections at times when he does not plan to have intercourse, having morning erections, or having good erections with masturbation or with partners other than his usual one, the organic causes of his erectile disorder can be considered negligible, and costly diagnostic procedures can be avoided. Male erectile disorder caused by a general medical condition or a pharmacologic substance is discussed later in this section.

Freud ascribed one type of erectile disorder to an inability to reconcile feelings of affection toward a woman with feelings of desire for her. Men with such conflicting feelings can function only with women whom they see as degraded (*Madonna–Putana complex*). Other factors that have been cited as contributing to impotence include a punitive superego, an inability to trust, and feelings of inadequacy or a sense of being undesirable as a partner. A man may be unable to express a sexual impulse because of fear, anxiety, anger, or moral prohibition. In an ongoing relationship, the disorder may reflect difficulties between the partners, particularly when a man cannot communicate his needs or his anger in a direct and constructive way. In addition, episodes of erectile disorder are reinforcing, with the man becoming increasingly anxious before each sexual encounter.

The mechanisms involved in the various diseases that lead to erectile disorder include general endothelial dysfunction or dysfunction of the penile endothelium specifically, atherosclerosis, dysregulation of nitric oxide synthase, a decrease of nitric oxide, low testosterone levels due to hyperprolactinemia-influenced changes in the hypothalamic–pituitary axis, vasculopathy, autonomic neuropathy, and disruption of neural proerectile processes.

<small>PHYSIOLOGIC TESTS.</small>

A number of procedures, benign and invasive, are used to help differentiate psychogenic erectile dysfunction. The procedures include monitoring nocturnal penile tumescence (erection that occurs during sleep) normally associated with rapid eye movement; monitoring tumescence with a strain gauge; measuring blood pressure in the penis with a penile plethysmograph or an ultrasound (Doppler) flow meter, both of which assess blood flow in the internal pudendal artery; and measuring pudendal nerve latency time. Neurologic impairment of penile function may be indicated by a decreased vibratory perception in the penis. Other diagnostic tests that delineate organic bases for erectile disorder include glucose tolerance tests, plasma hormone assays, liver and thyroid function tests, prolactin and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) determinations, and cystometric examinations. On physical examination, evidence of hypogonadism such as small testes, gynecomastia, reduced or absent body, and facial hair warrant attention. Invasive diagnostic studies include penile arteriography, infusion cavernosonography, and radioactive xenon penography. Invasive procedures require expert interpretation and are used only for patients who are candidates for vascular reconstructive procedures.

<small>MEDICAL VERSUS PSYCHOGENIC CAUSES.</small>

A good history is crucial to determining the cause of the male erectile disorder. If a man reports having spontaneous erections at times when he does not plan to have intercourse, having morning erections or only sporadic erectile dysfunction, or having good erections with masturbation or with partners other than his usual one, then organic causes for his disorder can be considered negligible, and costly diagnostic procedures can be avoided. When a medical basis for erectile dysfunction is found, psychological factors often contribute to the dysfunction, and psychiatric treatment may be helpful. Some diabetics, for instance, may experience psychogenic erectile dysfunction.

#### Sexual Dysfunction Due to a General Medical Condition

The category of sexual dysfunction due to a general medical condition covers sexual dysfunction that results in marked distress and interpersonal difficulty when there is evidence from the history, physical examination, or laboratory findings of a general medical condition judged to be causally related to the sexual dysfunction.

Male Erectile Disorder Due to a General Medical Condition. Many studies have focused on the relative incidences of psychological and organic male erectile disorder. Statistics indicate that 20% to 50% of men with erectile disorder have an organic basis for the disorder. A physiologic etiology is more likely in men older than 50 and the most likely cause in men older than age 60. Physiologically, erectile dysfunction may be due to a variety of medical causes ([Table 21.1–8](#tt21-1-8)). In the United States, it is estimated that two million men cannot gain erections because they suffer from diabetes mellitus; an additional 300,000 are dysfunctional because of other endocrine diseases; approximately 1.5 million are dysfunctional as a result of vascular disease; an estimated 180,000 because of multiple sclerosis; approximately 400,000 because of traumas and fractures leading to pelvic fractures or spinal cord injuries; and another 650,000 are estimated to be dysfunctional as a result of radical surgery, including prostatectomies, colostomies, and cystectomies. In addition, the clinician should be aware of the possible pharmacologic effects of medication on sexual functioning. The increased incidence of organic causes for erectile dysfunction may partly reflect the increased use of psychotropic and antihypertensive medications. Adverse effects of medication may impair male sexual functioning in a variety of ways. Castration (removal of the testes) does not always lead to sexual dysfunction, depending on the person. Erection may still occur after castration via a reflex arc that passes through the sacral cord erectile center. It is triggered when the inner thigh is stimulated.
