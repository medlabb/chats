Psychotropic Medications

<small>ANTIPSYCHOTICS.</small>

A small number (up to 15%) of the interictal psychosis resolves without any treatment, but a majority of patients require antipsychotics. There are no distinct differences in efficacies between second- antipsychotics and first-generation antipsychotics. Therefore, a second consideration is the seizure threshold lowering effect of psychotropic medications ([Table 2.4–9](#tt2-4-9)). This is usually not a problem but can occasionally reach clinical significance in poorly controlled epilepsy. Haloperidol, risperidone, paliperidone, and aripiprazole may be the least seizure threshold lowering agents in contrast to clozapine, olanzapine, quetiapine, chlorpromazine, thioridazine, and perphenazine. Quetiapine has clinically significant interaction with enzyme-inducing drugs, even for dosages below 700 mg/day. These psychotropic drugs are most convulsive with the rapid introduction of the drug and in high doses. Clozapine, for example, had induced seizures in 1% to 4.4% of patients, particularly when the dose was rapidly increased. In general, mild-to-moderate psychotic episode patients usually require 300 to 1,000 mg/day of chlorpromazine equivalent (CE) dose ([Table 2.4–10](#tt2-4-10)). Although patients with a severe psychotic episode, for example, unshakable delusion with severe aggression and suicidal ideation, may require dosages higher than 1,000 mg/day of CE, it is still the best to initiate with a low dose and titrate up slowly while monitoring antiepileptic levels and EEGs.

Therefore, the use of antipsychotic medications for the treatment of psychosis and agitation must be carefully considered after nonpharmacologic measures have been implemented, weighing the evidence for the potential benefit for target symptoms, against the risks of the medications themselves and the risks of alternative approaches to maintaining safety.

After 4 weeks of treatment with antipsychotic medications, if there is no clinical improvement, then the medication should be tapered and withdrawn. If an antipsychotic trial is beneficial, there should be a discussion with the patient and surrogate decision-maker about the benefits of the medication, potential harm, and previous attempts at a taper, with consideration being given to the recommendation to taper and withdraw the medication within four months of initiation. The patient should then be assessed at least monthly for the reemergence of symptoms for at least 4 months after discontinuation. Long-acting antipsychotic injectable medications should not be utilized unless it is also indicated for a co-occurring chronic psychotic disorder.

*Benzodiazepines.* Benzodiazepines have been compared to placebo as well as to antipsychotics for the treatment of behavioral disturbances. In general, they are more effective than placebo but less effective than antipsychotics in controlling behavior symptoms. However, they may be associated with paradoxical effects, disinhibition, worsening cognition, and falls. Cumulative toxicity tends to be greatest with long half-life agents, which should be avoided. At present, the use of benzodiazepines is largely limited to short-term control of acute agitated behavior with short-acting agents such as lorazepam (Ativan) or oxazepam (Serax).

*Anticonvulsants.* Data on the use of carbamazepine (Tegretol) is favorable. It has been shown to have modest benefits for agitation in short-term trials; however, its risk of side effects and drug-drug interactions are limiting. Oxcarbazepine and valproate (Depakene) have not been shown to be efficacious in randomized controlled trials. A 2019 review of 15 case series and reports suggest a possible benefit of gabapentin and pregabalin for the treatment of behavioral symptoms, but no randomized controlled trials have been performed. The newer anticonvulsants have not been studied for behavioral disturbances in dementia.

<small>ANXIETY AND MOOD DISTURBANCE</small>

*Antidepressants.* Special considerations in the use of antidepressant medication in ID include the common medical comorbidities as noted previously. Tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs) in particular (e.g., clomipramine) must be used with the knowledge that the risk of lowering seizure threshold is relevant. Although this risk in the general population is in the order of 1 in 1,000, in individuals with ID this risk may increase to closer to 1 in 5. Cardiac anomalies are common in some ID syndromes, and the anticholinergic side effects of some medications may be particularly significant in persons with DS. Individuals with ID may require lower levels of antidepressant drugs than their normally developing peers, and disinhibition has been described even with typical antidepressant doses of the serotonin reuptake inhibitors. Constipation associated with the anticholinergic effects of some TCAs can cause significant distress which, in people with ID, may be expressed as sleep disorders, loss of appetite, agitation, and aggression.

There are some individuals—particularly a subgroup within autism spectrum disorder—who appear to exhibit extraordinary sensitivity to drugs of this and other classes. This sensitivity, when it occurs, is manifest by disinhibition, and may present as an increase in target symptoms at doses of medication that might otherwise appear quite standard. In this context, individuals may be converted to responders with significant dose reductions, for example, reducing a starting dose of fluoxetine or citalopram from 10 to 2 mg, daily. Moreover, it is increasingly the norm that initiation doses for SSRIs in this population are lower, and the rate of dose increase is slower, than for the general population.

*Mood Stabilizers.* Some anticonvulsant drugs may improve cyclical mood disorders and impulsive aggression. Valproate has probably been studied most in this regard. Similarly, there is evidence to suggest that in the setting of cyclical mood disturbance, lithium may also be helpful.

*Anxiolytics.* Although benzodiazepines are commonly prescribed in treatment for anxiety in the general population, there are unique concerns when they are used in the context of developmental disorders, particularly regarding the possibility of increased confusion, cognitive impairment, unsteadiness, and paradoxical excitement. Barron and Sandman reviewed disinhibition associated with benzodiazepines that occurred in 35% to 68% of an institutionalized intellectually disabled population as compared to controls. Nevertheless, alprazolam, clonazepam, and lorazepam are widely used for the treatment of acute anxiety, particularly that associated with procedures. Their use should be considered in the absence of evidence of previous abnormal responses to these agents.

Delirium. Delirium may stem from physical disease or medication factors and is characterized by an acute change and fluctuations in mental status with inattention, altered awareness, and cognitive deficits. The management of delirium should always seek to identify and address the underlying cause and should begin with nonpharmacologic interventions, such as treating pain or supporting sleep. Anticholinergics, benzodiazepines, and opioid analgesics may precipitate or exacerbate delirium, and thus the use of these agents should be minimized or avoided altogether. If additional medication is required, second-generation neuroleptics such as risperidone, olanzapine, and quetiapine are considered first-line agents. Haloperidol is considered a second-line agent, and if neuroleptics are to be avoided, clonidine may be used. The use of physical restraints should be avoided in medically compromised youth, and risk for falls and medical complications should be monitored in any delirium cases but particularly after psychotropic medication has been administered.

Substance Intoxication or Withdrawal. The approach to agitation in a youth who is intoxicated or in withdrawal should ideally be tailored to the specific substance at hand. However, when intoxication is suspected and the offending substance is unknown, lorazepam is generally preferred for the management of acute agitation. If the patient is suffering from severe agitation or hallucinations, lorazepam may be given together with haloperidol. However, neuroleptics should be used with caution, as they may exacerbate anticholinergic or sympathomimetic toxidromes. Finally, the use of medication is preferable to physical restraints in intoxicated patients, as physical restraints may lead to rhabdomyolysis, a sudden increase in serum potassium, and cardiac arrest.
