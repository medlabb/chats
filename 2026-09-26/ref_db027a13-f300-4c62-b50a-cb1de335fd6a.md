<small>SECOND-GENERATION ANTIPSYCHOTICS.</small>

Our understanding of the safety profile of SGA use in pregnancy has made significant strides in recent years. A recent report on data belonging to the National Pregnancy Registry for Atypical Antipsychotics confirms that SGAs are unlikely to have a major teratogenic effect, with an odds ratio comparing exposed to unexposed infants of 1.48 (95% CI, 0.625 to 3.517). A review including data on 14,000 women utilizing SGAs during pregnancy found no association between congenital malformations and the use of either aripiprazole, olanzapine, or quetiapine. However, there was a statistically significant, though of questionable clinical significance, increased risk for malformations among users of risperidone and paliperidone. Authors could not comment on the safety of ziprasidone, clozapine, amisulpride, asenapine, lurasidone, and sertindole use during pregnancy due to limited data. Another study of over 1 million Finnish women, with data collected from 1996 to 2016, demonstrated an increased risk of pregnancy complications related to glucose metabolism, including gestational diabetes, Cesarean section, large-for-gestational-age infant, and preterm birth, among women taking either an FGA or SGA. However, controls were pregnant women without any history of antipsychotic use during pregnancy. Of note, maternal mental illness was not assessed nor controlled for and therefore a likely confounder in the study. A systematic review published in 2017 supports continuing women on the minimum effective dose of SGA throughout pregnancy, recognizing that discontinuation during pregnancy results in a high risk of relapse of mental illness, with weighty repercussions for both mother and offspring.

SGAs confer less risk for extrapyramidal side effects in the mother than FGAs, and the same is likely true for neonates exposed in utero. In a study of 142 pregnancies with exposure to SGAs, the antipsychotic dose was inversely related to gestational age at birth and positively related to the likelihood of NICU admission. Higher rates of respiratory distress were reported when SGAs were combined with mood stabilizers. Intake of SGA through milk leads to 0.1% to 10% maternal dose, and most are considered compatible with lactation. (There are theoretical concerns about the effect of aripiprazole on milk production, however, and this should be considered for women who are having difficulty establishing breastfeeding.) The National Pregnancy Registry for Atypical Antipsychotics continues to accept eligible subjects to further our understanding of the benefits and risks of SGA use during pregnancy ([http://womensmentalhealth.org/clinical-and-research-programs/pregnancyregistry/](http://womensmentalhealth.org/clinical-and-research-programs/pregnancyregistry/)).

While the above demonstrates that most FGAs and SGAs are compatible with both pregnancy and breastfeeding, there is one that should be used only with caution. Clozapine has the rare but serious possible side effect of inducing fatal agranulocytosis in adults, and therefore particular caution should be demonstrated with use in pregnancy and lactation.
