#### Chemistry

Benzodiazepines contain two benzene rings and a seven-member diazepine ring. Most benzodiazepines in current clinical use contain a 5-aryl substituent ring and a 1,4-diazepine ring and therefore are 5-aryl-1,4-benzodiazepines ([Fig. 33.9b–3](#ff33-9b-3)). A fused triazolo ring at positions RI and R2 on the diazepine ring in triazolobenzodiazepines such as alprazolam, triazolam, and estazolam, increases potency; the same is true of a fused imidazole ring at these positions in midazolam. Replacement of ring C with a keto substituent in position 5 and a methyl substituent in position 4 in flumazenil confers benzodiazepine antagonist properties. Clobazam, the only 1-5-benzodiazepine, is approved for adjunctive treatment of seizures associated with Lennox-Gastaut syndrome. #### Pharmacologic Actions

Absorption. All benzodiazepines except clorazepate are completely absorbed after oral administration and reach peak serum levels within 30 minutes to 2 hours. Metabolism of clorazepate in the stomach converts it to desmethyldiazepam, which is then completely absorbed. Intramuscular (IM) absorption of benzodiazepines other than lorazepam (Ativan) is slower than oral absorption. The onset of action is nearly immediate with intravenous administration of high-potency benzodiazepines such as midazolam.

Distribution. All benzodiazepines are lipid-soluble, but the degree of lipid solubility varies considerably between drugs. Benzodiazepines and their active metabolites are 70% to 99% protein bound, the extent of the binding being proportional to their lipid solubility. Benzodiazepines follow a two-compartment distribution, which involves a rapid central compartment phase followed by a redistribution phase to adipose tissue that ultimately determines the duration of action. As a result, a component of the long half-life of some highly lipophilic benzodiazepines such as diazepam is attributable to extensive storage in adipose tissue. At the same time, a single dose of a very lipophilic benzodiazepine (e.g., midazolam) redistributes rapidly to adipose tissue from CNS receptors, leading to a short duration of action while less lipophilic benzodiazepines like lorazepam remain at therapeutic concentrations at GABA<sub>A</sub> receptors longer because they do not redistribute as quickly. Reminazolam, a carboxylate benzodiazepine derivative with a high affinity for benzodiazepine receptors in the cerebral cortex, limbic system, midbrain and spinal cord, was developed as an alternative to midazolam for conscious sedation because it has a longer-acting metabolite that may reduce the risk of withdrawal and rebound seen with midazolam. This medication has not yet received regulatory approval. Because of high lipid solubility, high potency, and a short elimination half-life, alprazolam has a rapid onset and offset of action, with frequent interdose rebound and withdrawal that leads to increasing the dose which only intensifies symptoms when brain levels of the next dose fall.

### EFFECTS ON SPECIFIC ORGANS AND SYSTEMS

The primary action of benzodiazepines is on the CNS, where they have anticonvulsant, anxiolytic, muscle relaxant, and CNS depressant effects. Hyperpolarization of brainstem neurons at high benzodiazepine doses can cause respiratory depression. Diazepam increases coronary blood flow and has negative inotropic effects. Benzodiazepines used as preanesthetics reduce peripheral vascular resistance, thereby decreasing blood pressure and increasing heart rate. As discussed below, benzodiazepines cross the placenta and enter breast milk.

Benzodiazepines such as diazepam bind to PBRs. The therapeutic potential of these interactions has not been extensively explored, but it is possible that stabilizing the mitochondrial ion channel could reduce neurotoxicity in some degenerative disorders.

Metabolism. Benzodiazepine pharmacokinetics vary. Oxazepam and lorazepam are conjugated directly by glucuronidation to a water-soluble form and are excreted without any intermediate metabolites. Most other benzodiazepines are oxidized by cytochrome P450 (CYP) 3A4 and 2C19, frequently to active metabolites. These metabolites may then be hydroxylated to another active metabolite ([Fig. 33.9b–4](#ff33-9b-4)). For example, diazepam is oxidized to desmethyldiazepam, which, in tum, is hydroxylated to oxazepam. These products undergo glucuronidation to water-soluble inactive metabolites that can be excreted in the urine. A number of benzodiazepines (e.g., diazepam, chlordiazepoxide, clorazepate) have the same active metabolite (desmethyldiazepam), which has an elimination half-life of more than 120 hours. Flurazepam, a lipid-soluble benzodiazepine used as a hypnotic that has a short elimination half-life, has an active metabolite (desalkylflurazepam) with a half-life greater than 100 hours. The hypnotic drug quazepam is selective for the benzodiazepine-1 receptor, but it is metabolized to desalkylflurazepam, a nonselective benzodiazepine metabolite of flurazepam that accounts for 40% of total drug activity. With chronic dosing, this medication, therefore, is similar to other longacting benzodiazepine hypnotics.

Many benzodiazepines are substrates for CYP3A4 and CYP2C19. CYP3A inhibitors such as ketoconazole, nefazodone, and the antiretroviral lopinavir can substantially increase levels of CYP3A substrate benzodiazepines such as midazolam, diazepam and triazolam. Conversely, grapefruit juice, which induces CYP3A4, lowers levels of these benzodiazepines; however, even though alprazolam is a CYP3A4 substrate, it does not seem to be affected by grapefruit juice because of its high bioavailability.

Because administration of medications at shorter intervals than the elimination half-life leads to drug accumulation, medications such as diazepam and flurazepam accumulate with more than one daily dose, eventually resulting in increased daytime sedation. Clorazepate is not pharmacologically active but is a prodrug for desmethyldiazepam (nordiazepam), the primary active metabolite of diazepam and chlordiazepoxide. In contrast, temazepam, oxazepam, and lorazepam are conjugated directly and do not have active metabolites. Unlike barbiturates, benzodiazepines do not induce their own metabolism.

The combination of lipid solubility, potency, and elimination half-life of benzodiazepines ([Fig. 33.9b–5](#ff33-9b-5)) can be used to predict the onset and duration of action in clinical settings as well as the optimal frequency of drug administration. Benzodiazepines that are high in lipid solubility and potency and have a short half-life have a rapid onset but a brief duration of action and produce more intense and abrupt discontinuation syndromes. For example, midazolam, a high-potency and highly lipid-soluble nonselective full benzodiazepine receptor agonist with a short elimination halflife has an almost immediate onset of action when used intravenously for conscious sedation or anesthesia. As the medication leaves the CNS quickly, sedation diminishes rapidly, and after a single dose is followed by withdrawal symptoms (most frequently amnesia) in approximately 70% of patients. This profile makes midazolam useful for anesthesia and emergency treatment of agitation but impractical for chronic treatment. Alprazolam, which is high in lipid solubility and potency, reduces anxiety rapidly but can produce interdose withdrawal when it is administered in accordance with its intermediate half-life (three to four times per day) because it leaves the brain more rapidly than it is eliminated from the body. In contrast, chlordiazepoxide is low in lipid solubility and potency and has a long elimination half-life. This medication has a slow onset of action after a single dose and is administered less frequently in chronic treatment. Discontinuation of long half-life, low-potency, low lipidsoluble benzodiazepines such as chlordiazepoxide causes syndromes that are less intense but more prolonged than those associated with shorter-acting medications such as alprazolam.

### PRECAUTIONS AND ADVERSE EFFECTS

Although they are effective for most forms of anxiety and generally well-tolerated, benzodiazepines, like other medications, have the potential to reinforce reliance on something outside oneself for relief of anxiety, potentially undermining the development of confidence in the ability to master stressful situations. As noted earlier, rapid reduction of anxiety can enhance confidence in emergency settings such as the CCU, and it can facilitate exposure to phobic and other anxiety-provoking situations, but therapies such as CBT, mindfulness, and desensitization may be more effective at enhancing a sense of personal agency in patients with chronic anxiety.

The most common physical adverse effects of benzodiazepines include sedation, slowed reaction time, and impaired memory and psychomotor function, although these are not as severe as with barbiturates. Especially in older people, benzodiazepines can cause falls, syncope and fractures due to slowed reaction time, impaired balance and gait, and impaired vision. Falls are more frequent with chronic benzodiazepine use. The propylene glycol vehicle in parenteral lorazepam preparations has been linked to hyperosmolar metabolic acidosis.

#### Cognitive Side Effects

Withdrawal from short-acting, high potency benzodiazepines such as midazolam and triazolam can cause retrograde amnesia after discontinuation or between doses. Withdrawal probably also accounts for amnesia following a single dose of the high potency, highly lipid-soluble “date rape drug” flunitrazepam, especially when it is combined with alcohol. All benzodiazepines can cause anterograde amnesia and can interfere with new learning, although they usually do not impair access to memories that are already acquired. Impaired verbal memory, visuospatial memory, working memory, attention and executive function independent of the degree of underlying anxiety and depression have been demonstrated in people chronically using high doses of benzodiazepines. In a study of older patients with normal cognition, those taking benzodiazepines had poorer cognitive performance, but there was no difference in the rate of cognitive decline between people who were or were not taking benzodiazepines, suggesting the possibility of subclinical or prodromal features of dementia in patients taking benzodiazepines.

In a multicenter prospective depression trial, 353 patients treated with an SSRI alone were compared to 49 patients treated with an SSRI plus a benzodiazepine at baseline, 8 weeks, and 48 weeks of treatment on measures of depression, anxiety and cognitive function. Cognitive function was similar for both groups at baseline. Controlling for differences in severity of depression and anxiety, scores on the Digit Symbol Coding Test (a measure of information processing speed) were slightly better in patients treated with an antidepressant and a benzodiazepine than an antidepressant alone at 8 weeks and distinctly and significantly better at 1-year follow-up. The results suggest that the short-term addition of a benzodiazepine does not impair cognitive function in patients with MDD, while it could improve processing speed after short-term treatment and 1 year later.
