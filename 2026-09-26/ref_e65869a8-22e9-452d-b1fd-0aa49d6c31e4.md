<small>VALPROATE.</small>

Valproate (valproic acid [Depakene] and divalproex [Depakote]) is used to treat acute mania and for maintenance treatment of bipolar disorder. Therapeutic levels for acute mania are thought to range from 50 to 125 mcg/mL; based upon analysis of clinical trial data, most clinicians aim for a valproate level between 85 and 125 mcg/mL for acute mania. During maintenance treatment, clinicians work individually with patients to achieve a dose that is within a therapeutic range but remains tolerable. Side effects are more common with levels greater than 100 mcg/mL.

Because of the risk of hepatotoxicity and hematologic abnormalities during valproate treatment, baseline LFTs and hematologic measures are advised. Irreversible hepatic failure and agranulocytosis are rare, idiosyncratic effects of valproate; patients should be advised to report immediately for evaluation should they develop concerning symptoms. Hepatic necrosis is rare and is more likely to occur in the very young (younger than 2 years of age) and in individuals taking multiple anticonvulsants. More commonly, valproate may cause an asymptomatic elevation in liver transaminase levels and mild leukopenia or thrombocytopenia. Most clinicians monitor CBC and LFTs every 6 to 12 months during valproate treatment.

Valproate increases the risk of birth defects, including neural tube defects (with rates of 3% to 5%), craniofacial abnormalities, limb malformations, and cardiac defects. A pretreatment urine pregnancy test should be obtained in women of childbearing years. Women should be cautioned to use adequate contraception. Careful consideration of the known teratogenic effects of mood-stabilizing agents is essential when initiating treatment for a woman of childbearing age, regardless of whether or not she is currently pregnant. Given teratogenicity, many clinicians avoid prescribing valproate to any woman who has childbearing potential.

Lithium therapy can cause benign reversible T-wave changes; rarely, it can impair sinoatrial (SA) node function and can cause heart block. In treating patients with eating disorders, it may be reasonable to obtain ECG as patients commonly have low potassium levels that may result in abnormal ECG recordings. As the serum potassium drops below normal, T waves become flattened (or inverted), and U waves may appear.

### VALPROATE AND LABORATORY INTERFERENCE

Valproate occasionally interferes with the results of laboratory tests. As a fatty acid, serum levels, if measured, reflect an increase. Valproate metabolites may produce a false-positive result in urine analysis for ketones.

### DOSAGE AND ADMINISTRATION

In the outpatient setting, one should obtain a baseline hepatic panel and complete blood cell count before starting a patient on divalproex, although this can be ordered concomitantly with or after starting medications in more urgent treatment indications for hypomania or full-blown mania. An appropriate starting dose is 250 mg three times per day, with increases of 250 to 500 mg every several days to a therapeutic threshold (serum level greater than 50 mg/L). Once a steady-state dosage has been achieved, consolidating to bedtime dosing maximizes treatment adherence. The divalproex-ER preparation also lends itself well to single nighttime dosing. Rapid loading of divalproex (20 mg/kg/day) in acute mania has produced therapeutic serum levels more quickly than standard titration and has generally been well tolerated. However, high-dose IV valproate (20 mg/kg) over 30 minutes was not effective, and aggressive titration of the ER preparation was not more effective than less aggressive regimens. These findings add to the suggestion that actions induced by chronic administration of the drug are necessary for its antimanic and other psychotropic effects and suggest that more modest dosing strategies are likely to be as effective, produce fewer side effects, and be more clinically prudent.
