#### Psychosocial Treatment of Children with ADHD

Psychosocial treatment of children with ADHD refers to nonmedication treatment. This type of treatment includes different modalities such as psychoeducation, academic organization skill teaching and remediation, parent training, behavior modification, CBT, social skills training, and individual therapy. Of these modalities, parent training, intensive behavior modification, social skills training, and academic organizational skills have shown efficacy for children with ADHD in controlled trials. Standardized parent training programs have been evaluated in systematic reviews including parent–child interaction therapy, friendship coaching, and the positive parenting program. Children with ADHD may also have difficulties regulating emotions and anger management and would benefit from nonpharmacologic interventions to address these potential problems.

Behavioral interventions are the preferred choice of treatment for preschool children with ADHD and are recommended as adjuncts to medication in school-aged children and adolescents. Parent training and intensive behavioral interventions are based on principles that will enhance parenting techniques. These include parental psychoeducation about the course, risk factors, and long-term outcomes of ADHD, as well as education on and encouragement of the use of strategies that will address behavior both in and outside of the household. Techniques such as token economy, contingency management, setting small achievable goals, checklists, and closer attention to a child’s behavior especially when the child is behaving appropriately are encouraged. While the effects on behavior and performance in the classroom have been shown, the effects do not tend to generalize to other settings. In practice, behavioral therapy is often carried out in consultation with parents and teachers to establish ideal in-school and home management. However, published trials tend to rely on parent and teacher ratings of improvement but do not have independent evaluators. There is some evidence in recent reviews that, even with blinded assessments, the effects of behavioral therapy persist for improving parenting and decreasing child conduct problems.

Academic interventions can take several forms to aid the child with ADHD. Organizational skills such as time management education are useful and have been shown to be effective. However, for some children, their academic difficulties may have put them behind their peers, and they might also require some level of academic support or remediation. A diagnosis of ADHD may qualify a child for special education or related services, as well as accommodations in a regular classroom. All these avenues should be explored to best support the child’s learning.

Certain alternative interventions including physical activity and mindfulness may be useful supplements in the treatment of ADHD but have not yet been rigorously studied. Other interventions such as dietary interventions, neurofeedback/biofeedback, and mineral and vitamin supplementations are still largely unproven. Recent studies suggest some benefit from fish oil.

<small>PRESCHOOL ADHD TREATMENT STUDY (PATS).</small>

The Preschool ADHD Treatment Study (PATS) was also a large federally funded 14-month study designed to examine medication efficacy for younger children. All children and their parents first participated in a pre-trial, 10-week behavioral therapy, and training course. Only those children who still demonstrated significantly impairing ADHD symptoms were then randomized to receive immediate-release methylphenidate or placebo. Methylphenidate was significantly more effective than placebo at treating preschoolers with ADHD. However, at lower weight-adjusted, smaller effect sizes compared with school-aged children. In this regard, the mean optimal dose of methylphenidate was 14 mg/day (0.7 mg/kg/day), which is lower than the mean optimal dose of 1 mg/kg/day in the MTA study. In terms of tolerability, spontaneously reported moderate to severe adverse effects were present in approximately one in three preschoolers, including emotional outbursts, initial insomnia, repetitive behaviors/thoughts, decreased appetite, and irritability. Moreover, one in ten children discontinued treatment because of intolerable methylphenidate adverse effects (vs. <1% of school-age children in the MTA study).

Clinical Implications and the Role of Pharmacotherapy. Pharmacotherapy is a fundamental component of ADHD treatment in children and adolescents and is superior to behavioral therapy alone. Stimulant medications are among the most effective pharmacotherapies, although they vary considerably in release properties and duration of action. Additionally, in patients who have an incomplete or partial response to stimulants, adjunctive atomoxetine, guanfacine, and clonidine may boost response.

There has been considerable debate about the management of cardiovascular side effects of stimulant medications, and, at times, conflicting recommendations have been provided. Stimulants have been associated with elevations in mean blood pressure (<5 mm Hg) and heart rate (<10 beats/min). A subset of individuals (5% to 10%) may have an even greater increase in heart rate or blood pressure. Although there are limited prospective data related to the relationship between cardiac risk factors and outcomes in stimulant-treated youth, prudent recommendations from the American Heart Association and the American Academy of Pediatrics recommend obtaining a thorough history to identify high-risk youth with potential cardiac abnormalities or pre-existing cardiac disease prior to initiation of stimulant pharmacotherapy. Routine screening electrocardiography is *not* indicated in the absence of other cardiac risk factors.

<small>NOREPINEPHRINE REUPTAKE INHIBITORS (NRIS).</small>

The NRI atomoxetine has demonstrated efficacy in multiple controlled trials, although its efficacy is less than that of the stimulants. One meta-analysis reported atomoxetine’s effect size as 0.62, while the stimulants were from 0.91 (immediate release) to 0.95 (sustained release). However, atomoxetine exposure varies substantially (30-fold) across individuals, and part of this variability is explained by CYP2D6 activity. Dosing recommendations are available for atomoxetine when the CYP2D6 genotype/phenotype is known.

The second NRI to be approved for children and adolescents with ADHD is viloxazine which also has *in vitro*, antagonistic activity at 5-HT2B and agonistic activity at 5-HT2C receptors (and has high receptor occupancy at clinical doses).

<small>α-AGONISTS.</small>

Multiple RCTs support the efficacy of extended-release preparations of clonidine and guanfacine as monotherapy in children and adolescents with ADHD. Typically, extended-release clonidine is administered BID, and the extended-release guanfacine is given either qHS or qAM with equal efficacy throughout the day. Extended-release α-agonists are FDA approved as monotherapy as well as in combination with stimulants. Moreover, in youth who have a partial response to stimulants, α-agonists may boost ADHD response. Immediate-release formulations of clonidine and guanfacine are also used off-label in the treatment of youth with ADHD.

Selected Studies

<small>MULTIMODAL TREATMENT STUDY OF CHILDREN WITH ADHD (MTA).</small>

In MTA, children were randomized to one of four groups: medication treatment alone (with a structured algorithm-based approach), psychosocial treatment alone, algorithm-driven medication management, psychosocial treatment, or “community treatment.” The treatment algorithm involved monthly appointments with a physician during which the stimulant was titrated based on parent and teacher ratings. Treatment-related improvements were observed across all groups (at 14 months relative to baseline). However, algorithm-driven medication management was superior to intensive behavioral treatment alone and community treatment. It is noteworthy that the community treatment group had more limited physician follow-up, and, in this group, the average daily stimulant dose was significantly lower than in the algorithmic medication management group. Finally, approximately a quarter of youth who were randomized to behavioral management treatment required pharmacologic treatment secondary to a lack of effectiveness of the behavioral treatment. This finding highlights the role of pharmacotherapy in the management of many pediatric patients with ADHD.

Stimulant medication can be misused or diverted for the purpose of academic performance enhancement or euphoric effects, with immediate-release stimulants more often misused compared to extended-release formulations. A systematic review of 21 studies indicated the prevalence of psychostimulant misuse in the year prior to the study ranged from 5% to 9% in grade and high school–aged children and 5% to 35% in college-aged individuals. Close monitoring, pill counts, or switching to long-acting, less easily abused formulations should be tried and, if not successful, nonstimulant medication should be employed by the clinician if misuse or diversion is suspected.

Summary of Stimulant Medication. Continuous treatment of children with ADHD with psychostimulants is both effective and safe over the first 2 years. Long-acting preparations provide the convenience of once-daily dosing, better coverage, and reduced stigma associated with taking medication at school and can decrease the potential for abuse. Short-acting forms are useful for younger children who often require smaller doses than are available in extended-release preparations. Many adverse events can be handled by adjustment of dose and/or the time of administration of the medication. Long-term adverse events such as growth rate suppression concern parents but may be managed by instituting drug holidays to permit growth rebound.

#### Nonstimulant Medication in the Treatment of Children with ADHD

Whereas psychostimulants constitute the first-line treatment for children with ADHD, other medications need to be used for the treatment of patients who do not respond to or cannot tolerate stimulants. Some nonstimulant medications, such as Atomoxetine (ATX) and long-acting clonidine or guanfacine, have been approved by the FDA for the treatment of ADHD in children and adolescents. Advantages of nonstimulant medications include longer duration of action and the absence of psychostimulant effects such as behavioral rebound or delay of sleep onset. These drugs provide a moderate rather than a large effect size of stimulants but, nonetheless, provide clinical benefit.

Atomoxetine. ATX, a norepinephren reuptake inhibitor (NRI), is an approved alternative to stimulants. ATX may be considered in individuals where stimulants have failed or parents have refused stimulant treatment. In particular, for patients who are unable to tolerate stimulants because of worsening anxiety or tics, ATX is a reasonable alternative since it may ameliorate these comorbid conditions. ATX is well absorbed after oral administration and is minimally affected by food. At steady state, ATX provides 24 hours of coverage.

ATX is metabolized by the cytochrome P450 (CYP) 2D6 hepatic enzyme system. A fraction of the population are poor metabolizers of CYP 2D6–metabolized drugs. Such individuals may experience elevated plasma concentrations and extended plasma half-life; therefore, very slow initial titration may be required for all patients to prevent excessive dosing of these patients. Patients taking other medications metabolized by CYP 2D6, such as fluoxetine, paroxetine, and quinidine, might show signs of competitive inhibition, namely increased plasma levels of both ATX and the competing drug.

Common side effects of the α-adrenergic agents are sedation, fatigue, somnolence, headache, hypotension, syncope, and bradycardia. Clonidine is very sedating and has been used to treat sleep difficulties of children with ADHD, as well as to offset the delay in sleep onset caused by stimulant medications. Guanfacine is a more selective α-adrenergic agonist and is slightly less sedating than clonidine. The α-adrenergic agonists need to be discontinued very slowly to prevent rebound adrenergic overdrive, with hypertension, agitation, fever, headache, tachycardia, chest pain, sleep disturbance, nausea, and vomiting. Families need to be warned of the potential dangers of sudden discontinuation of this medication, even for holidays or weekends with another parent.

Bupropion. Bupropion is an antidepressant with dopaminergic and noradrenergic properties that has shown modest efficacy in ADHD but is less effective than stimulants or ATX. It is available in extended-release preparations that permit once-daily dosing. Bupropion may be considered as an option for patients presenting with significant depression. Starting dose for young adolescents is 75 mg twice a day to a maximum of 200 to 300 mg/day. The doses can be increased once weekly until improvement is noted. Bupropion usually takes 4 to 6 weeks at a therapeutic dose to show effect. Side effects of bupropion include fatigue, dry mouth, insomnia, headaches, nausea, vomiting, constipation, tremor, and skin rash. Seizures will reliably occur if the adolescent’s dose exceeds 400 mg/day. This medication is FDA approved for the treatment of depression and smoking cessation, but not ADHD, in individuals older than 18 years of age. Bupropion is considered a second-line, off-label treatment for ADHD.

Other Alternative Medications. Anticonvulsants, including carbamazepine, lithium, and SSRIs have not been shown to be effective for the treatment of ADHD. Second-generation antipsychotic medications such as risperidone have been shown to be effective for the treatment of aggression in children with disruptive behavior disorders, especially when combined with parent training and MPH.

#### Summary of Medication Treatments

Most children with ADHD respond to stimulant medication with good effect on all the core symptoms, making stimulant treatment the first-line medication treatment. If stimulants are ineffective or poorly tolerated, nonstimulant alternatives such as ATX can be used. Monotherapy with α-adrenergic agents is a third choice because they are less effective. Patients with a suboptimal response to stimulants or inability to tolerate an optimally effective dose may benefit from combination treatment with a stimulant and nonstimulant. Clonidine and guanfacine are sedating and have significant cardiovascular side effects. For all these drugs, careful monitoring and slow, cautious discontinuation are essential.
