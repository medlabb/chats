 ## Hepatic encephalopathy (HE)

**Definition and manifestations:** HE is a potentially reversible neuropsychiatric syndrome associated with liver dysfunction. Findings range from subtle cognitive impairment to coma and may include impaired attention, reaction time and working memory; sleep–wake disturbance, asterixis, bradykinesia, hyperreflexia, rigidity, and myoclonus. (1)

### Severity of overt HE
- **Grade I:** Behavioral or sleep changes  
- **Grade II:** Lethargy and moderate confusion  
- **Grade III:** Marked confusion or stupor, incoherent speech, arousable sleep  
- **Grade IV:** Coma and unresponsiveness to pain (2)

Patients with grades III–IV generally require hospital admission, typically intensive care; airway protection and possible intubation should be considered. Grade I may be managed outpatient only when reliable caregivers can monitor the patient. (2)

### Diagnosis and evaluation
Diagnosis is clinical and requires assessment of cognitive and neuromuscular function, with exclusion of other causes of altered mental status. Psychometric testing may help identify covert HE. Laboratory testing should evaluate metabolic and electrolyte abnormalities; brain CT is reserved for situations suggesting another neurologic cause, such as trauma or focal neurologic findings. (1)

An elevated ammonia level is **not required** for diagnosis, and an elevated level without clinical manifestations is not an indication for treatment. (2; 1)

### Search for precipitating factors
Evaluate for and treat:
- Gastrointestinal bleeding
- Infection, including spontaneous bacterial peritonitis and urinary infection
- Hypokalemia or metabolic alkalosis
- Kidney failure or hypovolemia
- Hypoxia
- Sedatives or benzodiazepines
- Hypoglycemia
- Constipation
- Less commonly, hepatocellular carcinoma or vascular occlusion (3)

Evaluation may include medication/toxin history, examination for bleeding or hypovolemia, blood and urine cultures, paracentesis when ascites is present, serum chemistries, and a urine drug screen. (1)

### Initial treatment
1. **Correct precipitating causes** and provide supportive care, including prevention of dehydration and electrolyte abnormalities, nutritional support, a safe environment, and fall precautions. (4; 3)  
2. **Lactulose or lactitol:** Typical oral lactulose dosing is **30–45 mL (20–30 g) two to four times daily**, titrated to **two to three soft stools daily** without diarrhea. Enemas may be used when oral administration is not possible. (4; 3)  
3. **Rifaximin:** If there is no improvement within 48 hours or lactulose/lactitol cannot be used, rifaximin **550 mg orally twice daily** may be added or used as an alternative. For recurrent HE, rifaximin can be added to ongoing lactulose or lactitol. (4; 3)

### Nutrition
Routine dietary protein restriction is **not recommended**. Suggested nutritional support includes approximately **35–40 kcal/kg/day** and **1.2–1.5 g protein/kg ideal body weight/day**. Small meals throughout the day and a late-night complex-carbohydrate snack are recommended. (2; 4)
