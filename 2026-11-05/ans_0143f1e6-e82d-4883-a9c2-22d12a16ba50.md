## Answer

 ## **Harrison-based management of enteric fever**

### 1. Initial principles
- Start **prompt, susceptibility-guided antibiotic therapy**; the initial choice depends on local or travel-associated antimicrobial susceptibility patterns. Appropriate therapy reduces mortality to **<1%**. (1)
- Because decreased ciprofloxacin susceptibility is common in the Indian subcontinent and some African regions, **fluoroquinolones should not be used empirically** there. In patients with possible ceftriaxone-resistant *S. Typhi* after travel to Pakistan, empiric **azithromycin or a carbapenem** is recommended. (1)

### 2. Antibiotic therapy in adults

| Clinical situation | Preferred regimen | Duration |
|---|---|---:|
| **Empirical treatment** | Ceftriaxone **2 g IV daily** | 10–14 days |
|  | Ciprofloxacin **500 mg PO twice daily** or **400 mg IV every 12 h** | 5–7 days |
|  | Azithromycin **1 g PO daily** | 7–10 days |
| **Fully susceptible infection** | Ceftriaxone, ciprofloxacin, or azithromycin as above | As above |
| **Fully susceptible—alternatives** | Amoxicillin **1 g PO three times daily** or **2 g IV every 6 h** | 14 days |
|  | Chloramphenicol **25 mg/kg three times daily** PO/IV | 14–21 days |
|  | Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole **160/800 mg PO twice daily** | 7–14 days |
| **Ceftriaxone-resistant infection** | Meropenem **1 g IV every 8 h** or azithromycin **1 g PO daily** | 10–14 or 7–10 days, respectively |

These regimens and durations are from **Harrison Table 171-1, “Antibiotic Therapy for Enteric Fever in Adults.”** (1)

Other third-generation cephalosporin options include cefotaxime **2 g IV every 8 h** or cefixime **400 mg PO twice daily**. Carbapenems are increasingly used for complicated XDR *S. Typhi* infection, although cost and the need for IV administration are limitations. (2)

### 3. Outpatient versus inpatient treatment
- Most patients with **uncomplicated enteric fever** can be treated at home with **oral antibiotics and antipyretics**. (2)
- Hospitalize patients with **persistent vomiting, diarrhea, and/or abdominal distension**. Provide supportive therapy and a parenteral third-generation cephalosporin, fluoroquinolone, or carbapenem according to susceptibility. Treatment should continue for **at least 10 days or for 5 days after fever resolution**. (2)

### 4. Severe or complicated disease
- **Intestinal bleeding or perforation** requires immediate fluid resuscitation, surgical intervention, broadened antibiotic coverage for polymicrobial peritonitis, and management of gastrointestinal hemorrhage; bowel resection may be required. (3)
- In critically ill patients with **shock and obtundation**, dexamethasone was associated with lower mortality when given with chloramphenicol: an initial **3 mg/kg**, followed by eight doses of **1 mg/kg every 6 h**. Severe enteric fever remains one of the few acute bacterial infections for which glucocorticoids may be indicated; therapy beyond **48 hours may increase relapse**. (2)

### 5. Chronic carriage
- Chronic carriage occurs in approximately **2–5%** of patients with fluoroquinolone-susceptible *S. Typhi*. Treat with **ciprofloxacin 500–750 mg PO twice daily for 28 days**, with an eradication rate of approximately 80%. (2)
- For fluoroquinolone-resistant strains, **azithromycin for 4 weeks** may be used, although clinical experience is limited. Amoxicillin may be considered if the isolate is ampicillin-susceptible. When biliary, renal, or bladder stones are present, eradication may require both antibiotics and surgical correction. (2)
