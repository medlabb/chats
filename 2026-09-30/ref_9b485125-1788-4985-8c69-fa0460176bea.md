#### NONARTERITIC ANTERIOR ISCHEMIC OPTIC NEUROPATHY

Idiopathic ischemic infarction of the anterior portion of the optic nerve is the major cause of visual loss after age 50. Men and women are equally affected. Risk factors include hypertension, diabetes, and a small optic disc, all of which impair perfusion of the optic nerve. Although atherosclerosis is often assumed to be the cause, the fact that symptoms are commonly first noted on awakening suggests that nocturnal arterial hypotension (eg, from antihypertensive medications) may be responsible.

Visual loss is sudden in onset, painless, always monocular, and without premonitory ocular symptoms. The visual deficit is usually maximal at onset and frequently subtotal; the pattern of visual loss varies between patients. In some cases, the course is stuttering or progressive.

Examination reveals ipsilateral disk swelling, often with peripapillary hemorrhages. A relative afferent pupillary defect is present. MRI of the optic nerve is normal. Treatment with glucocorticoids (eg, [prednisone](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660281) 80 mg daily for 14 days, then tapered over 1 month), beginning during the first 2 weeks, may enhance recovery. [Aspirin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660322) is ineffective.

Spontaneous improvement occurs over 6 months, and as disk swelling resolves, ophthalmoscopic evaluation shows optic atrophy. Recurrence in the same eye is uncommon, but 15-25% of patients experience involvement of the contralateral eye within 3-5 years. Modification of vascular risk factors is indicated.


#### ARTERITIC ANTERIOR ISCHEMIC OPTIC NEUROPATHY

Arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy occurs most often after age 70, is especially common in patients of Scandinavian descent, and is usually due to **giant cell (temporal) arteritis**, which involves medium- and large-diameter arteries. The female to male ratio is 3:1. Visual loss is usually accompanied by systemic symptoms, such as fever, malaise, night sweats, weight loss, and headache (see [Chapter 6](/content.aspx?legacysectionid=greenberg11_ch6), Headache & Facial Pain), and often by **polymyalgia rheumatica** (see [Chapter 9](/content.aspx?legacysectionid=greenberg11_ch9), Motor Disorders). Scalp tenderness and jaw claudication may occur. Visual loss is usually sudden and painless, is often total, and can be monocular or binocular. Transient visual obscuration may precede optic nerve infarction. Established visual loss is rarely reversible, and monocular visual loss becomes bilateral in 25-50% of patients. On examination, the optic disk appears swollen and pale. The erythrocyte sedimentation rate (Westergren method) is typically 50-100 mm/h, and C-reactive protein is increased. Definitive diagnosis is by temporal artery biopsy.

#### SARCOIDOSIS

Sarcoidosis can produce mononeuropathy or, rarely, polyneuropathy. The mononeuropathy commonly involves cranial nerves, especially the **facial nerve**. In some instances, a small-fiber neuropathy leads to pain, dysesthesias, and autonomic involvement. Clinical evaluation for extraneural disease, x-rays of the lungs and bones, examination of the CSF, and determination of serum levels of angiotensin-converting enzyme are helpful in establishing the diagnosis of sarcoidosis. Treatment with [prednisone](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660281), 60 mg/d orally followed by tapering doses, may speed recovery.


#### SEPSIS & MULTIORGAN FAILURE

Patients with sepsis and multiorgan failure may develop a **critical illness polyneuropathy**. This manifests primarily as weakness and is therefore discussed in [Chapter 9](/content.aspx?legacysectionid=greenberg11_ch9), Motor Disorders.
