### TREATMENT

Good long-term clinical care is contingent on adequate patient education about the nature of the disorder and the effects of treatment. Once a diagnosis is made, clinicians should provide sufficient information to assist adult patients in choosing treatment options that meet individual patient needs. Given the degree of misinformation about ADHD from the Internet and other lay sources, patients are well served when clinicians guide them to appropriate reference materials. Many individuals are relieved to understand that they have a biologically based condition that is shared by a significant number of other adults. National organizations such as Children and Adults with ADD (CHADD, [www.chadd.org](http://www.chadd.org)) or the National Alliance for the Mentally Ill (NAMI, [www.nami.org](http://www.nami.org)) provide access to local support networks and reliable educational materials.

Pharmacotherapy remains a treatment cornerstone. Medications found to be useful in the treatment of childhood ADHD are generally shown to be effective in adults. FDA-approved medications for adult ADHD include both stimulants and nonstimulants. Of the nonstimulants, atomoxetine has the greatest amount of clinical trial evidence supporting its use in adults. Medications commonly used off-label include bupropion, immediate alpha-2-agonists, tricyclic antidepressants, and the wake-promoting agents modafinil and armodafinil. Stimulants are the most frequently prescribed ADHD medications in adults, and their greatest clinical effects are seen with robust dosing. One noteworthy difficulty for adults is the frequent necessity for multiple daily dosing, even with extended-release formulations, in order to provide adequate symptom relief throughout the day. College students, in particular, have irregular daily class schedules and might not be best served with reliance on morning-administered extended-release stimulants. Well-considered use of immediate-release compounds is necessary at times to meet the clinical needs of these patients.

Many clinicians are reluctant to prescribe stimulants based on concerns about abuse, misuse, and illicit diversion. Misuse and diversion of prescribed stimulants among college students are common. In most cases, these medications are used with the expectation of improving academic performance, although there are no data to support this outcome. Approximately 30% of stimulant misuse among college-age students is for recreational purposes, that is, to cause euphoria. Misuse for academic purposes is highest among white male students attending academically competitive universities, who are in fraternities, are in academic distress, and with prior histories of conduct-disordered behaviors and recreational drug, alcohol, and nicotine use. Several research reports recommend using prodrug and certain extended-release stimulant formulations, deemed less likely to be used for recreational purposes compared with older immediate-release forms if the clinician is concerned about potential misuse. Nonstimulant medications remain an option in patients at high risk for stimulant abuse or misuse. Proper record keeping and thorough documentation of prescriptions written are essential to good practice. When available, clinicians can verify that patients are not obtaining inappropriate amounts of controlled substances by checking prescription records on government-sponsored databases.

The Preschool ADHD Treatment Study (PATS) is a double-blind, placebo-controlled, crossover trial that examined the effects of MPH-IR in 165 children, aged 3 to 5.5 years, diagnosed with severe ADHD. Subjects were randomly assigned to four treatment arms, each at a different MPH dose (1.25, 2.5, 5, or 7.5 mg, three times daily), or placebo. Significant improvements in ADHD symptoms were found with an average MPH dose of 14.2 ± 8 mg/day. This average effective dose was considerably lower than the effective dose reported in the MTA study of older children (30.5 ± 14.2 mg/day). Twenty-one percent of the total subjects in the trial were reported to be “excellent responders.” During the 10-month maintenance phase of the PATS, 95 of the 140 children demonstrated continued improvement in global functioning, including improvement in social skills. However, parent and teacher symptom rating scales declined over the same time period for about one-third of these children. PATS had a relatively lower effect size range (Cohen’s d = 0.22 to 0.87) compared to the MTA study (Cohen’s d = 0.8 to 1.2).

Several adverse effects were reported in the PATS. Approximately 30% of PATS subjects developed moderate to severe side effects, and up to 11% of the subjects discontinued treatment due to medication intolerability. Side effects included insomnia, appetite loss, emotional outbursts, repetitive thoughts/behaviors, and irritability. Thus, based on findings from the MTA and PATS studies, it appears that compared to school-aged children, psychostimulants have lower short-term efficacy and lower tolerability in preschoolers. One-third of preschool-aged subjects discontinued stimulants due to adverse events such as worsening behaviors, while others discontinued to switch to a long-acting stimulant or discontinued due to inadequate benefit. Indeed, over a 6-year follow-up period in PATS, the diagnosis of ADHD in preschoolers was relatively stable, with a course characterized generally as chronic, with high symptom severity and impairment, especially in very young children with moderate-to-severe ADHD, despite treatment with medication.

A Cochrane review of seven RCTs, with 1,091 subjects combined, examined the efficacy and tolerability of three different AMPH agents (dextroamphetamine, lisdexamfetamine, and mixed AMPH salts [MAS]) in adults with ADHD. While dexamphetamine, lisdexamfetamine, and MAS, all improved core ADHD symptoms, MAS was associated with the lowest incidence of adverse effects and highest retention in treatment. In other pooled analyses of 19 trials in adults, effect sizes varied based on whether the psychostimulants were long-acting (pooled Cohen’s d = 0.73) or immediate-release (pooled Cohen’s d = 0.96) formulations. LDX had a significantly higher pooled effect size (Cohen’s d = 1.5) based on several RCTs compared to other psychostimulants.
