### DEFINITIONS

*Tics* are rapid, recurring motor movements (motor tics) or vocalizations (phonic or vocal tics) that are nonrhythmic, involuntary or semivoluntary, and sudden in onset. Tics typically involve one muscle or a group of muscles and are characterized by their anatomical location, number, frequency, duration, and complexity ([Table 47–1](#tt47-1)). They can be classified as *simple* (involving one muscle, muscle group, or sound) or *complex* (slower, more purposeful movements involving coordinated muscle groups or multiple sounds). Simple motor tics cause a brief, jerking movement (*clonic tic*); a briefly sustained abnormal posture (*dystonic tic*); or an isometric contraction (*tonic tic*). Examples of simple motor clonic tics are eye blinking, shoulder shrugging, and head-turning. Dystonic tics include sustained eye closure (blepharospasm), ocular deviations, teeth grinding (bruxism), mouth opening, twisting and tilting of the neck (torticollis), and shoulder rotation. Tonic tics are typically manifested by tensing of abdominal or limb muscles. Complex motor tics may include touching objects, jumping, rotating, or imitating others’ gestures (*echopraxia*). Examples of simple vocal tics are throat clearing, coughing, and sniffing. Repeating syllables, words, or one’s own phrases (*palilalia*) or repeating others’ words or phrases (*echolalia*) are examples of complex vocal tics. Less commonly occurring tics include the involuntary vocalization of obscenities (*coprolalia*) and the involuntary production of obscene gestures (*copropraxia*).

Tics are often emitted in bouts of one or several tics separated by variable periods of no tic activity. Tics change in frequency, anatomical location, number, complexity, and severity over minutes, hours, and days, as well as over longer periods of time. Tics may persist during sleep. Simple motor tics are the most common initial presentation of a tic disorder, and eye blinking is the most common initial tic.

Tics are experienced as irresistible but can be suppressed for varying periods of time. Some patients describe suppressing tics for several hours, for example, while at work or school, and then experiencing a bout of tics. There is usually a reduction in the frequency and severity of tics during concentration on mental or physical tasks such as reading. Stress, excitement, boredom, fatigue, and exposure to heat can exacerbate tics. Tic frequency may also increase during relaxation following a period of stress or sometimes when an individual is actively attempting to suppress tics. Some children, and occasionally adults, are unaware of their tics.

Many individuals with TD characterize their tics as a voluntary response to an uncomfortable feeling that precedes them. These feelings, or premonitory sensations, have been described by many individuals with TD; they can be localized or general, and physical or mental, in nature. These premonitory sensations distinguish tic disorders from other movement disorders. An example is a burning feeling in the eye before an eye blink. However, there are often nonlocalizable and more generalized premonitory phenomena, such as urges, sensations, or anxiety. Many individuals report a “just right phenomenon,” that to relieve the uncomfortable urge they must repeat a particular movement or sound or pattern of movements or sounds, until “it feels good” or “it feels just right.”

Complex tics may be difficult to distinguish from compulsive behaviors. This is further complicated by the frequent presentation of obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) symptoms with tics. The difference between some complex tics and compulsions is subtle. It is important to determine whether the behavior is being pursued as a strategy to prevent or relieve anxiety rather than simply to satisfy an urge. If anxiety relief or prevention is the case, a compulsion rather than a tic is likely. Studies by Miguel and coworkers demonstrated that more cognitions and autonomic anxiety, and fewer sensory phenomena are reported with repetitive behaviors in individuals with OCD without concomitant tics, whereas more sensory phenomena, such as the “just right phenomenon,” and fewer cognitions are associated with repetitive behaviors in individuals with TD.

The fifth edition of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders text revision (DSM-5-TR) and the eleventh revision of the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems (ICD-11) provide diagnostic criteria in common use for tic disorders. According to the DSM-5-TR (with the equivalent ICD-11 diagnoses in parentheses), tic disorders include provisional tic disorder (transient motor tics in ICD-11), persistent motor or vocal tic disorder (chronic motor or vocal tic disorder in ICD-11), TD (Tourette syndrome in ICD-11), and other specified tic disorder and unspecified tic disorder (other specified primary tics or tic disorders, primary tics or tic disorders, unspecified, other specified tic disorders, and tic disorders, unspecified in ICD-11).

By DSM-5-TR criteria, a provisional tic disorder is characterized by the presence of single or multiple motor and/or vocal tics for less than 1 year. A persistent motor or vocal tic disorder is diagnosed when either motor or vocal tics, but not both, are present for more than 1 year. For this category of tic disorders, DSM-5-TR provides specifiers to indicate whether motor or vocal tics are present: *With motor tics only* and *With vocal tics only.* TD (Tourette syndrome in ICD-11) is defined by the presence of both multiple motor and one or more vocal tics for more than 1 year.

According to the DSM-5-TR, other specified tic disorder is a tic disorder that does not meet the criteria for any of the other tic disorders, and the clinician specifies the reason criteria are not met. An unspecified tic disorder, on the other hand, is a condition that also does not meet criteria for any of the other tic disorders, but for which the clinician does not elaborate a specific reason. Included are presentations for which information to make a more specific diagnosis is lacking.

### COURSE AND PROGNOSIS

The typical course of TD is characterized by onset of simple motor tics of the eyes, face, head, or neck by about age 6 or 7 years, followed by a rostral-caudal progression of motor tics over several years to involve the shoulders, trunk, and extremities. Eye blinking is the most common initial tic. Vocal tics typically start at ages 8 to 9 years; more-complex tics often begin later, as do obsessive-compulsive symptoms, at about ages 11 to 12 years. As many as half of clinically referred TD patients may show signs of ADHD, such as hyperactivity, impulsivity, and distractibility, prior to the onset of tics. Tics range from mild to severe and tend to stabilize over time; most children evaluated even in specialty clinics have mild to moderate tics. Some patients have lengthy periods during which most or all manifestations attenuate or remit. Recent studies indicate that tic severity improves significantly in most patients by early to mid adolescence.

Social functioning may be impaired because of bullying or rejection by peers or anxiety about having tics in social situations. Tics may impair academic functioning by their disruptive impact in class or interference with homework completion. Academic underachievement across educational levels is seen in many seeking treatment for TD or chronic tic disorders. Occupational functioning may be impaired by inability to use a computer or complete work tasks. By early adulthood, tics are greatly diminished in most individuals, and some are tic-free. Persistent tic symptoms may cause considerable distress and can lead to social isolation and personality changes. In severe cases, tics may directly interfere with daily activities. For example, violent head and neck tics may cause physical complications, such as compressive cervical myelopathy.

Fernández de la Cruz and peers report that there is a fourfold increase in the risk of suicide in individuals with tic disorders. The strongest predictor of risk is the presence of tics beyond the second decade of life. Psychiatric comorbidity and a history of suicide attempts increase risk as well.

#### Comorbid Psychiatric Disorders

Hirschtritt and colleagues conducted a large cross-sectional study of TD patients and unaffected family members, estimating a lifetime prevalence of psychiatric comorbidity in those with TD at 85.7%. 57.7% had two or more psychiatric comorbidities. The relationship between TD and frequently observed comorbid psychiatric disorders has not yet been disentangled. Obsessive-compulsive symptoms, developmentally inappropriate motoric hyperactivity and inattention, anxiety, and aggressive dyscontrol have often been described in patients with TD. Motor, vocal, behavioral, cognitive, and emotional dysfunction may represent manifestations of an underlying central core disinhibition.

Those with tics are more likely to experience separation anxiety and physical symptoms of anxiety than anxious youth without tics.

Coffey and colleagues reported that comorbid mood disorders were the most robust predictors of psychiatric hospitalization and illness severity in a cohort of TD patients. Tic severity was only marginally significant as a predictor of psychiatric hospitalization. In a controlled study of older adolescents with TD, Gorman and colleagues found that, independent of comorbidity with ADHD, TD patients had significantly higher rates of depression and poorer overall outcome than healthy controls.

More recently, the work of Rizzo and peers demonstrated that depression in those with TD is associated with tic severity, the presence of comorbid ADHD, co-occurring anxiety, disruptive behavior, and a family history of depression. Whether mood symptoms are primary (i.e., related to the underlying pathophysiology of TD) or secondary to the demoralization and/or impairment related to having a chronic illness remains to be further clarified.

A substantial minority of youth with TD evaluated in clinical settings may meet criteria for intermittent explosive disorder manifested by rage attacks or oppositional defiant disorder. Budman and colleagues reported that children with TD as well as ADHD and OCD are more likely to experience explosive outbursts, and the number of outbursts is positively correlated with the number of comorbid conditions. Tic type and severity are not linked to the presence of explosive outbursts.

Learning and academic problems are common in children with TD; in a database of 5,450 patients with TD, 1,235 (22.7%) had learning disabilities. Young patients with TD may experience problems with executive functions, such as planning, persistence, organizational skills, and social problem-solving. Patients with TD have been consistently shown to have difficulties with procedural learning, fine motor control, motor inhibition, nonverbal memory, and visual motor integration. In addition, handwriting may be poor. Comorbid ADHD confers a greater risk of learning and academic problems in those with tic disorders.

### TREATMENT

Individuals with mild and even some moderate tics may not need treatment if the tics are not causing significant distress or impairment. Simple, active monitoring may be all that is necessary for some individuals with mild tics. Indications for treatment of tics include physical pain or discomfort, interference with social interactions, and impairment in any aspect of educational or occupational functioning. The first step in treatment is proper education of the patient, family members, teachers, and others who interact with the patient. Parents, educators, and physicians must work as partners to advocate for the optimal school environment for children with tic disorders. National and local support groups, such as the Tourette Association of America ([https://www.tourette.org](https://www.tourette.org)), can provide additional information and can serve as a valuable resource for patients and their families.

Nonpharmacologic treatments have been developed that are as successful as pharmacotherapy. In fact, the first-line treatment for mild to moderate tics is cognitive-behavioral intervention. Medications are usually considered only when symptoms are unable to be addressed by psychotherapeutic interventions and/or interfere with academic or occupational performance, peer relationships, social interactions, or activities of daily living. Pharmacotherapy for tic disorders is individualized and tailored specifically to the needs of the patient; the most distressing and/or impairing symptoms are targeted first. The goal of treatment is not to eliminate tics but to relieve tic-related discomfort or distress and to achieve a degree of control of tics that allows the patient to function as normally as possible.

#### General Guidelines

Behavioral intervention is the cornerstone of effective treatment for tic disorders. Treatment goals should be to reduce symptoms, support adaptive functioning and strengths, and enhance developmental progress. Self-esteem should always be supported, given that most patients with tic disorders will have at least some sense of personal and emotional vulnerability. Early in the diagnostic process, education in phenomenology and the natural history of tic disorders is essential.

Clarification that the patient’s symptoms are not voluntary in most situations eases psychological burdens for both patients and families. This is especially important because families observe that tics, at times, can be suppressed. Struggles over whether the symptoms are tics or “negative behavior” are pointless and should be curtailed; a more practical goal involves personal management of, and responsibility for, symptoms and behavior, regardless of their origin.

Containment is another cornerstone of treatment. Even with the use of effective medications, it is rare for tics to remit completely. The use of a “tic room” or a “time out” area provides an opportunity to contain problematic tics or compulsions and to “de-stimulate.” Because emotional conflicts and stress frequently increase symptom intensity and frequency, time-limited withdrawal from stressful situations can be beneficial. A balanced, healthy diet and exercise regimen may contribute to stress reduction and overall well-being. Caffeine and excess amounts of sugar should be minimized because they might exacerbate tics in some patients.
