## Answer

 ## **Osteoporosis**

**Osteoporosis** is a skeletal disorder characterized by compromised bone strength, increasing the risk of fracture. Bone strength depends on both **bone density** and **bone quality**, including bone architecture, geometry, turnover, mineralization, and accumulated microfractures. (1)

### Epidemiology and impact
- It affects approximately **50% of women and 20% of men older than 50 years**. (1)
- In the United States, approximately **2 million osteoporotic fractures** occur annually, including nearly **300,000 hip fractures**. Hip fractures have a first-year mortality rate exceeding 20%. (2)
- Vertebral fractures are frequently clinically silent; only about one third of radiologically diagnosed vertebral fractures receive medical attention. (3)

### Risk factors and causes
Risk factors include:
- **Postmenopausal estrogen deficiency**
- Low calcium or vitamin D intake/insufficiency
- Low physical activity or immobilization
- Smoking, alcohol abuse, excessive thinness, and falls
- Older age, poor vision, impaired balance, gait problems, weak muscles, and previous falls
- Family history of osteoporosis or hip fracture
- Glucocorticoids and other medications, including anticonvulsants, excess thyroid hormone, heparin, aromatase inhibitors, and androgen-deprivation therapy
- Hyperparathyroidism, hyperthyroidism, hypercortisolism, hypogonadism, gastrointestinal malabsorption, and chronic illnesses (3)

Unexplained osteoporosis with diffuse osteopenia, bone pain, or fractures should prompt consideration of infiltrative disorders. (1)

### Clinical presentation
Osteoporosis is usually **silent until a fracture occurs**. Vertebral fractures may occur with minimal stress, such as lifting, sneezing, or bending. Acute vertebral fracture can cause back pain, while chronic vertebral osteoporosis may lead to height loss, kyphosis, and severe cervical lordosis (“dowager’s hump”). (4)

### Bone mineral density classification
The WHO classification based on central DXA is:

| Classification | BMD T-score |
|---|---:|
| Normal | ≥ −1.0 |
| Low bone mass/osteopenia | Between −1.0 and −2.5 |
| Osteoporosis | ≤ −2.5 |

FRAX estimates 10-year hip or major osteoporotic fracture risk using factors such as femoral-neck T-score, age, sex, height, weight, previous fracture, parental hip fracture, smoking, glucocorticoid use, rheumatoid arthritis, alcohol intake, and secondary osteoporosis. (5)

The National Osteoporosis Foundation recommends bone mineral density assessment for women aged **65 years or older**, men aged **70 years or older**, adults aged 50 years or older with a fracture, and younger individuals with relevant risk factors or conditions associated with bone loss. (5)

### Treatment options described in the references
- **Bisphosphonates** are described as the mainstay of osteoporosis prevention and treatment. Listed agents include alendronate, risedronate, ibandronate, and zoledronic acid. (6)
- **Denosumab** is administered as a 60-mg subcutaneous injection every 6 months; calcium intake is encouraged because hypocalcemia may occur. Bone loss and vertebral compression fractures can occur after discontinuation, and switching to an oral or intravenous bisphosphonate is suggested. (7)
- **Teriparatide** is an osteoanabolic agent used for up to 2 years in patients at high fracture risk; antiresorptive therapy afterward may help prevent bone loss. (8)
- The referenced FDA-approved therapy table includes antiresorptive, anabolic, hormone/estrogen, and dual-action agents, with their approved indications and dosing. (6)
