### A. Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus

Type 1 diabetes is an autoimmune disorder with progressive destruction of pancreatic islet β cells resulting in endogenous insulin deficiency and hyperglycemia. In this catabolic disorder, circulating insulin is virtually absent, plasma [glucagon](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660113) is elevated (since not suppressed by insulin), and pancreatic β cells fail to respond to all insulinogenic stimuli. Exogenous insulin is required to reverse the catabolic state, prevent ketosis, reduce hyperglucagonemia, and decrease blood glucose.

The rate of β cell destruction is variable, being rapid in some individuals and slow in others. The number of islet autoantibodies combined with parameters of glucose tolerance form the basis of risk prediction for type 1 diabetes. There are three distinct stages in the development of type 1 diabetes. Stage 1 occurs when there is β cell autoimmunity (presence of islet autoantibodies) but there is normoglycemia. Stage 2 occurs with there is abnormal glucose tolerance but patients remain asymptomatic. Stage 3 is the development of symptomatic type 1 diabetes.

Although type 1 diabetes commonly occurs in children and young adults, it can occur at any age and 50% of new cases are diagnosed in adults. Misclassification of type 1 diabetes in adults is common, and > 40% of those developing type 1 diabetes after age 30 years are initially treated for type 2 diabetes. The term *latent autoimmune diabetes of adults* (LADA) has been used to identify adults who develop immune-mediated type 1 diabetes. The rate of β cell destruction in patients with LADA is slower than in those who have a “classic” presentation of type 1 diabetes (diabetic ketoacidosis [DKA]). While there is a debate as to whether the term LADA should be used as a diagnosis, the ADA Standards of Care suggests that for the purpose of classification, any form of diabetes mediated by autoimmune β cell destruction should be classified as type 1 diabetes. Therefore, the use of the term LADA in adults who may have progressive autoimmune β cell destruction is acceptable since it can increase clinicians’ diagnostic awareness and accelerate appropriate insulin initiation to prevent diabetic ketoacidosis. Some individuals may retain enough β cell function to avoid ketosis, but as their β cell mass diminishes, the need for insulin therapy develops. Islet cell antibody surveys among persons of northern European descent indicate that up to 15% of patients with “type 2” diabetes may have LADA.

Many patients with type 1 diabetes mellitus have circulating antibodies to islet cells (ICA), glutamic acid decarboxylase 65 (GAD65), insulin (IAA), tyrosine phosphatase IA2 (ICA-512), and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8) at the time the diagnosis is made ([Table 32–2](#papacmdt26_ch32tb2)); screening with these autoimmune markers may identify about 98% of patients who have an autoimmune basis for their β cell loss. These antibodies also facilitate screening of siblings of affected children and adults with atypical features of type 2 diabetes mellitus. Up to 10% of patients with new-onset type 1 diabetes have no detectable islet autoantibodies; therefore, the absence of autoantibodies does not exclude type 1 diabetes. Antibody levels also decline with increasing duration of disease.

### C. Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus

Type 2 diabetes is defined by several characteristic abnormalities including resistance to the action of insulin in peripheral tissues (muscle, liver, and fat), defective β cell insulin secretion, decreased skeletal muscle glucose uptake, and increased production of glucose by the liver. Early in the disease process, hyperplasia of pancreatic β cells occurs and probably accounts for the fasting hyperinsulinism and exaggerated insulin and proinsulin responses to glucose and other stimuli. With time, chronic deposition of amyloid in the islets may combine with inherited genetic defects to progressively impair β cell function. β Cell function is not completely lost and is usually sufficient to prevent ketoacidosis but is inadequate to prevent hyperglycemia. This form of diabetes used to occur predominantly in adults, but it is now more frequently encountered in children and adolescents. The clinical presentation of type 2 diabetes is heterogeneous, with widely variable age at onset, severity of the condition, and degree of obesity.

Genetic and environmental factors combine to cause both the β cell loss and the insulin resistance. Most epidemiologic data indicate strong genetic influences, since in monozygotic twins > 40 years of age, concordance develops in > 70% of cases within a year whenever type 2 diabetes develops in one twin. Genome-wide association studies have identified > 700 risk variants and putative regulator mechanisms for type 2 diabetes. A significant number of the identified loci appear to code for proteins that have a role in β cell function or development (*CDKAL1*, *SLC30A8*, *HHEX-IDE*, *CDKN2A/B*, *KCNJ11*, *IGF2BP2*). One of the genetic loci with the largest risk effect is *TCF7L2*. This gene codes for a transcription factor involved in the Wnt signaling pathway that is required for normal pancreatic development. Two loci (*FTO* and *MC4R*) affect fat mass and obesity risk. The *PPARG* locus has a critical role in adipocyte differentiation, survival, and function. The identification of at-risk alleles has made it possible to generate a polygenic risk score to estimate an individual's lifetime genetic risk for type 2 diabetes. These risk scores, however, are not yet routinely used by clinicians in the absence of guidelines on their use. Several environmental factors may play a crucial role in the development of the type 2 diabetes. These include excessive caloric intake leading to obesity and a sedentary lifestyle, medications that cause metabolic abnormalities, and disruption of the gut microbiome and circadian rhythm.

**Obesity is the most important environmental factor causing insulin resistance.** The degree and prevalence of obesity with type 2 diabetes vary among different racial groups. In patients with type 2 diabetes, obesity or overweight is apparent in just 50% of Chinese and Japanese patients, 80% of North American or European patients, 50–80% of African patients, and nearly 100% of patients who are Pima Indians or Pacific Islanders from Nauru or Samoa.

## EPIDEMIOLOGIC CONSIDERATIONS

An estimated 38.4 million persons (11.6%) in the United States have diabetes mellitus, of whom approximately 5–10% have type 1 diabetes and most others (90–95%) have type 2 diabetes. There is a global epidemic of type 2 diabetes, and the economic burden is significant. The International Diabetes Federation estimated that 537 million adults between 20 to 79 years have diabetes worldwide in 2021 and that this number will increase to 738 million by 2045. A third group designated as “other specific types” by the American Diabetes Association (ADA) ([Table 32–1](#papacmdt26_ch32tb1)) numbers in the thousands ([https://www.cdc.gov/diabetes/php/data-research/](https://www.cdc.gov/diabetes/php/data-research/)).
