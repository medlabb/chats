## Answer

 A maternal Hb of **6 g/dL at delivery does not by itself establish that the baby will have complications**. The neonatal implications depend on the cause of the maternal anemia and the baby’s own hemoglobin, bilirubin, blood group, and antibody testing. The selected references note that the degree of anemia at birth can correlate with maternal hemoglobin, but they do not provide a specific follow-up schedule for an otherwise normal baby of an anemic mother. (1)

If the baby has **hemolytic disease of the fetus/newborn (HDFN)**—for example, due to Rh, ABO, or another blood-group incompatibility—possible complications include:

- **Early jaundice/hyperbilirubinemia**, usually within the first 24 hours; severe bilirubin elevation can cause kernicterus. (2)  
- **Anemia appearing or worsening at 4–6 weeks**, sometimes presenting as poor feeding or poor growth. (3)  
- **Late hyporegenerative anemia**, with a low reticulocyte count; ongoing hemolysis is associated with reticulocytosis and increased bilirubin. (3)  
- In affected infants, serial **hemoglobin, reticulocyte count, and bilirubin** monitoring is recommended under specialist hematology supervision; some infants may require iron, erythropoietin, or further red-cell transfusions. (3)

A normal examination is reassuring, but the references do not state that it excludes later anemia. Discuss with the baby’s pediatrician whether neonatal hemoglobin, bilirubin, reticulocyte count, blood group, and antibody testing were performed, and arrange follow-up—particularly around **4–6 weeks** if HDFN or neonatal anemia was suspected. The selected references do not provide further information about consequences specifically from maternal iron-deficiency anemia or the maternal blood transfusion.
