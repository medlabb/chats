The ADA approach for people with diabetes is briefly outlined here:
- **Primary prevention**– The ADA advises the following regardingaspirinuse for the primary prevention of cardiovascular disease:

For patients at low risk of ASCVD (10 year risk <5%) and for those ≥70 years old, bleeding risk typically outweighs potential benefit, and use is not advised.

For those with elevated ASCVD risk,**consideration**ofaspirinuse after a comprehensive individualized discussion regarding risks and benefits is advised.

- Aspirinuse for primary prevention of cardiovascular disease requires a careful review and discussion of the risks and benefits for individual patients. In most cases, the benefits of reduced cardiovascular risk are closely matched with the harms from increased bleeding risk. However, the potential benefit of aspirin increases with higher absolute risk of ASCVD, and use may be appropriate for those with elevated ASCVD at low risk for bleeding.

- In the ASCEND trial, 15,480 patients with diabetes (94 percent with type 2 diabetes) but without established ASCVD were randomly assigned toaspirin(100 mg daily) or placebo [62]. After a mean follow-up of 7.4 years, serious vascular events (a composite of MI, stroke [excluding intracranial hemorrhage], transient ischemic attack, or death from any vascular cause [except intracranial hemorrhage]) occurred in a smaller proportion of patients in the aspirin group (8.5 versus 9.6 percent, rate ratio 0.88, 95% CI 0.79-0.97). Aspirin did not significantly reduce the risk of any of the individual endpoints. However, the benefits of aspirin in reducing serious vascular events were offset by an approximate 1 percent absolute increased risk of bleeding, largely gastrointestinal and extracranial.

- **Secondary prevention**— The ADA recommendsaspirin(75 to 162 mg/day) for secondary prevention in patients with diabetes and known ASCVD, which includes a history of MI, vascular bypass, stroke or transient ischemic attack, peripheral vascular disease, claudication, or angina [20]. They advise dual antiplatelet therapy (eg, low-dose aspirin plus a P2Y12 inhibitor) after an acute coronary syndrome or coronary revascularization procedure with stenting and state that additional indications for dual antiplatelet therapy (eg, ischemic stroke) and the duration of treatment should be determined on an individualized basis. They also advise considering combination therapy with aspirin plus low-doserivaroxabanfor patients with stable coronary and/or peripheral artery disease and low bleeding risk to prevent major adverse limb and cardiovascular events. They adviseclopidogrel(75 mg/day) for patients with ASCVD and documented aspirin allergy.

Warnings/Precautions*Disease-related concerns:*
• Bariatric surgery: ­­Presurgical assessment of the indication for use, symptoms, and goals of therapy should be documented to enable postsurgical assessment. Aspirin use following bariatric surgery is controversial; while efficacy may improve, the risk of adverse effects may increase. A small pharmacokinetic study showed faster absorption, increased peak concentration, and increased exposure of aspirin after bariatric surgery, although not clinically significant at low doses (eg, 81 mg) (Mitrov-Winkelmolen 2016). The risk of marginal ulcers is increased with high-dose aspirin (Kang 2017; Portela 2023; Rodrigo 2020; Sverden 2016), but due to increased gastric pH following bariatric surgery, enteric-coated aspirin dissolves in the stomach, inactivating its protective coating (Porat 2021). However, aspirin-induced platelet inhibition improves post bariatric surgery, possibly due to weight loss–associated restoration of platelet inhibition(Norgard 2017). Monitor for efficacy and toxicity after bariatric surgery and consider switching to an alternative formulation or medication if symptoms worsen.
• Bleeding disorders: Use with caution in patients with platelet and bleeding disorders.
• Dehydration: Use with caution in patients with dehydration.
• Ethanol use: Heavy ethanol use (>3 drinks/day) can increase bleeding risks.
• Gastrointestinal disease: Use with caution in patients with erosive gastritis. Avoid use in patients with active peptic ulcer disease.
• Hepatic impairment: Avoid use in severe hepatic failure.
• Renal impairment: Use high dosages (eg, analgesic or anti-inflammatory uses) with caution (NKF [Henrich 1996]; Whelton 2000). Low-dose aspirin (eg, 75 to 162 mg daily) may be safely used in patients with any degree of renal impairment (KDOQI 2005; KDOQI 2007).
*Concurrent drug therapy issues:*
• Thrombolytics: In the treatment of acute ischemic stroke, avoid aspirin for 24 hours following administration of a thrombolytic; administration within 24 hours increases the risk of hemorrhagic transformation (AHA/ASA [Powers 2019]; Jauch 2013).
