**Dose reduction after adequate diuresis**– Patients with an adequate clinical response can continue their current dose of diuretics with periodic monitoring until adequate diuresis is achieved (eg, minimal ascites and edema). (See'Patient monitoring'above.)

Once adequate diuresis is achieved, a dose reduction can be attempted to minimize side effects [3]. Patients with a component of alcohol injury to their liver disease may become less sodium avid with alcohol abstinence due to healing of the reversible component of their liver injury. Some may be able to be tapered off diuretics, and others may revert from diuretic resistance to diuretic sensitivity. Clues that the patient may no longer need diuretics include orthostatic symptoms, azotemia, and the absence of ascites and edema. Once patients lose their sodium avidity, we begin tapering the diuretics, reducing to a final dose of 50 mg ofspironolactoneplus 20 mg offurosemidebefore discontinuing altogether.

- **Adjustments for adverse effects**— We reduce the dose of diuretics (or discontinue them entirely if the patient is taking only a low dose) if:

Serum sodium decreases by ≥10 mEq/L or to <125 mEq/L.

Potassium is <3 or >6 mEq/L despite appropriate measures (ie, adjusting ratio ofspironolactonetofurosemide, potassium supplement for hypokalemia).

Serum creatinine increases by >100 percent or to >2 mg/dL. In patients with poor kidney perfusion (elevated creatinine due in the setting of low blood pressure), the addition ofmidodrinemay allow for the reinstitution of diuretics. (See'Low blood pressure'above.)

Encephalopathy develops with no other inciting factor.

- These criteria are generally in keeping with the International Ascites Club definition of refractory ascites [48].

- It is important to avoid hypokalemia in patients with cirrhosis because potassium depletion can enhance renal ammonia production and possibly precipitate hepatic coma [49]. A transcellular cation exchange, in which potassium moves out of cells (to partially replete extracellular potassium stores) in exchange for hydrogen, is thought to be responsible for this effect, since the ensuing intracellular acidosis stimulates ammonia synthesis in the proximal tubular cells, which can contribute to hyperammonemia.

#### Diagnosing diuretic resistance
Dietary sodium restriction and diuretic therapy is initially effective in approximately 90 percent of patients with cirrhosis and ascites [50]. However, in patients with no reversible component to their liver injury, the ascites usually worsens over time and progresses from being diuretic-sensitive to being diuretic-resistant. The diagnosis of diuretic resistance is discussed in detail separately. (See"Ascites in adults with cirrhosis: Refractory ascites", section on 'Diagnosis'.)

### Follow up

#### Patient monitoring

- **Hospitalized patients**– We monitor weights, electrolytes, and kidney function daily in hospitalized patients being treated for ascites. This enables the clinician to titrate the dose up more rapidly than is usually feasible in outpatients and to respond promptly if adverse effects (ie, decreased kidney function or abnormal potassium levels) develop.

- For patients who are admitted to the hospital for the treatment of ascites, we schedule the first clinic visit for one to two weeks following discharge. In our experience, patients sent home with an appointment far in the future are regularly readmitted with worsening fluid overload, overdiuresis, or electrolyte imbalances before they are due back in the clinic. If distant travel is a problem, telemedicine can permit adjustment of diuretic doses based upon weights, faxed laboratory results, and symptoms [42]. A study in heart failure showed that a seven-day clinic return helped prevent readmission within 30 days of discharge [43].

- **Outpatients**— For outpatients, we check electrolytes and kidney function to evaluate for adverse effects 7 to 10 days after starting diuretics or increasing the dose of diuretics. We recommend that patients weigh themselves at home daily while the diuretic dose is being titrated.

- We typically monitor outpatients on stable diuretic doses at least every three months.

#### Dose adjustment
We adjust diuretic doses based on a patient’s clinical response as well as any adverse effects.
- **Titrate to clinical response**— The goals of therapy in patients with ascites are to minimize ascitic fluid volume and decrease peripheral edema, without causing intravascular volume depletion.

**Finding an effective dose**– Weight loss of at least 0.8 over four days is considered an adequate clinical response [44]. To reduce the risk of adverse effects, diuretic-induced weight loss should not exceed 0.5 kg/day in patients without edema and 1 kg/day in patients with edema [3].

If a clinical response is not evident after three to five days at a given dose, or if the degree of weight loss is less than desired, the doses ofspironolactoneandfurosemidecan be increased by 100 mg and 40 mg, respectively. This can be repeated if needed. The maximum recommended doses are spironolactone 400 mg per day and furosemide 160 mg per day [2,3]. When ascites does not respond adequately to the standard regimen, we try an alternative loop diuretic (torsemideorbumetanide) before concluding that the ascites is diuretic-resistant. We prefer torsemide due to its longer half-life. Torsemide 20 mg or bumetanide 1 mg are equivalent to furosemide 40 mg (all given by mouth) [45].

We monitor for adverse effects during dose escalation and reduce the dose or discontinue the diuretics if specific criteria are met (see below).

We avoid diuresing too rapidly (eg, beyond the above weight loss parameters) because attempting to remove fluid too quickly increases the risk of adverse effects. When a diuresis is induced, the fluid is initially lost from the vascular space; the ensuing fall in intravascular pressure allows the edema fluid to be mobilized to replete the plasma volume. Fluid mobilization can be rapid in patients with peripheral edema. By comparison, patients who only have ascites without edema can mobilize ascitic fluid solely via the peritoneum.
