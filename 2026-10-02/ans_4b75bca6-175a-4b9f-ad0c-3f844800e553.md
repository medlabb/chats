## Answer

 **Guillain–Barré syndrome (GBS)** is an acute or subacute disorder of peripheral nerves. It can follow an infection, an inoculation, or surgery, though sometimes no trigger is apparent. (1)

It commonly causes **progressive, relatively symmetric weakness**, often starting in the legs and moving upward, with reduced or absent reflexes. Tingling or other sensory symptoms may occur. Weakness affecting swallowing or breathing can be life-threatening; GBS can also cause problems with heart rate, blood pressure, and other automatic body functions. (1, 2)

**Diagnosis** is based on the clinical pattern and supporting tests. The diagnostic criteria in **Table 10-3** list progressive weakness in more than one limb and reduced or absent reflexes as required features. Cerebrospinal fluid may show increased protein with a normal cell count, although this may not appear in the first week; nerve-conduction studies may show slowed signals or other abnormalities. (2)

**Treatment** includes plasmapheresis or intravenous immunoglobulin; the source describes these as equally effective and says they are not additive. Severe cases need close monitoring, including respiratory monitoring, and may require intensive care and assisted breathing. (2, 3)

GBS often stops progressing by about four weeks, with improvement over weeks or months. The cited source reports complete recovery in approximately 70% of patients, mild lasting deficits in 25%, and death in 5%, usually from respiratory failure. (3)
