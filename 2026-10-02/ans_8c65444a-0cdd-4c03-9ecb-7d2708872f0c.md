## Answer

 Treatment depends on **how quickly hyponatremia developed, symptom severity, and the underlying cause**. Severe or symptomatic cases, and cases requiring aggressive treatment such as hypertonic saline, generally require hospital admission for close monitoring. (1) (2)

- **Severe symptoms** such as seizures or confusion: Give **3% hypertonic saline** emergently. A sodium increase of 4–5 mEq/L is generally sufficient to improve severe neurologic symptoms; the reference describes 100 mL boluses over 10 minutes, repeatable twice if needed. For less severe symptoms, it describes an infusion of 3% saline at 0.5–2 mL/kg/hour. Check laboratory values frequently—every 1–2 hours during treatment. (3)
- **Chronic hyponatremia:** Fluid restriction below urinary output is a usual starting approach, though it may be only minimally effective. Other options described include loop diuretics, oral urea, SGLT-2 inhibitors, and vasopressin receptor antagonists; vaptans carry risks including liver toxicity and sodium overcorrection. (rf_9b)
- **Avoid overly rapid correction:** Consensus guidance cited in the reference recommends not exceeding 8 mEq/L in 24 hours; this is a limit, not a target. For people at high risk of osmotic demyelination, a lower goal of 4–6 mEq/L per day is appropriate. A desmopressin “clamp” may be considered in selected high-risk situations. (4)
- **Treat the cause:** Pseudohyponatremia from hypertriglyceridemia or hyperproteinemia requires no treatment beyond laboratory confirmation; hyperglycemia-related hypertonic hyponatremia can be managed by correcting glucose. Hypovolemic hyponatremia requires fluid resuscitation, undertaken carefully because sodium may rise too quickly as the kidneys excrete water. (5)

Nephrology consultation is recommended for uncertain, refractory, or complicated cases, and for aggressive therapies such as hypertonic saline. (rf_e5)
