### A. Symptomatic Hyponatremia

If a patient has hyponatremia and severe symptoms (eg, seizures, confusion), regardless of etiology, then emergent treatment with hypertonic saline should commence. A relatively small increase in serum [sodium](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659912) of 4–5 mEq/L is generally sufficient to promptly reverse severe neurologic symptoms and decrease intracranial pressure. This can be accomplished most effectively by using boluses of 100 mL of 3% NaCl over 10 minutes, which can be repeated twice if needed. Each bolus might be predicted to raise the serum [sodium](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659912) by 1–2 mEq/L. In patients with less severe symptoms, 3% NaCl may be infused intravenously at a rate of 0.5–2 mL/kg/hour.

*The importance of frequent laboratory monitoring—every 1–2 hours—during treatment of hyponatremia cannot be overemphasized.*

### D. Hypotonic Hyponatremia

In addition to the considerations discussed above, patients with **hypovolemic hyponatremia** require fluid resuscitation. There is no clear consensus regarding the optimal fluid for resuscitation, due to conflicting data, between normal saline and balanced solutions such as [Ringer](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660196) [lactate](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659912) or Plasma-lyte. The correction of the volume depletion removes the stimulus for ADH and permits renal excretion of a dilute urine. The fluid resuscitation should be undertaken carefully because rapid renal excretion of the excess water may correct the serum [sodium](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659912) level too quickly.
