## Answer

 **Aortic regurgitation (AR)** is leakage of blood back into the left ventricle during diastole. It may result from **aortic valve disease**, **aortic root disease**, or both. Causes include bicuspid aortic valve, endocarditis, rheumatic disease, and aortic root dilation associated with conditions such as hypertension, aortic dissection, or Marfan syndrome. (1) (2)

**Effects and symptoms:** Chronic AR increases the volume load on the left ventricle, which can enlarge and thicken to compensate. Eventually, ventricular function and forward blood flow may decline; ventricular dysfunction can develop before symptoms appear. (3)

**Examination:** Chronic AR typically causes a high-pitched, blowing, decrescendo diastolic murmur, best heard along the left sternal border with the patient leaning forward at end expiration. Severe chronic AR may also cause a low-pitched apical **Austin Flint murmur**. In acute severe AR, the murmur may be short and soft. (4) (5)

**Assessment:** Echocardiography evaluates valve structure, the regurgitant jet, and left-ventricular size and function. Assessment may include color Doppler jet width, vena contracta, pressure half-time, and blood-flow reversal in the descending aorta. The aortic root and ascending aorta should also be measured and followed. (6)

**Treatment:** Acute severe AR generally requires urgent surgery; stabilization with intravenous diuretics and vasodilators may be short-lived. In chronic AR, surgery is indicated when symptoms or left-ventricular systolic dysfunction develop. Patients not meeting intervention criteria should have periodic clinical and echocardiographic follow-up. Vasodilators may help temporarily, and hypertension should be treated. ([Fig. 273-2](#hpim22_ch273fg2)) (7)

The references also describe increased risk when the left ventricle dilates beyond an end-systolic diameter of **5.5 cm** or the ejection fraction falls below normal. (6)
