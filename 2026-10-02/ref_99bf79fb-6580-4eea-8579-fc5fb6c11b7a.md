## Diabetes mellitus
The first step is to diagnose diabetes; the second step is to differentiate T2DM from other types of diabetes. (See'Type 2 versus type 1 diabetes'below and'Other causes of diabetes'below.)
Diabetes mellitus is diagnosed based upon any one of following  (table 4) [77,78]: (See'Laboratory tests'above.)
- A1C ≥6.5 percent (48 mmol/mol)
- FPG ≥126 mg/dL (7 mmol/L)
- Random plasma glucose ≥200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L) in a patient with classic symptoms of hyperglycemia
- OGTT with plasma glucose ≥200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L) two hours after a glucose load

Unless unequivocal symptomatic hyperglycemia is present, the diagnosis should be confirmed by repeat testing on a different day. Hyperglycemia that is associated with acute stress (eg, acute infection or surgery) is not diagnostic of diabetes; it should be treated but may be transient. These diagnostic criteria are the same as those used in adults. (See"Clinical presentation, diagnosis, and initial evaluation of diabetes mellitus in adults".)
Glucosuria is suggestive of diabetes but not diagnostic. For example, patients with renal glucosuria or Fanconi syndrome present with glycosuria but will be normoglycemic.
Severe hyperglycemia (plasma glucose ≥600 mg/dL [33 mmol/L]) suggests the possibility of hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state (HHS), which is a medical emergency. (See'Hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state'above.)

# SUMMARY AND RECOMMENDATIONS

- **Comprehensive diabetes education**– All patients with newly diagnosed diabetes should participate in a comprehensive diabetes self-management education program, which includes instruction on nutrition and eating pattern, physical activity, optimizing metabolic control, and preventing complications. Weight reduction through diet, exercise, and behavioral modification can all be used to improve glycemic management, although the majority of patients with type 2 diabetes will require medication. (See'Diabetes education'above.)

- **Glycemic goals**– Target glycated hemoglobin (A1C) levels in patients with type 2 diabetes should be tailored to the individual, balancing the anticipated reduction in microvascular complications over time with the immediate risks of hypoglycemia and other adverse effects of therapy. A reasonable goal might be an A1C value of <7 percent (53.0 mmol/mol) (calculator 1) for most patients. Glycemic targets are generally set somewhat higher for older adults and for those with comorbidities or a limited life expectancy and little likelihood of benefit from intensive therapy. (See'Glycemic management'above and"Glycemic management and vascular complications in type 2 diabetes mellitus", section on 'Choosing a glycemic target'.)

- **Asymptomatic, not catabolic**–****The majority of patients with newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes are asymptomatic, without symptoms of catabolism (eg, without polyuria, polydipsia, or unintentional weight loss).

**Initial treatment with****metformin**– There are many pharmacologic options to treat diabetes  (table 1). In the absence of specific contraindications or strong indication(s) for another medication class, we suggestmetforminas initial therapy for most patients (Grade 2B). Although some guidelines and experts endorse the initial use of alternative agents as monotherapy or in combination with metformin, we prefer initiating a single agent (commonly metformin) and then sequentially adding additional glucose-lowering agents as needed. (See'Metformin'above and'Glycemic efficacy'above.)

We suggest initiatingmetforminat the time of diabetes diagnosis (Grade 2C), along with consultation for lifestyle intervention. However, for those patients who have clear and modifiable contributors to hyperglycemia and who are motivated to change them (eg, commitment to reduce consumption of sugar-sweetened beverages) or an A1C near target (ie, <7.5 percent), a three- to six-month trial of lifestyle modification prior to initiation of pharmacologic therapy is reasonable. (See'When to start'above.)

The dose ofmetforminshould be titrated to its maximally effective dose (usually 2000 mg per day in divided doses) over one to two months, as tolerated. Metformin should not be administered when estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) is <30 mL/min/1.73 m2or conditions otherwise predisposing to lactic acidosis are present. (See"Metformin in the treatment of adults with type 2 diabetes mellitus", section on 'Contraindications'.)

**Strong indication(s) for another medication class or contraindication to**metformin– For patients who cannot take metformin or have a strong indication for a different medication class, we choose an alternative glucose-lowering medication guided initially by patient comorbidities  (table 1). (See'Strong indication for another class or contraindication to metformin'above.)

# SUMMARY AND RECOMMENDATIONS

- **Evaluation**– All children and adolescents with presumed type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) require an evaluation to exclude type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) as the clinical presentations in this population have substantial overlap. (See'Evaluation'above.)

- **Acute glycemic complications**– Pediatric patients with T2DM who present with diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state (HHS), moderate to severe ketosis, or severe hyperglycemia require urgent inpatient management  (table 2andtable 3). (See'Acute glycemic complications'above.)

- **Education at T2DM diagnosis**– All patients with newly diagnosed T2DM and their caregivers should be instructed in the following:

**Blood glucose self-monitoring (BGM)**– This should be performed while fasting, at mealtimes, and two hours after dinner in patients using multiple daily insulin injection (MDI) regimens (ie, regimens including basal and rapid-acting mealtime insulin); when changing therapy or adjusting doses; and when hemoglobin A1c (A1C) is above target. Continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) is an alternative for patients using MDI regimens.

For most other patients, BGM may be performed twice daily, typically while fasting and two hours postmeal, either after the largest meal of the day or at the most convenient time for the patient. (See'Home glucose monitoring'above.)

**Glycemic targets**– For most children and adolescents with T2DM, it is reasonable to target a A1C below 6.5 percent unless the patient is at high risk of hypoglycemia. In this case, a higher target individualized to patient characteristics may be used. (See'Glycemic targets'above.)

- **Lifestyle modification for all patients**– For all children with T2DM, it is important to provide clear guidance on lifestyle modification in addition to pharmacologic therapy at the time of diagnosis. Lifestyle modification includes promoting healthy nutrition and physical activity for weight loss or maintenance. We also counsel on pregnancy prevention and substance use avoidance. (See'Lifestyle modification for all patients'above.)

- **Choice of agent for initial outpatient pharmacologic therapy**– The treatment regimen is chosen based on patient characteristics  (algorithm 1).

**Patients with acute glycemic complications (eg, DKA, HHS, or moderate to severe ketosis)**– Patients with these characteristics  (table 4) require initial treatment with insulin until acute complications resolve.

For outpatient management after resolution of acute complications, we suggest ongoing insulin therapy in addition tometformin(Grade 2C). Insulin induces more rapid resolution of hyperglycemia and prevents a return to a catabolic state. A typical regimen includes long-acting basal insulin and rapid-acting insulin at mealtimes and for correction of hyperglycemia. (See'Patients with acute glycemic complications'above.)

**Mixed features of T1DM and T2DM**– For outpatient management of patients with mixed features of T1DM/T2DM (eg, young age or one or more positive pancreatic autoantibody with most features of T2DM), we suggest insulin in addition tometforminrather than either agent alone (Grade 2C).

Metforminmay be added after the acute metabolic complications have resolved and blood glucose values have returned to normal or near-normal.
