Alternative to this fixed-dose schedule, another option, especially for those with mild to moderate withdrawal, is to adopt a symptom-triggered approach. The gold-standard tool to measure alcohol withdrawal severity is the Clinical Institute Withdrawal Assessment for Alcohol-Revised (CIWA), which can be administered to arrive at a score. Above a certain cut-off, typically 8, medications are administered but withheld if scoring below this cut-off. Studies have shown that a symptom-triggered approach can still treat withdrawal adequately but reduces the duration of treatment and the overall need for medications without increasing the risk of progression to severe withdrawal.

Severe Withdrawal. For the less than 3% of AUD patients with extreme autonomic dysfunction, agitation, and confusion (alcoholic withdrawal delirium or delirium tremens [DTs]), there is no perfect treatment. DTs represent a medical emergency and will usually require treatment in intensive care. The first important step is to determine why such severe withdrawal symptoms occurred, especially whether there is a concomitant medical problem that needs immediate treatment. The withdrawal symptoms in DTs can then be minimized with benzodiazepines (high doses are sometimes required), along with the adjunctive use of an antipsychotic (e.g., haloperidol [Haldol]) to control severe agitation and hallucinations. If the patient does not respond to benzodiazepines, a clinician experienced in treating withdrawal delirium in an ICU can consider inducing a state of arousable deep sleep with propofol or the α-2-receptor agonist dexmedetomidine. Here, higher doses are used on the first or second day to control behavior, and the patient is then typically weaned off medications by about the fifth day.

Another 1% of patients in alcohol withdrawal have a single grand mal convulsion, with a peak incidence on day 2. This requires a neurologic evaluation, but in the absence of an independent seizure disorder, a single seizure does not indicate using anticonvulsant drugs.

Protracted Withdrawal. Finally, regarding withdrawal, mild symptoms of anxiety, insomnia, and autonomic overactivity are likely to continue for 2 to 6 months following acute withdrawal. Although no pharmacologic treatment for this syndrome has been identified, a medication discussed below for the rehabilitation phase, acamprosate, might diminish some of these symptoms. It is important to warn patients that some sleep problems or feelings of nervousness might remain after acute withdrawal and consider efforts to help them feel more comfortable because these protracted withdrawal symptoms enhance the probability of relapse.

#### Rehabilitation

Rehabilitation includes three major components: (1) increasing and maintaining high levels of motivation for change; (2) helping patients readjust to a lifestyle that maximizes functioning and decreases the risk for drinking; and (3) relapse prevention. These steps often begin in the context of acute and protracted withdrawal syndromes and life crises.

#### Detoxification

Most people with an AUD have relatively mild, if any, withdrawal symptoms when they stop drinking. If the patient is in relatively good health and has a good social support system, the withdrawal syndrome associated with alcohol usually resembles a mild flu or bad hangover. The essential first step in detoxification is a thorough physical examination. In the absence of a serious medical disorder or a comorbid SUD, severe alcohol withdrawal is unlikely. The second step is to offer rest, nutrition, and oral multivitamins, including thiamine.

Mild or Moderate Withdrawal. Alcohol withdrawal develops because the brain, adapted to the presence of this drug, downregulates the inhibitory systems and cannot function adequately in the absence of alcohol. Many patients, especially young and healthy individuals, have no clinically significant withdrawal symptoms and can go directly into the rehabilitation phase of treatment. Others will benefit from medications, giving enough of a brain depressant on the first day to diminish symptoms and then weaning the patient off the medication over the course of acute withdrawal, usually over about 5 days. If the symptoms are mild and the person is in good health, this can be done as an outpatient with daily brief visits. Any depressant can work, but most clinicians choose benzodiazepines for their relatively low cost and higher safety. Adequate treatment can be given with either short-acting drugs, such as lorazepam, or long-acting medications such as chlordiazepoxide or diazepam.

An example is administering 25 mg of chlordiazepoxide by mouth three or four times a day on the first day, with a notation to skip a dose if the patient is sleepy. Up to two additional 25-mg doses during the first day can be used if tremor or autonomic dysfunction increases. The first day’s dose can be decreased by about 20% each subsequent day, with a resulting need for no medication after 4 or 5 days. However, the prior day’s dose should be restored if withdrawal symptoms increase and further decreases in doses delayed a day or two. When using a long-acting agent, such as chlordiazepoxide, the clinician must avoid producing excessive sedation; if the patient is sleepy, the next scheduled dose should be omitted. When using a short-acting drug, such as lorazepam, the patient must not miss any dose because rapid changes in benzodiazepine concentrations can precipitate a seizure. Other depressants, including anticonvulsants (e.g., gabapentin) can also be effective but have added dangers and costs compared to benzodiazepines. Some clinicians recommend β-adrenergic receptor antagonists (e.g., propranolol) or α-adrenergic receptor agonists (e.g., clonidine), although these medications do not decrease the risk of seizures or delirium and can mask the intensity of some withdrawal signs that are used to monitor withdrawal severity.

The standard treatment of alcohol withdrawal syndromes is with benzodiazepines, most frequently lorazepam, diazepam, chlordiazepoxide, or clorazepate. Some protocols use standardized protocols with a progressively decreasing dose, while others adjust each dose based on the response to the previous dose. Alcohol withdrawal syndromes refractory to benzodiazepines are often treated with the addition of phenobarbital, propofol, or dexmedetomidine. The latter medication is an α<sub>2</sub>-agonist that is more potent than clonidine, with an onset of action of 15 minutes and an elimination half-life of 2 hours, the antiadrenergic effect of which decreases sympathetic tone without causing respiratory depression.

A retrospective chart review comparing phenobarbital (*N* = 143) and benzodiazepines (*N* = 419) in treating alcohol withdrawal found equal outcomes in both, although 16 patients who did not respond to benzodiazepines did well when switched to phenobarbital. Amplifying this finding, a Cochrane review of 64 RCTs involving 4,309 subjects comparing benzodiazepines to placebo, other treatments, and each other in the treatment of alcohol withdrawal found benzodiazepines to be significantly superior to placebo for seizures and suggested superiority of benzodiazepines for many other outcomes, but the studies were too heterogeneous to draw definitive conclusions about comparative effectiveness and safety.

#### Agitation

Since the antimanic and antipsychotic effects of antipsychotic drugs are delayed, treatment of agitation associated with mania and schizophrenia with these medications relies on their sedative side effects. Adding or substituting a benzodiazepine has the advantage of rapid sedation without the risks of high or repeated doses of antipsychotics. In the treatment of mania, improvement of sleep with a benzodiazepine may contribute to a reduction of manic symptoms. The addition of a benzodiazepine to an antipsychotic drug may also ameliorate akathisia, which can be mistaken for agitation. IM lorazepam has been used most frequently for agitation in acutely ill psychiatric patients, but midazolam is also available in an IM formulation. High lipid solubility and potency and a very short elimination half-life make midazolam an appealing choice when a rapid onset of action without drug accumulation with repeated dosing is desired. Recent experience suggests that midazolam is rapidly effective and well tolerated in the prehospital treatment of agitation.

In one study, oral risperidone plus lorazepam was effective in the emergency treatment of acutely psychotic patients. Similarly, a multicenter controlled trial in acute psychosis reported that risperidone plus lorazepam more rapidly reduced aggression, produced more sedation, and was better tolerated than risperidone alone. Anticipating that an antipsychotic drug will be necessary anyway, most clinicians add a benzodiazepine as an adjunct. However, particularly in the treatment of mania, it may be reasonable to treat agitation and insomnia with a benzodiazepine with or without an antipsychotic drug and gradually transition to lithium or another mood stabilizing medication that does not have prominent sedative properties.
