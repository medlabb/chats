[Vasopressin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660177) (ADH) is often used as an *adjunctive therapy* to catecholamine vasopressors in the treatment of distributive shock. [Vasopressin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660177) causes peripheral vasoconstriction via V1 receptors located on smooth muscle cells and attenuation of nitric oxide (NO) synthesis and cGMP, the second messenger of NO. The rationale for using low-dose [vasopressin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660177) in the management of septic shock includes the relative deficiency of [vasopressin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660177) in late shock. [Vasopressin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660177) also potentiates the effects of catecholamines on the vasculature and stimulates cortisol production. Some studies have reported reduced catecholamine requirements with [vasopressin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660177) administration. Intravenous infusion of [vasopressin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660177) at a low dose (0.01–0.04 units/min) as a second agent to [norepinephrine](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660259) has been beneficial in patients with sepsis and hypotension refractory to fluid resuscitation and conventional catecholamine vasopressors. Higher doses of [vasopressin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660177) decrease cardiac output and may put patients at greater risk for splanchnic and coronary artery ischemia. Studies do not favor the use of [vasopressin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660177) as first-line therapy. In the [Vasopressin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660177) and Septic Shock Trial (VASST), low doses of [vasopressin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660177) did not reduce mortality compared with [norepinephrine](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660259) in patients with septic shock who were being treated with catecholamine vasopressors.

Angiotensin II, a component of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system axis, is a potent direct vasoconstrictor that acts on the arteries and veins to increase blood pressure. The ATHOS-3 trial demonstrated that angiotensin II could increase blood pressure in patients with vasodilatory shock not responsive to high doses of conventional vasopressors. Angiotensin II (Giapreza) is an *additional agent* for vasodilatory shock that is refractory to catecholamines and [vasopressin.](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660177) In the postmarketing setting, angiotensin II was administered to patients with shock that was nonresponsive to vasopressors. More than two-thirds of patients were hemodynamically responsive to angiotensin II, displaying a > 10 mm Hg increase in MAP and a reduction in vasopressor dose at 3 hours compared to nonresponders. The recommended starting dose is 20 ng/kg/min via continuous intravenous infusion through a CVC. It can be titrated every 5 minutes by increments of up to 15 ng/kg/min as needed to achieve MAP goals, but not to exceed 80 ng/kg/min during the first 3 hours of use. Maintenance doses should not exceed 40 ng/kg/min. Concurrent VTE prophylaxis is indicated as studies revealed a higher incidence of VTE with angiotensin II use.

##### B. CARDIOGENIC SHOCK

Given meta-analyses documenting decreased mortality, [norepinephrine](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660259) should be the first-line vasopressor for cardiogenic shock (see doses in [Distributive (Vasodilatory) Shock](#papacmdt26_ch14lev5sec4), above). [Dobutamine](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660311), a predominantly β-adrenergic agonist, increases contractility and decreases afterload. It is used for patients with low cardiac output and high PCWP but who do not have hypotension. [Dobutamine](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660311) can be added to a vasopressor if there is reduced myocardial function (decreased cardiac output and elevated PCWP), or if there are signs of hypoperfusion despite adequate volume resuscitation and an adequate MAP. The initial dose is 0.1–0.5 mcg/kg/min intravenous infusion, which can be titrated every few minutes to hemodynamic effect; the usual dosage range is 2–20 mcg/kg/min intravenously. Tachyphylaxis can occur after 48 hours from the downregulation of β-adrenergic receptors. [Milrinone](/drug.aspx?gbosId=658874) is a phosphodiesterase inhibitor that can be substituted for [dobutamine](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660311). A 2021 study of patients with cardiogenic shock found no significant difference in mortality when comparing [milrinone](/drug.aspx?gbosId=658874) to [dobutamine.](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660311) Amrinone is another phosphodiesterase inhibitor that can be used. These phosphodiesterase inhibitor drugs increase cyclic AMP levels and increase cardiac contractility, bypassing the β-adrenergic receptor. Vasodilation is a side effect of both amrinone and [milrinone](/drug.aspx?gbosId=658874).

### C. Myocardial Dysfunction

The severity of cardiac dysfunction is proportionate to the extent of myocardial necrosis but is exacerbated by preexisting dysfunction and ongoing ischemia. Persons with hypotension not responsive to fluid resuscitation or refractory HF or cardiogenic shock should be considered for urgent echocardiography to assess left and right ventricular function and for mechanical complications, right heart catheterization, and continuous measurements of arterial pressure. These measurements permit the accurate assessment of volume status and may facilitate decisions about volume resuscitation, selective use of vasopressors and inotropes, and mechanical support. [eTable 10–2](#papacmdt26_ch10etb2) categorizes patients based on these hemodynamic findings.

#### 1. Acute LV failure

Dyspnea, diffuse rales, and arterial hypoxemia usually indicate LV failure. General measures include supplemental oxygen to increase arterial saturation to above 95% and elevation of the trunk. Diuretics are usually the initial therapy unless RV infarction is present. Intravenous [furosemide](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660308) (10–40 mg) or [bumetanide](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660391) (0.5–1 mg) is preferred because of the reliably rapid onset and short duration of action of these medications. Higher dosages can be given if an inadequate response occurs. [Morphine](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659925) [sulfate](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659912) (4 mg intravenously followed by increments of 2 mg) is valuable in acute pulmonary edema.

Diuretics are usually effective; however, because most patients with acute infarction are not volume overloaded, the hemodynamic response may be limited and may be associated with hypotension. Vasodilators will reduce PCWP and improve cardiac output by a combination of venodilation (increasing venous capacitance) and arteriolar dilation (reducing afterload and LV wall stress). In mild HF, sublingual isosorbide dinitrate (2.5–10 mg every 2 hours) or [nitroglycerin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659177) ointment (6.25–25 mg every 4 hours) may be adequate to lower pulmonary capillary wedge pressure (PCWP). In more severe failure, especially if cardiac output is reduced and BP is normal or high, [sodium](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659912) nitroprusside may be the preferred agent. It should be initiated only with arterial pressure monitoring; the initial dosage should be low (0.25 mcg/kg/min) to avoid excessive hypotension, but the dosage can be increased by increments of 0.5 mcg/kg/min every 5–10 minutes up to 5–10 mcg/kg/min until the desired hemodynamic response is obtained. Excessive hypotension (mean BP <65–75 mm Hg) or tachycardia (> 10/min increase) should be avoided.

Intravenous [nitroglycerin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659177) (starting at 10 mcg/min) also may be effective but may lower PCWP with less hypotension. Oral or transdermal vasodilator therapy with nitrates or ACE inhibitors is often necessary after the initial 24–48 hours.

Inotropic agents should be avoided if possible, because they often increase heart rate and myocardial oxygen requirements and worsen clinical outcomes. [Dobutamine](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660311) has the best hemodynamic profile, increasing cardiac output and modestly lowering PCWP, usually without excessive tachycardia, hypotension, or arrhythmias. The initial dosage is 2.5 mcg/kg/min, and it may be increased by similar increments up to 15–20 mcg/kg/min at intervals of 5–10 minutes. [Dopamine](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660137) is more useful in the presence of hypotension, since it produces peripheral vasoconstriction, but it has a less beneficial effect on PCWP. [Digoxin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660024) has not been helpful in acute infarction except to control the ventricular response in atrial fibrillation, but it may be beneficial if chronic HF persists.

Stress imaging is positive in about 75–90% of patients with anatomically significant coronary disease and in 20–30% of those without it. Occasionally, other conditions, including infiltrative diseases (sarcoidosis, amyloidosis), left bundle branch block, and dilated cardiomyopathy, may produce resting or persistent perfusion defects. False-positive radionuclide tests may occur as a result of diaphragmatic attenuation or, in women, attenuation through breast tissue. Tomographic imaging (single-photon emission CT [SPECT]) can reduce the severity of artifacts. Gated imaging allows for analysis of ventricular size, EF, and regional wall motion.

#### 2. Radionuclide angiography

This procedure, also known as **multi-gated acquisition scan,** or **MUGA scan,** uses radionuclide tracers to image the LV and measures its EF and wall motion. In coronary disease, resting abnormalities usually represent infarction, and those that occur only with exercise usually indicate stress-induced ischemia. Exercise radionuclide angiography has approximately the same sensitivity as myocardial perfusion scintigraphy, but it is less specific in older individuals and those with other forms of heart disease. In addition, because of the precision around LVEF, the test is also used for monitoring patients exposed to cardiotoxic therapies (such as some chemotherapeutic agents).

#### 3. Stress echocardiography

Echocardiograms performed during supine exercise or immediately following upright exercise may demonstrate exercise-induced *segmental wall motion abnormalities* as an indicator of ischemia. In experienced laboratories, the test accuracy is comparable to that obtained with scintigraphy—though a higher proportion of tests is technically inadequate. While exercise is the preferred stress because of other information derived, pharmacologic stress with **high-dose [dobutamine](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660311)** (20–40 mcg/kg/min) can be used as an alternative to exercise.
