# Hypoglycemia



Neonatal hypoglycemia occurs in 5% to 15% of all neonates with the potential of harm in the form of brain injury, yet the thresholds in defining and treating neonatal hypoglycemia remain much debated. Additionally, the critical value of glucose concentration that causes long-term neurologic injury is unknown. 15 , 16 The statistical definition of hypoglycemia is a glucose concentration that falls below the 5th to 10th percentile in epidemiologic datasets generated for blood glucose concentrations from large numbers of infants. Two pediatric organizations, American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) and Pediatric Endocrine Society (PES), suggest different glucose value ranges using two different approaches. AAP recommends screening for hypoglycemia in all asymptomatic at-risk infants. These include late preterm infants 34−36+6/7 weeks’ gestation and term infants born to mothers with diabetes and small for gestational age or large for gestational age babies. The AAP algorithm recommends intervention at glucose concentrations between 25 and 40 mg/dL in the first 4 hours of life. Between 4 and 24 hours after birth, the lowest level requiring action is less than 45 mg/dL to ensure a margin of safety ( Fig. 90.3 ). Early initiation of feedings and use of an oral glucose gel, when indicated, is the first line of therapy toward maintaining the postnatal age-related glucose value in the acceptable range. PES 17 established mean glucose concentrations of 55 to 65 mg/dL in the first 48 hours of life based on ensuring safety using neuroendocrine and metabolic characteristics of transitional hyperinsulinemia which is accompanied by low ketones and limited glycogenolysis. By 72 hours of age glucose concentrations are to reach greater than 70 mg/dL (see Fig. 90.3 ).

The definition of hypoglycemia for preterm infants should not be any different from that for full-term infants. Additionally, hypoglycemia in the neonate should be described as transient or persistent, and in either or both cases, as symptomatic or asymptomatic. Such a description has implications for both clinical management and long-term consequences. Transient hypoglycemia implies low glucose values that resolve within 2 to 3 days of life. In contrast, persistent and recurrent hypoglycemia implies a form that requires prolonged management (glucose infusions for several days at high rates of infusion) and perhaps pharmacologic intervention. Several of these hypoglycemia syndromes may present during the newborn period only to continue throughout infancy and childhood.

The clinical manifestations of hypoglycemia are nonspecific and similar to those of many disorders in newborn infants ( Box 90.1 ). It is difficult to distinguish between symptomatic and asymptomatic infants, particularly when the symptoms are subtle and can be generic to many other conditions, not just hypoglycemia. The clinical signs andsymptoms of hypoglycemia should improve with correction of the low glucose concentration. In addition, careful attention should be paid in ensuring that other associated disorders (e.g., sepsis, asphyxia) are not overlooked.

Neuroglycopenia is known to cause seizures and permanent brain damage. 18 The critical glucose concentration at which there is an inadequate supply of glucose for the brain has been suggested to be less than 47 mg/dL. This limit was based on observations of altered somatosensory-evoked potentials and lower scores for subsequent mental and motor development when blood glucose concentrations presented below this threshold. However, many of the extensive observational cohort studies of hypoglycemic neonates do not support this observation. Using a lower than traditional blood glucose threshold of less than 36 mg/dL in 689 asymptomatic and otherwise healthy neonates born at or greater than 35 weeks of gestation and weighing greater than 2000 g failed to demonstrate any adverse outcomes at 18 months of age. 19 In contrast, another study from the University of Arkansas for Medical Sciences of 1400 10-year-old fourth graders with transient neonatal hypoglycemia (glucose <45 mg/dL) reported an association with decreased literacy and mathematics proficiency at fourth-grade achievement testing when compared to age- and size-matched controls. 20

## Temperature Instability and Hypoglycemia



Cold stress manifests as tachypnea, poor color secondary to peripheral vasoconstriction, altered pulmonary vascular tone, and metabolic acidosis. In a late preterm infant, this condition can worsen respiratory transition and exacerbate hypoglycemia, and these signs can be misinterpreted as something more ominous, such as sepsis, prompting unnecessary interventions and work-ups. Very few studies have looked at temperature instability in late preterm infants, but from experience, preterm infants are more likely to have hypothermia and cold stress. A survey of rectal temperatures among 196 term infants in a Dallas hospital found that nearly 28% were less than 36.5° C. 55 Similarly, admission temperatures at a Rhode Island hospital NICU in infants weighing more than 2 kg and of greater than 32 weeks’ gestation were reported to be between 34.5° C and 36.5° C. 5 Wang and colleagues 50 found that late preterm infants were more likely to present with temperature instability (10% vs. 0%; *P* <.0012), and another study found that among late preterm infants admitted to the NICU, hypothermia was listed as the primary reason for admission in almost 5.2% of this group. 52

Hypoglycemia is another component that can affect transition. The incidence of hypoglycemia in late preterm infants is three to four times greater than in term newborns, especially within the first 12 hours of life. 3 Severehypoglycemia is a well-known risk factor for neuronal cell death and adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes. In one study, neonatal hypoglycemia was associated with an increased risk of poor executive function and visual motor function at 4.5 years of age. 56 In 2011, the AAP Committee on Fetus and Newborn provided guidelines for screening and management of postnatal glucose homeostasis in late preterm infants, recognizing that they are an at-risk population along with term small-for-gestational-age (SGA) and infants of diabetic mothers (IDM)/large-for-gestational-age (LGA) infants ( Fig. 41.9 ). These infants should receive their initial feed within the first hour of life and have a screening glucose test 30 minutes after the first feed. The threshold for low blood glucose is less than 25 mg/dL in the first 4 hours of life, less than 35 mg/dL from 4 to 24 hours of life, and less than 40 mg/dL when the infant is symptomatic (irritability, tremors, jitteriness, exaggerated Moro reflex, high-pitched cry, seizures, lethargy, floppiness, cyanosis, apnea, poor feeding). The algorithm then provides guidelines for treatment of hypoglycemia. The Pediatric Endocrine Society recommends that high-risk infants without a suspected congenital disorder (which would include late preterm infants) should maintain a goal plasma glucose concentration greater than 50 mg/dL less than 48 hours and greater than 60 mg/dL greater than 48 hours to minimize any long-term effects. 57

One treatment identified for neonatal hypoglycemia is dextrose gel. This is a 40% dextrose gel that is massaged into the infant’s buccal mucosa, usually followed by oral breast milk or formula feeding. The Sugar Babies Study, a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial, showed that treatment with dextrose gel is inexpensive and easy to administer in the newborn nursery and suggested that it should be considered first-line treatment for late preterm and term hypoglycemic infants in the first 24 hours after birth. 58 Subsequent studies have supported its use, showing the decreased need for intravenous fluids and decreased admissions to the NICU. 59 Buccal administration of dextrose gel is not included in the current AAP hypoglycemia guideline; however, with more recent studies supporting its use, manyhospitals are establishing their own algorithms for the use of dextrose gel in the setting of neonatal hypoglycemia.

### Hypoglycemia



Hypoglycemia is most frequent in small newborns, most of whom are small for gestational age, and in infants of mothers who are diabetic or prediabetic. Hypoglycemia is thought to be responsible for approximately 3% of neonatal seizures, although the incidence has been falling with improved neonatal care. The most critical determinants for the occurrence of neurologic symptoms in neonatal hypoglycemia are the duration of the hypoglycemia and, as a corollary, the amount of time elapsed before treatment is begun. Neurologic symptoms consist most commonly of jitteriness, stupor, hypotonia, apnea, and seizures. The onset is usually the second postnatal day. Hypoglycemia significant enough to trigger seizures is often associated with adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes. 72
