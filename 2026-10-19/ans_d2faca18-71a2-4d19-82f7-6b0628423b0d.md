## Answer

 ## Progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy (PML)

### Epidemiology

PML is a **rare, often fatal demyelinating disease of the central nervous system** caused by reactivation of the **JC polyomavirus (JCV)**. It occurs almost exclusively in people with impaired cellular or other significant immunosuppression. (1; 2; 3)

It is seen particularly in patients with:

- **HIV infection**, especially advanced HIV
- **Lymphoproliferative or myeloproliferative diseases**
- **Hematologic malignancies**
- **Primary immunodeficiency disorders**
- Immunosuppression after **solid-organ transplantation**
- Treatment with certain immunomodulatory or lymphocyte-targeted agents, including **natalizumab** (1; 2; 3)

### Clinical manifestations

Classic PML is a **progressive, multifocal white-matter disease**. It usually presents subacutely with progressive focal neurologic deficits, including:

- **Altered mental status and cognitive impairment**
- **Motor deficits**, such as hemiparesis or monoparesis
- **Visual symptoms**, including hemianopia and diplopia
- **Limb or gait ataxia**
- Language or cortical-appearing symptoms, such as aphasia or apparent cortical blindness, due to involvement of underlying white-matter tracts (4; 1; 5)

PML often begins in the cerebral hemispheres and spreads to other regions. The optic nerves and spinal cord are typically spared, although rare spinal cord involvement has been reported. (4)

The disease is generally progressive and may lead to severe disability and death. Seizures have been reported; the summary reports occurrence in up to **18 percent** of patients, while another clinical description reports up to **44 percent among survivors**, particularly when lesions are adjacent to the cortex. (1; 4)

Other JCV-associated clinical syndromes include:

- **JCV granule cell neuronopathy**: a pure cerebellar syndrome
- **JCV encephalopathy**: a cortical syndrome with encephalopathy
- **JCV meningitis** (1)

### Neuroimaging findings

The typical MRI pattern consists of **discrete unilateral or bilateral white-matter demyelinating lesions** that:

- Do not conform to vascular territories
- Produce little or no mass effect
- Usually do not enhance with contrast

Lesions commonly involve the subcortical white matter of the parieto-occipital or frontal lobes but may also involve the corpus callosum, brainstem, pyramidal tracts, and cerebellum. (6; 7)

On MRI, lesions are typically hypointense on T1-weighted images and hyperintense on T2-weighted and FLAIR sequences. Contrast enhancement is unusual in classic PML but may occur with **PML–immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (PML-IRIS)**. (6)

### Diagnosis

In a patient with compatible neurologic symptoms and neuroimaging findings:

1. Obtain **brain MRI with and without gadolinium**. (8)
2. Perform lumbar puncture for cerebrospinal fluid analysis, including **PCR testing for JCV DNA**. In the appropriate clinical and radiologic setting, CSF JCV PCR can usually establish the diagnosis. (1; 8)
3. If the initial CSF JCV PCR is negative, evaluate for alternative neurologic disorders and **repeat lumbar puncture and CSF JCV PCR**. (1; 8)
4. If repeat testing remains unrevealing and diagnostic concern persists—particularly if another condition such as CNS lymphoma is being considered—**brain biopsy may be pursued**. (1; 8)

The diagnostic approach also includes routine blood testing, including a complete blood count with differential and HIV serology and viral load when HIV status is unknown. The referenced diagnostic algorithm is identified as **algorithm 1**, and the approach after a negative initial JCV PCR as **algorithm 2**. (8)
