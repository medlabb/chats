The existence of a cannabis psychosis in Western cultures is still a matter for debate. In its favor are case series of cannabis psychoses and a small number of controlled studies that compare the characteristics of cannabis psychoses with those of psychoses in individuals who were not using cannabis at the time of hospital admission. Critics of the hypothesis emphasize the fallibility of clinical judgments about etiology, the poorly specified criteria for diagnosing cannabis psychoses, the dearth of controlled studies, and the striking variations in the clinical features of “cannabis psychoses.” Nevertheless, there is robust evidence base for cannabis-induced psychotic disorder, with cannabis being a substance that carries an elevated risk of causing this reaction compared to other substances.

#### Cannabis and Schizophrenia

Schizophrenia and regular cannabis use are associated in community surveys and clinical populations. A substantial number of longitudinal studies also suggest that regular cannabis use can precipitate schizophrenia in vulnerable individuals and exacerbate its symptoms in those who have already developed the disorder. For example, a follow-up of Swedish conscripts found that cannabis use reported in adolescence predicted an increased likelihood of receiving a diagnosis of schizophrenia over a 35-year follow-up. The Swedish findings have been supported by prospective studies in Australia, France, Germany, the Netherlands, and New Zealand that have all found associations between self-reported cannabis use in young adults and later reports of psychotic symptoms. A meta-analysis of these studies provided strong support for the hypothesis that regular cannabis use, especially early-onset cannabis use and use of cannabis with a high THC content, can precipitate schizophrenia.

Meta-analyses of these studies have found that those who had ever used cannabis had 1.4 times higher odds of psychotic symptoms. The odds increased 2.1 times among regular cannabis users and were nearly four times higher in persons who used more potent forms of cannabis. Young people with psychoses or psychotic symptoms who use cannabis have an average age of first episode 2 years earlier than those who did not use cannabis. People with a first episode of psychosis who stop using cannabis have better clinical outcomes than those who persist in using it.

These findings suggest that cannabis use precipitates schizophreniform disorders in vulnerable individuals. It is less certain whether cannabis use can produce psychotic disorders that would not otherwise have occurred. Most of the 274 Swedish conscripts who developed schizophrenia had not used cannabis, and only 7% of cases of schizophrenia could be attributed to cannabis use. Trends in the *treated* incidence of schizophrenia, particularly of early onset, acute cases, during the 1970s and 1980s, when cannabis use increased among young adults in the United States and Australia, have been unclear. Recent studies in Britain and Switzerland have suggested that the incidence of psychoses may have increased in recent birth cohorts with high rates of cannabis use.

#### Substance Abuse

Substance abuse is, at present, by far the most common associated health problem among patients with schizophrenia and may involve any drug of abuse or a polydrug combination. The addictive use of cannabis, stimulants, and nicotine is disproportionately high among patients with schizophrenia and may be related to the underlying neurobiology of the disorder. Reasons for the comorbidity of substance use disorders and schizophrenia are unclear, and several theories have been proposed to explain this, including common genetic vulnerabilities and neural networks, cumulative risk and environmental stressors, or attempts at self-medication.

Alcohol and illicit drug use in schizophrenia patients are relevant clinically, as they worsen the overall course of the illness and increase morbidity and mortality. Affected individuals are more likely to get hospitalized or become homeless, violent, suicidal, or incarcerated. It is also linked with decreased treatment compliance and risk of relapse. In a systemic review and meta-analysis of 123 studies on substance use disorders in schizophrenia in community and clinical settings between 1990 and 2017, the overall prevalence was 0.417. The overall prevalence of alcohol use disorder was 0.243, with the prevalence in Asia being lower (0.077) than that in other countries. Illicit drug use disorder use in schizophrenia patients was 0.275 and higher in those with first-episode psychosis (0.362) compared to those with schizophrenia (0.197). The highest prevalence of illicit drug use disorder was found among patients in North America and Australia. The point estimate for prevalence for stimulant use disorder in schizophrenia patients was 0.073, while that for opioid use disorder was 0.051. Male patients also have a two to three times greater risk of substance use disorders than females.

About 3.9% of the global adult population uses cannabis, which is higher than all the other illicit substances combined. Cannabis use has particular relevance to schizophrenia as it has been directly linked to an increased risk of developing psychosis. Cannabis has been extensively reported to induce psychotic experiences (PEs), and active cannabis users report higher ratings of positive symptoms. Previous meta-analyses indicate that the risk of developing psychotic disorders is increased twofold in those with schizophrenia. A systematic meta-analysis of 10 selected studies involving 66,816 individuals showed that among the heaviest cannabis users, the risk of schizophrenia and other psychotic disorders was even higher (OR = 3.90). In addition to the degree of cannabis exposure, existing evidence indicates that the use of high-potency cannabis and early onset of use are stronger risk factors for developing psychotic disorders. A systematic review of published data on cannabis exposure and the onset of schizophrenia concluded that early use increased the risk of psychosis in a dose-related manner, especially in persons at high genetic risk of schizophrenia. The point estimate for prevalence for studies of subjects with current or lifetime CUD was 0.262. First-episode patients had higher prevalence rates (0.356) of CUD compared to chronic schizophrenia patients (0.208). The highest CUD prevalence in schizophrenia patients was found in the United States, Australia, and Western Europe. The lowest rates of CUD globally are seen in sub-Saharan Africa and North Africa/Middle East, and South Asia.

The hypothesis that cannabis use is a *contributory* cause of schizophrenia (one factor among others that increase the likelihood of developing the disorder) is more plausible than the alternative hypotheses that (a) persons with schizophrenia use cannabis as a form of self-medication or that (b) cannabis use is associated with other drug use (e.g., amphetamines) that are linked to schizophrenia or (c) of an increased genetic vulnerability to schizophrenia.

Epidemiologic studies have tested the self-medication hypothesis by measuring the temporal sequence between cannabis use and psychosis and only conducting analyses on data from those participants who reported no symptoms before the use of cannabis. Other studies have specifically recruited participants who reported no history of psychotic symptoms or statistically controlled for a prior history of symptoms of mental disorders. These studies report that cannabis use usually preceded the onset of schizophrenia symptoms, suggesting that cannabis use increases the risk of psychosis rather than psychosis increasing cannabis use. Prospective studies of outcomes in people with psychoses who used cannabis before diagnosis and discontinued their use afterward are inconsistent with the self-medication hypothesis. In systematic reviews, people with psychoses who discontinue cannabis use have much lower rates of relapse and fewer positive symptoms than those who continue to use cannabis.

A variety of studies have assessed genetic explanations of the association between cannabis use and schizophrenia. These include studies of the size of the association in cohorts of persons with varying degrees of genetic relationship (e.g., twins, parents, siblings, cousins, and unrelated), Mendelian randomization studies, and studies that have adjusted the association for polygenic risk scores for cannabis use and psychosis. These studies suggest that there are shared genetic risks for both cannabis use and psychosis and that emerging psychosis increases the risks of cannabis use, but there is also consistent evidence that cannabis use plays a small contributory causal role in the development of psychosis.

A contributory causal role for cannabis use is biologically plausible. Psychotic disorders involve disturbances in the dopamine neurotransmitter systems: Drugs that increase dopamine release produce psychotic symptoms when given in large doses (e.g., methamphetamine), and neuroleptic drugs that reduce psychotic symptoms also reduce dopamine levels. THC appears to increase dopamine release; hence it can potentially produce psychotic symptoms. Most directly, double-blind provocation studies indicate that THC produces dose-related increases in psychotic symptoms in persons who do and do not have schizophrenia.

#### Cannabis and Anxiety

Some cannabis users report acutely increased anxiety, panic, and a fear of going mad. These experiences are most often reported by naive users who are unfamiliar with the effects of the drug, including some patients given THC for medicinal reasons. More experienced recreational users may also report these effects if they use more potent forms of cannabis than usual or if they use the oral route, in which case they may find it difficult to titrate their cannabis dose and experience more pronounced effects of much longer duration than usual.

#### Intoxication

Most young people report using cannabis to experience a “high,” that is, mild euphoria, relaxation, and perceptual alterations, including time distortion, and the intensification of ordinary experiences, such as eating, watching films, listening to music, and engaging in sex. When used in a social setting, the high may be accompanied by infectious laughter, talkativeness, and increased sociability.

Cognitive changes include impaired short-term memory and attention, making it easy for the user to become lost in pleasant reverie and more difficult to engage in sustained goal-directed mental or physical activity. Motor skills, reaction time, motor coordination, and many forms of skilled psychomotor activity are impaired while the user is intoxicated, increasing the risks of accidents if users drive an automobile.

#### Intoxication Delirium

Psychotic symptoms, such as delusions and hallucinations, may occur at very high doses of THC and in lower doses in individuals with a vulnerability to psychosis, for example, a first-degree affected relative.

High doses of THC have been reported to produce visual and auditory hallucinations, delusional ideas, and thought disorders in normal volunteers.
