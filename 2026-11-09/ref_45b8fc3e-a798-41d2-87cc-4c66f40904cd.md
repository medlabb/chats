### NEUROLEPTIC MALIGNANT SYNDROME

#### Clinical Presentation

The core symptoms of NMS include generalized “lead pipe” *muscle rigidity* and an *elevated temperature* which may range from modest to severe (e.g., 106 °F). *Altered consciousness* and *autonomic system dysregulation* are also common characteristics. These complications typically occur within days after exposure to an antipsychotic. NMS, however, can vary in terms of symptom presentation (e.g., core symptoms are not always present with certain SGAs); progression (e.g., rapid to delayed onset); and outcome (e.g., full recovery to death, particularly in unrecognized cases).

NMS can exhibit a rapid or gradual onset of the following symptoms:

- Autonomic instability:

  - Diaphoresis

  - Tachycardia

  - Elevated or labile blood pressure

  - Urinary incontinence

  - Pallor

  - Sialorrhea

- Other symptoms:

  - Dysphagia

  - Dysarthria

  - Trismus

  - Myoclonus

  - Akinesia

  - Tremor

  - Changes in the level of consciousness, ranging from confusion to stupor to coma

- Laboratory findings:

  - Leukocytosis

  - Metabolic acidosis

  - Evidence of muscle injury (e.g., creatine kinase elevation)

  - Liver enzyme elevation

NMS-like symptoms should not be due to a general medical (e.g., thyrotoxicosis, malignant hyperthermia [MH]) or neurologic (e.g., parkinsonian hyperthermia) disorder; the adverse effects of another type of medication (e.g., serotonin syndrome); or be primary to the underlying psychiatric illness (e.g., malignant catatonia) ([Table 33.2c–3](#tt33-2c-3)).

#### Pathophysiology

A hypodopaminergic state which interferes with temperature regulation is the postulated mechanism for the development of this syndrome. NMS is associated with dopamine antagonists, the abrupt discontinuation of antiparkinsonian medications, and, rarely, the abrupt discontinuation of a dopaminergic agent. This suggests that fluctuation in dopamine binding may be important in its etiology. Because dopamine receptors are widely distributed in the CNS and spinal cord, it is likely that the various symptoms seen in NMS are accounted for by dopamine dysregulation in different areas. Thus, blockade of dopamine receptors in the corpus striatum causes muscle contraction and rigidity, which then initiates heat generation, whereas blockade in the hypothalamus may lead to impaired heat regulation. The autonomic dysfunction commonly seen in advanced cases may be caused by dopamine blockade of spinal neurons. The fluctuations in mental status (e.g., delirium; coma) may be secondary to hyperthermia and/or effects on the mesocortical dopamine tract.

#### Neuroleptic Malignant Syndrome

Neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS), a potentially fatal complication of antipsychotic medication, is characterized by muscle rigidity, fever, diaphoresis, delirium, mutism, and blood pressure abnormalities. Some view NMS as the most severe end of a spectrum that starts with antipsychotic-induced parkinsonism and progresses to extrapyramidal syndrome with fever and then to fulminant NMS.

#### Rabbit Syndrome

This uncommon drug-induced extrapyramidal syndrome is often misdiagnosed as tardive dyskinesia. It most closely resembles a limited expression of a parkinsonian tremor. Patients make rapid chewing movements similar to those made by rabbits, ordinarily faster and more regular than the orofacial tic of tardive dyskinesia. The tongue is spared.

#### Serotonin Syndrome

Serotonin syndrome is a disorder caused by excessive serotonergic input in the CNS, probably within the hypothalamus. The most common cause of serotonin syndrome is the combination of two or more medications with serotonin-enhancing properties, usually by different mechanisms. It is characterized by restlessness, myoclonus, hyperreflexia, diaphoresis, shivering, tremor, autonomic changes, including fever, and mental status changes, such as confusion.

While NMS is one of the few life-threatening psychotropic-induced complications, its infrequent occurrence and severe nature make controlled studies impossible. The best available data supports the following strategies:

1. Prevention: Antipsychotics should be used cautiously in patients with dehydration, catatonic symptoms, basal ganglionic disease, or a history of NMS or MH.

2. Early identification: Patients with early signs should have their antipsychotic immediately discontinued, supportive measures initiated, and careful reintroduction of these agents considered only if absolutely necessary.

3. Treatment: The most important step is early recognition and prompt withdrawal of the offending agent. All cases require supportive interventions, which may include IV hydration, temperature control, and, in more severe cases, ventilator support or dialysis. In advanced cases, admission to an intensive care unit is required, with a fatal outcome occurring in up to 20% of cases. A variety of other treatments may also help, including the use of dopamine agonists (e.g., bromocriptine, amantadine); muscle relaxants (e.g., benzodiazepines, dantrolene); and, in selected patients, electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) (Table 33.2c–4). There has been some limited success reported with psychotropic agents such as bupropion (Wellbutrin), levodopa (Sinemet), carbamazepine (Tegretol), and apomorphine (Apokyn).

4. In patients who require an antipsychotic for their ongoing disorder after an episode of NMS, several steps may reduce the chance of recurrence. These include waiting at least 2 weeks after symptoms resolve before reintroduction is attempted, resolving prior risk factors (e.g., dehydration; avoiding concomitant lithium), and considering alternative therapies when appropriate (e.g., mood stabilizers; ECT). If rechallenge with an antipsychotic is necessary, choosing an alternate agent, starting with low doses and titrating slowly, can also reduce the risk of recurrence.

The occasional familial clustering of NMS led to an analysis of genetic factors which may predispose to this condition. For example, the A1 allele of the dopamine D<sub>2</sub> receptor gene is observed to be overrepresented in patients who develop NMS compared with those who do not. This allele reduces the density and function of the dopamine receptor in the striatum and caudate nuclei, leading to reduced dopaminergic metabolism in those CNS areas with high concentrations of the neurotransmitter. By contrast, polymorphisms of the serotonin receptor gene do not appear to be associated with NMS but may increase the risk of developing acute EPS.

#### Treatment Options and Implications

NMS can be fatal or produce residual sequelae (e.g., cognitive or other neurologic deficits). Therefore, management involves the aggressive use of supportive measures, as well as more specific interventions. Early identification is important, and patients at higher risk should be carefully monitored.

Factors that may increase the risk of developing NMS include the following:

- **Concurrent medical and neuropsychiatric issues:**

  - Dehydration

  - Psychomotor agitation

  - Exhaustion

  - Comorbid neurologic disorders such as encephalitis and traumatic brain injury

  - Low serum iron levels

- **Psychiatric diagnosis and symptom history:**

  - Mood disorders

  - Pre-existing catatonia

  - History of NMS

  - History of MH

- **Medication related:**

  - Parenteral administration

  - High-potency FGAs (e.g., haloperidol)

  - High doses of an antipsychotic

  - Dose increases or intermittent nonadherence

  - Adjunctive lithium treatment

- **Demographic factors:**

  - Male gender

  - Younger age

One easily treatable risk factor is dehydration. Thus, as a preventative measure, severely ill or catatonic patients on an antipsychotic should receive oral fluids or intravenous (IV) hydration. In addition, patients should avoid excessively high temperatures. Very mild cases of low-grade temperature or mild creatine kinase elevations may be treated in a psychiatric unit, while a rapid onset or worsening of symptoms usually requires transfer to a medical intensive care unit. Re-exposure to an antipsychotic can be considered after full resolution of the episode. While switching to an SGA (e.g., clozapine) is often recommended, NMS also occurs with these agents. A past history of this syndrome is another reason patients are started or switched to SGAs. The relative risk of developing NMS between the two generations of antipsychotics, however, is difficult to compare.

A typical laboratory workup of NMS includes a CBC, serum electrolytes, BUN, Cr, and CK, as well as evaluation for other serious neurologic or medical conditions that may be mistaken for NMS. A urinalysis, including an assessment of urine myoglobin, is also usually performed. As part of the differential diagnosis, blood and urine cultures are performed as part of a fever workup. Pronounced elevations in the WBC may occur in NMS. WBCs are typically in the range of 10,000 to 40,000/mm<sup>3</sup>.

CK elevations are typically more than 1,000 IU/L. Moderate elevations in CK may occur in the earliest stages of the disease. CK level may be normal or near-normal if significant rigidity has not yet developed. Other causes of muscle injury leading to increases in serum CK levels may include repeated intramuscular (IM) injections, prolonged or agitated periods in restraint, or dystonic reactions from neuroleptic administration. Importantly, elevated CK levels in the absence of hyperthermia and rigidity are not indicative of NMS.

Mood Stabilizers

<small>CARBAMAZEPINE.</small>

Carbamazepine (Tegretol) may produce changes in the levels of WBCs, platelets, and, under rare circumstances, red blood cells. Aplastic anemia, thrombocytopenia, agranulocytosis, exfoliative dermatitis, and hepatic failure may all occur but are rare. Hyponatremia is common, particularly in the older adults. Pretreatment evaluation typically includes a CBC with differential and platelet count, as well as LFTs and renal function tests. Given the risk for hyponatremia, baseline serum electrolytes may also be obtained, particularly in more vulnerable populations such as the older adults.

The risk of exfoliative dermatitis (e.g., Stevens–Johnson syndrome) is greater in patients with the HLA-B*1502 allele. This allele is more common in patients of Asian descent and is largely absent in individuals of other ethnic backgrounds. Prior to initiating carbamazepine, genetic testing for this allele should be pursued in patients with ancestry, which puts them at a higher likelihood of having the HLA-B*1502 allele. If this allele is identified, that patient should not be treated with carbamazepine. Many clinicians monitor the CBC and LFTs every 2 weeks for the first 2 months of administration. Then, if the laboratory values have been within normal limits, CBC, serum carbamazepine level, and LFTs are monitored every 3 to 6 months. Patients should be cautioned to watch for petechiae, fever or signs of infection, pallor, or undue weakness, as well as signs and symptoms of hepatic injury or dermatologic reactions.

Carbamazepine is teratogenic and may produce a variety of congenital abnormalities, including neural tube defects. A pretreatment urine pregnancy test usually is obtained in women of child-bearing years, and women should be cautioned to use adequate contraception when taking carbamazepine. Carbamazepine may decrease plasma concentrations of hormonal contraceptives.
