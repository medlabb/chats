### PSYCHIATRIC EMERGENCIES RESULTING FROM SUBSTANCE INTOXICATION

Intoxicated patients tend to evoke negative responses from hospital staff, which in turn may further agitate an intoxicated patient. As with all difficult patients, personal feelings should not be allowed to influence professional judgment and clinical care. Utilization of all available resources is the standard of care to ensure safety, as intoxicated patients can be unpredictable. Security forces, though seldom needed, should be alerted before interviewing an intoxicated patient. If possible, the interview should be conducted in a large comfortable room. The clinician should assume a tolerant, nonthreatening manner and be prepared for potential verbal insults and physical threats. Offering food, coffee, or juice may also be helpful, as well as other strategies for intervention with aggressive patients.

Consideration of patient needs longitudinally will require an understanding of each patient’s level of intoxication or potential for withdrawal, medical comorbidities, emotional and psychiatric comorbidities, acceptance or rejection of treatment, relapse potential, and availability of a supportive environment. Due to the heterogeneity of institutional resources, it is important for each provider to become familiar with available resources, both within the care setting and within the community. Resources such as The Patient Placement Criteria developed by the American Society of Addiction Medicine can be very helpful for determining the level of aftercare for patients with substance-related pathology.

#### Stimulant Intoxication

Sympathomimetics substances, also known as stimulants, which include cocaine and amphetamine salts, also are relatively short-acting substances with rapid effects when snorted, smoked, or used intravenously (IV). Symptoms of acute stimulant intoxication include a heightened sense of well-being, increased sense of alertness, decreased anxiety, reduced social inhibitions, increased talkativeness, and poor judgment manifested by loss of money, sexual indiscretion, and potentially illegal activities. Patient symptoms usually resolve within 48 hours. Stimulant intoxication may also present with grandiosity, psychomotor agitation, paranoid ideation, formication (sensation of insects crawling on the skin), bizarre behavior (such as sorting objects into various groups), stereotyped movements of the mouth and the tongue (such as teeth grinding, lip biting, and skin picking), tactile or visual hallucinations, and distorted perceptions. Delusional states, especially paranoia, can also occur.

Physical findings include tachycardia or bradycardia, hypertension or hypotension, hyperthermia, dilated pupils, perspiration or chills, hyperreflexia, nausea, and vomiting. Patients may have evidence of weight loss, muscular weakness, respiratory depression, chest pain, or cardiac arrhythmias. Intoxication can lead to confusion, seizures, or coma. Patients who abuse stimulants IV are at risk for numerous medical conditions, including endocarditis, tetanus, hepatitis, abscesses (MRSA), and AIDS. Urine and serum drug screens provide corroboration of the diagnosis and should be part of the PES intervention, if suspected or if there is a need for determination of diagnosis based on readily available information.

#### Multi-Visit Patient Program (MVP)

As discussed above, the goal of a successful community psychiatry experience is the smooth transition of patients from inpatient acute hospitalizations when needed to outpatient care. This lofty goal is not always possible, and the literature is rife with descriptions of patients who often present to emergency services. These patients are often substance users but sometimes have psychosis, mood, or personality disorders. Derogatory terms such as frequent flyers were much used in the 1980s and were gradually replaced by more neutral terms such as MVP. The MVP program provides linkage of patients who have more than six emergency center visits per year to rehabilitation resources, housing resources, and other community resources as indicated. The main drivers of utilization seem to be divided into behavioral, social, and medical. Solving these issues is often not within the hands of the medical system or the psychiatrists, testifying to the interdependence of agencies on each other in the community psychiatry structure. The MVP program has been successful in mitigating recidivism for multiple individuals, much to the relief of staff and patients alike.

Risks of Psychiatric Illness During Pregnancy

<small>IMPACT ON MOTHER.</small>

Untreated illness can lead to clinical deterioration, with concomitant psychological distress, social and occupational dysfunction, financial hardship, an inability to plan for and successfully cope with the impending life transition of motherhood, and possible suicidal ideation and self-harm. Recently acquired data from nearly 600,000 American women of reproductive age demonstrates a dramatic risk in suicidal ideation and self-harm behaviors over the last 12 years. Suicidal ideation increased from 0.1% per 100 women in 2006 to 0.5% per 100 women in 2017, and self-harm behaviors were reported by 0.1% per 100 women in 2006, subsequently doubling to 0.2% per 100 women in 2017. With many psychiatric disorders, functional impairment results in sleep disturbance, poor nutrition, lower use of prenatal vitamins, weight loss, and increased risk of cigarette, alcohol, and drug use. Poor rates of prenatal care can fail to detect gestational anemia, diabetes, hypertension, and hypothyroidism, which can harm both mother and fetus. Close attention must be paid to the host of behaviors that accompany psychiatric relapse in framing the risk-benefit discussion. As an example, cigarette smoking is associated with increased rates of miscarriage, intrauterine and perinatal death, preterm birth, and intrauterine growth restriction, outcomes that may be worse than any risk of psychotropic medications.

<small>IMPACT ON OFFSPRING.</small>

Untreated mental illness carries significant independent risks for pregnancy outcomes. Increasingly appreciated is the impact of the prenatal milieu on fetal and infant health. The maternal HPA axis can have fetal programming effects that increase offspring risk for subsequent adult-onset medical and psychiatric disorders. Severe prenatal maternal stress during the first trimester of pregnancy has been associated with congenital anomalies. Furthermore, prenatal anxiety, pregnancy-specific anxiety, depression, and maternal stress hormones have all been associated with preterm birth, increased rates of obstetrical interventions during labor and delivery, small-for-gestational-age, low birth weight, impaired neonatal stress regulation, infant fearful temperament, adverse cognitive outcomes, and reduced gray matter brain volumes. Novel tests of fetal cardiovascular reactivity during maternally stressful tasks are associated with altered nervous system function in utero in fetuses of depressed and anxious mothers.

Safety of Psychopharmacology During Pregnancy and Lactation

<small>MAKING SENSE OF THE LITERATURE.</small>

Although ethical considerations limit performing randomized, prospective medication studies with pregnant women, improved methods for naturalistic studies (large samples, propensity score matching) and ongoing collection of observational data in registries have increased the information available to women facing perinatal treatment decisions. Increasingly sophisticated study designs and statistical methods have been important for reducing sources of bias, such as confounding by indication, which refers to the host of demographic, behavioral, and health-related characteristics associated with the illness itself (i.e., increased smoking, poor prenatal care) or use of medication (i.e., potentially more severe illness). Both medication exposure and maternal mental illness contribute to the outcomes of interest, and thus the most informative studies can measure and control for each. A true teratogen is associated with a consistent birth defect across studies; therefore, inconsistent findings of disparate defects are harder to interpret, especially in view that the psychiatric disorder itself is not always accounted for. Additionally, well-powered studies that properly control for confounding are needed to detect rare infant malformations. The multifactorial nature of infant development suggests that large studies are needed to control for numerous contributory variables. Greater outcomes standardization and data pooling across research centers will facilitate advances in this domain.

Pharmacotherapy is an important modality for women with illnesses unresponsive to alternative treatments and for those with moderate-to-severe symptoms. The psychiatrist plays an important role in supporting the patient to make an informed decision and providing education to and fostering communication with the obstetrician and pediatrician. A guiding principle in treating pregnant women is minimizing fetal exposures both to the untreated illness and to psychotropics. The medication to which a mother has responded in the past is often the best choice, even absent substantial data, given the known risk of severe, untreated illness. Most drugs cross the placenta, albeit to a varied extent. Psychotropic transfer to the baby via lactation is much lower than placental transfer due to incomplete psychotropic absorption into breast milk and first-pass metabolism effects in the infant. If fetal exposure to a particular psychotropic has already occurred, this agent would be reasonable to continue during lactation rather than switching to one with theoretically lower exposure. If the drug was started postpartum, observing the infant’s behavior before the mother is treated permits clinicians to avoid misinterpreting typical behavior as potentially drug-related. For each psychotropic class below, the available evidence is summarized in order of reproductive domain: congenital teratogenicity, late gestation effects, neonatal withdrawal/toxicity, developmental (neurobehavioral) teratogenicity, and lactation.
