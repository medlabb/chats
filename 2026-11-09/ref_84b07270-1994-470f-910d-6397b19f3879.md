#### Drug Interactions

Pharmacokinetic interactions are mainly related to methadone being a substrate for CYP450 2B6 and 3A4. Inducers of P450 enzymes such as the anticonvulsants, phenytoin and carbamazepine, the antituberculous medication rifampin, and antiretroviral medications, nevirapine and efavirenz increase the metabolism of methadone so that the patient could go into opioid withdrawal suddenly and become destabilized. Newer antiviral agents are generally well tolerated in patients taking methadone with routine monitoring. Inhibitors of the enzymes such as the antidepressant fluvoxamine, the antibiotic, erythromycin, and antifungal medications, like ketoconazole, have the potential to reduce methadone metabolism and lead to side effects or toxicity, though clinically meaningful effects are rarely observed with inhibitors. Pharmacodynamic interactions obviously can occur with opioid antagonists, such as naloxone or naltrexone, which would cause a patient on methadone maintenance to go into severe, precipitated withdrawal. Other opioids, sedative–hypnotics, and alcohol also have a pharmacodynamic interaction with methadone, leading to additive excess sedation and a greater risk of suppression of respiratory drive and overdose.

#### Laboratory Interference

None known.

#### Dosing

Federal eligibility requirements for MMT stipulate that individuals entering treatment have a minimum 1-year history of OUD. The minimum age requirement is 18 years of age; however, individuals between 16 and 18 years of age may enter methadone treatment under certain conditions. There is no upper age limit on program entry. Pregnant women with OUD may be accepted for methadone treatment. Individuals with major medical conditions are also eligible for treatment, as are those who use multiple substances where OUD is one component of their addictive disease.

Methadone dose determination should always be performed by an experienced physician based on their clinical assessment of the patient. Data indicate that adequate methadone doses are correlated with reduced illicit drug use and improved patient retention in treatment. A comparison by Eric C. Strain and colleagues of moderate daily doses of methadone (40 to 50 mg) and high doses (80 to 100 mg) showed significantly lower illicit opioid use in the high-dose group. Similar findings have been reported in regard to treatment retention, with one study showing that patients receiving 80 mg/day of methadone were twice as likely to remain in treatment as those receiving 60 to 79 mg, who were, in turn, twice as likely to remain in treatment as patients receiving less than 60 mg. The lowest effective dose of methadone is now widely accepted to be approximately 60 mg, with an optimum average dose for most patients of approximately 80 mg (±20 mg), although some patients require a dose higher than this range.
