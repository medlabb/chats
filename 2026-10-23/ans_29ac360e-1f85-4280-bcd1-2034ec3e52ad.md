## Answer

 ## Blood transfusion in trauma

- **Activate a massive transfusion protocol (MTP)** for profound or ongoing hemorrhage, especially when replacement of the patient’s total blood volume is anticipated. Transfusing red blood cells (RBCs) alone can cause dilutional coagulopathy; plasma and platelets should be administered early as well. (1)
- Consider MTP when at least **two** of the following are present: penetrating injury, positive FAST examination, systolic blood pressure ≤90 mmHg, or heart rate ≥120 beats/min. (2)
- For major hemorrhage, use **whole blood** when available or balanced components in approximately a **1:1:1 ratio of RBCs:plasma:platelets**. (3)
- Begin blood products as soon as they are available rather than continuing large-volume crystalloid; ideally, administer no more than **1 L of warm crystalloid** before blood is available. Warm blood and intravenous fluids to prevent hypothermia. (3)
- If cross-matched blood is not immediately available, use **emergency-release blood**. Type O RhD-negative blood can be given to anyone, although it may be limited. Type O RhD-positive blood may be used for males, females not of childbearing potential, or when lifesaving transfusion is required and RhD-negative blood is unavailable. (4)
- **Tranexamic acid (TXA)** is recommended for suspected moderate-to-severe active hemorrhage when the patient presents within 3 hours of injury: 1 g intravenously over 10 minutes followed by 1 g over 8 hours; an alternative is 2 g intravenously as a single bolus. (2)
- Use **TEG or another rapid coagulation assessment** to guide transfusion when available. For patients without severe bleeding, suggested thresholds include hemoglobin <7 g/dL without acute coronary syndrome risk, platelets <50,000/µL, INR >2, and fibrinogen <150 mg/dL. (5)
- During resuscitation, avoid unnecessary crystalloids and monitor for hypothermia, acidosis, and hypocalcemia. (3)
