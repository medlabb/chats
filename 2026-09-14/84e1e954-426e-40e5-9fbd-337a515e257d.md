## Answer

 **ALT 500–700, later 150 but still elevated, needs evaluation by a pediatrician/pediatric hepatologist.** ALT reflects liver-cell injury, not liver function by itself; the trend in ALT does not reliably show severity. (1) (2)

### Important next steps

1. **Repeat and expand liver testing**
   - AST and ALT  
   - Total and **direct/conjugated bilirubin**
   - GGT and alkaline phosphatase
   - Albumin
   - PT/INR, preferably with glucose  
   These tests assess hepatocellular injury, cholestasis, and liver synthetic function. (2)

2. **Assess severity urgently**
   Check for hypoglycemia, acidosis, high ammonia, rising bilirubin, low albumin, prolonged PT/INR, vomiting, excessive sleepiness, confusion, bleeding, jaundice, ascites, or enlarged liver. Prolonged PT/INR that does not correct after parenteral vitamin K suggests severe liver disease. (2) (3)

   **Immediate hospital assessment** is needed for altered consciousness, bleeding, significant jaundice, repeated vomiting, low blood sugar, or abnormal/prolonged INR. Abnormal synthetic function—especially a high INR—is an indication for prompt referral to a transplant center. (4)

3. **Perform abdominal imaging**
   - Abdominal ultrasound with Doppler is an important initial investigation, particularly if bilirubin or GGT is elevated. (5)

4. **Investigate causes according to the clinical history and results**
   The evaluation may include:
   - Review of all medicines, supplements, and possible toxin exposure
   - Viral, immune, and toxic causes of hepatitis (6)
   - α1-antitrypsin concentration/phenotype or genetic testing
   - Thyroid testing
   - Metabolic testing, including urine organic acids, plasma amino acids, lactate, ammonia, glucose, creatine kinase, acylcarnitine profile, and related tests when indicated (5) (7)
   - Testing for disorders such as galactosemia, tyrosinemia, hereditary fructose intolerance, cystic fibrosis, bile-acid disorders, and genetic cholestatic diseases when suggested by the findings (5)

5. **Consider specialist-directed genetic testing or liver biopsy**
   If the elevation persists and initial testing is unrevealing, rapid genotype testing/genetic panels may assist diagnosis; liver biopsy is considered in selected cases. (1) (7)

**Do not start treatment solely to lower ALT.** Treatment depends on the identified cause; monitoring of bilirubin, glucose, albumin, and especially PT/INR is more important for determining severity than ALT alone. The selected references do not provide a specific treatment regimen for an otherwise well 1-year-old with isolated fluctuating ALT.
