## Phototherapy
If the newborn is not yet receiving phototherapy, intensive phototherapy should be started immediately. Intensive phototherapy is defined as an irradiance of 30 to 35 microW/cm2per nm in the blue-green spectrum (wavelength between 460 and 490 nm, optimal at 478 nm) applied to as much of the newborn's skin surface as possible by using a combination of light sources  (figure 3) [1,6]. If the newborn is already receiving phototherapy, efforts should be made to optimize delivery by maximizing skin exposure to illuminate approximately 80 percent of the skin surface and eliminating interruptions to phototherapy.
The administration, duration, and other details related to phototherapy are discussed in more detail separately. (See"Unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia in newborns ≥35 weeks of gestation: Initial management and phototherapy", section on 'Initiating phototherapy'.)

- Clinically significant hyperbilirubinemia (ie, approaching or above the treatment threshold) at age >14 days and/or direct hyperbilirubinemia at any time during the neonatal period is concerning [70]. Infants with either of these findings should undergo additional evaluation directed by expert consultation with pediatric gastroenterology, hematology, and metabolic specialists.

- **Treatment**– The threshold for treatment in these cases is based upon the hour-specific TSB thresholds summarized in the figures  (figure 2A-Bandfigure 1A-B), which provide guidance up to age 14 days. Neonates with persistent hyperbilirubinemia at >14 days often have a mixed picture (ie, both elevated direct and unconjugated bilirubin). There are few data to guide treatment in these cases. Phototherapy can be used to reduce the unconjugated fraction (using the 14-day TSB threshold as the trigger for intervention). However, phototherapy is generally not useful if the direct bilirubin level is >50 percent of TSB. Neonates with mixed hyperbilirubinemia who are exposed to phototherapy may develop transient bronzing of the skin known as bronze baby syndrome. This condition is not harmful and usually resolves without sequelae after several weeks or months. (See'Goals and thresholds for treatment'above and'Adverse effects'above.)

- Newborns exposed to persistently high TSB levels for a prolonged time may be at increased risk for bilirubin-induced neurologic disorders (BIND). Such newborns are best managed in a care setting where they can receive prompt supportive care and close monitoring of neurologic signs (eg, using BIND scores  (table 1)). In addition, care for these newborns should be directed in consultation with experts in pediatric gastroenterology, hematology, and metabolism. (See"Unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia in newborns ≥35 weeks of gestation: Escalation of care and exchange transfusion", section on 'Setting and transfer of care'.)
