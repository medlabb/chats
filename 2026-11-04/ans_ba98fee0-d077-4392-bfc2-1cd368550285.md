```
A term 3.6kg boy was born to an Rh negative primi mother (Mother had negative ICT, no anti D antibodies and had received anti D injection at 28 week gestation). The baby was born with Bpositive blood group and had cord blood TSB of 3.1mg/dl with Hb 18g/dl and 15% retic counts. His DCT was negative. Now the baby’s TSB is increasing more than 0.3mg/dl per hour since birth. We started phototherapy considering the significant rise but the TSB keeps increasing but stays around the phototherapy range only. The hb level has stayed constant throughout. At 24h of life the TSB was 8mg/dl and at 30h 9.4mg/dl desoite being on continuous phototherapy. Should I start looking for causes of non immune hemolysis or wait for a few more readings. Any suggestion would be really helpful.
```

## Answer

 The pattern should **not simply be observed for several more readings**. A TSB rise of **≥0.2 mg/dL/hour after the first 24 hours**, or a bilirubin level that continues to rise despite phototherapy, warrants escalation of management because of the risk of bilirubin-induced neurologic injury. The reported rise from 8.0 to 9.4 mg/dL over 6 hours is approximately **0.23 mg/dL/hour**. (rf_81)

First, verify that **intensive phototherapy is being delivered correctly**: irradiance should be approximately **30–35 µW/cm²/nm**, with illumination of about **80% of the skin surface**, minimal interruptions, and maximal skin exposure. (1)

The TSB should be rechecked within **4–6 hours** when the response is inadequate. If the infant is approaching the exchange-transfusion threshold, monitoring should be more frequent, with TSB checks every **2 hours** as described for that situation. (rf_81) (2)

It is reasonable to begin evaluating for an **ongoing bilirubin-producing process**, including hemolysis, rather than waiting indefinitely. The selected references specifically mention obtaining **G6PD enzyme activity** if not already performed and pursuing additional testing according to clinical concern, including evaluation for infection or congenital disorders when indicated. (3) They do not provide a complete neonatal nonimmune-hemolysis workup, so the remaining details cannot be found in the selected references.

Whether escalation to exchange transfusion is required depends on the infant’s **hour-specific TSB exchange threshold**, proximity to that threshold, and neurologic status; a TSB merely “within the phototherapy range” is not sufficient information to determine this. Signs of acute bilirubin encephalopathy or a TSB within **2 mg/dL of the exchange threshold** require escalation. (rf_81)
