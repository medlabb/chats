# INTRODUCTION

Eosinophils are a distinctive type of granulocyte that are predominantly localized in tissues and normally circulate in the peripheral blood in low numbers. Activation of eosinophils releases inflammatory mediators and cytotoxic proteins, but excessive tissue infiltration and activation can cause disease and dysfunction in multiple organ systems.
Hypereosinophilia (HE) refers to a substantial and sustained increase of eosinophils in the blood and/or tissues. Hypereosinophilic syndromes (HES) describe organ damage or dysfunction caused by eosinophil infiltration. This topic describes the clinical features, evaluation, and diagnosis of patients with HES.

# SUMMARY

- **Eosinophil biology**– Eosinophils are distinctive granulocytes involved in immune responses to infections, allergic phenomena, asthma, and other conditions. They are primarily tissue-dwelling cells, but circulating eosinophils can increase with various infectious and inflammatory conditions.

- Eosinophil biology and numerous causes of eosinophilia are discussed separately. (See"Approach to the patient with elevated eosinophil counts".)

- **Definitions**

**Hypereosinophilia (HE)**– Absolute eosinophil count (AEC) ≥1500 eosinophils/microL in blood ≥2 weeks apart and/or pathologically confirmed tissue eosinophilia.

**Hypereosinophilic syndrome (HES)**– Organ damage/dysfunction from tissue eosinophilia together with blood HE and/or tissue HE.

- **Presentation**– Clinical manifestations are protean, ranging from asymptomatic to life-threatening emergencies, and it is essential to consider a broad differential  (table 3). Skin, respiratory, gastrointestinal, and cardiac symptoms are common manifestations of HE/HES, but eosinophils can affect nearly every organ system. (See'Clinical features of HES'above.)

- **Evaluation**– The urgency of evaluation is informed by the clinical presentation and level of AEC. ## Diagnostic criteria
The diagnosis of HES requires blood or tissue hypereosinophilia (HE) accompanied by documented organ damage or dysfunction attributable to eosinophil infiltration  (table 1). Other causes for organ effects must be excluded [1].
The term HES is used for any patient in whom eosinophils cause the organ damage, regardless of whether the eosinophilia results from a reactive process, a malignant process, or another underlying disease. By contrast, HES should not be used when the clinical manifestations and the predominant cause of organ damage can be explained by another pathophysiologic mechanism. As an example, a patient with chest pain from known coronary artery disease who is found to have reactive HE would not****be considered to have HES unless there were other clinical manifestations that were attributable to eosinophilic tissue infiltration.
Individuals with blood HE but no detectable organ effects are described as having HE of uncertain significance (HE-US). These patients must be followed closely with periodic re-evaluation for eosinophil-related organ damage. (See'HE of uncertain significance'below.)
