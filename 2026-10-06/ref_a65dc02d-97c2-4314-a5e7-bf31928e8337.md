##### Investigative Studies

Patients with suspected TIA or acute ischemic stroke should be evaluated promptly with **blood tests** (complete blood count, prothrombin and partial thromboplastin time, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, treponemal test for syphilis, glucose, troponin) and **ECG** to identify underlying causes or mimics of cerebrovascular disease and guide subsequent therapy.

**Noncontrast CT scan** or **MRI** should be performed immediately to exclude intracerebral hemorrhage and other disorders that can mimic ischemic stroke.

Patients with symptoms or imaging findings consistent with anterior circulation ischemia should undergo **CT angiography** or **MR angiography** to detect clinically consistent stenosis or occlusion of the internal carotid or proximal middle cerebral artery, which may be amenable to intraarterial clot retrieval, or operable lesions in the extracranial carotid artery (discussed later in this chapter).

**Perfusion CT** and **diffusion-perfusion MRI** can detect **diffusion-perfusion mismatch** and identify ischemic brain tissue that is potentially salvageable by thrombolysis 4.5-9 hours post-stroke or thrombectomy 6-24 hours post-stroke (**[Table 13-5](#greenberg11_ch13tb5)**).

**Echocardiography** should be performed if there is a predisposing cardiac disorder, if symptoms suggest cardiogenic embolus (eg, recurrent TIAs with symptoms related to different vascular territories), or if no extracardiac (vascular or hematologic) cause is identified.


##### Medical Treatment

1. **Blood pressure** should usually not be lowered acutely, except for patients with acute ischemic stroke in whom it is high enough (>185 mm Hg systolic or >110 mm Hg diastolic pressure) to make an otherwise suitable candidate ineligible for thrombolytic therapy (see later). When acute antihypertensive therapy is required, recommended drugs include intravenous [labetalol](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660418), [nicardipine](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660570), or [clevidipine](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659025).
2. **Hyperthermia** (temperature >38°C), which may adversely affect outcome, should be corrected, and any infectious cause identified. However, induced hypothermia has not been shown to improve outcome in TIA or acute ischemic stroke.
3. **Hypoxia** (oxygen saturation ≤94%) should be treated with supplemental oxygen.
4. **Hypoglycemia** (blood glucose <60 mg/dL) should be corrected, and **hyperglycemia** should be treated to reduce blood glucose levels to 140-180 mg/dL.

##### Electrocardiogram (ECG)

An ECG should be obtained routinely to detect unrecognized myocardial infarction or cardiac arrhythmias, such as atrial fibrillation, which predispose to stroke.


##### Lumbar Puncture

Lumbar puncture should be performed only in selected cases, to exclude subarachnoid hemorrhage (manifested by xanthochromia and red blood cells) or to document meningovascular syphilis (reactive CSF VDRL) or certain forms of vasculitis as the cause of stroke.


##### Brain Imaging

1. **Noncontrast CT scan** or **MRI** (**[Figure 13-3](#greenberg11_ch13fg3)**) should be obtained routinely (and always prior to thrombolytic therapy) to distinguish between infarction and hemorrhage as the cause of stroke, to exclude other lesions (eg, tumor or abscess) that can mimic stroke, and to localize the lesion. Noncontrast CT is usually preferred for initial diagnosis because it is widely and rapidly available. However, its sensitivity within the first 6 hours is limited, and MRI may be superior for demonstrating early infarcts and brainstem or cerebellar infarcts and for detecting thrombotic occlusion of venous sinuses.
2. **CT perfusion** and **perfusion-diffusion MRI** can detect diffusion-perfusion mismatch corresponding to the ischemic penumbra—brain tissue at risk of infarction but potentially salvageable by thrombolysis or thrombectomy.

#### DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS

Putaminal, thalamic, and lobar hypertensive hemorrhages may be difficult to distinguish from infarction in the same locations. However, severe headache, nausea and vomiting, and impaired consciousness suggest hemorrhage. CT or MRI (**[Figure 13-20](#greenberg11_ch13fg20)**) provides a definitive diagnosis.

#### TREATMENT


##### Medical

1. **Initial management** of intracerebral hemorrhage includes airway support with ventilatory assistance if required.
2. **Hypertension** should be treated by reducing systolic blood pressure to 140-179 mm Hg with intravenous [nicardipine](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660570) and, if needed, intravenous [labetalol](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660418). This is as effective as more intensive reduction to 110-139 mm Hg in improving clinical outcome and is associated with fewer adverse hypotensive effects.
3. **Coagulopathy** should be reversed by clotting factor replacement with prothrombin complex concentrate or fresh frozen plasma and severe **thrombocytopenia** corrected by platelet transfusion. If coagulopathy is due to anticoagulation or thrombolytic or antiplatelet therapy, the agent responsible should be discontinued and, if necessary, its effect reversed (see [Table 13-4](#greenberg11_ch13tb4)). Platelet transfusion for patients on antiplatelet agents is indicated only if they are to undergo surgical evacuation of a hematoma.


4. **Hyperglycemia** and **hypoglycemia** should both be avoided and insulin or glucose administered as needed.
