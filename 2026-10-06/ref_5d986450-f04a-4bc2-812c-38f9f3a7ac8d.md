## 4.2

## Rhinosinusitis

### 4.2.1

### Acute Rhinosinusitis

Key Features

- •Rhinosinusitis is an inflammatory condition of the nose and sinuses.

- •By definition, signs and symptoms last less than 4 weeks.

- •The condition resolves with treatment; inadequate treatment may lead to disabling chronic disease.

Acute bacterial rhinosinusitis is frequently managed by primary care providers. The otolaryngologist often sees patients who are inadequately treated or who have recurrent disease. Aggressive care is necessary with immunocompromised patients. Collection of mucous during nasal endoscopy enables one to obtain data for culture-directed antibiotic therapy. Treatment is usually medical in the absence of orbital or intracranial complications.

#### Epidemiology

True incidence is difficult to establish because of some overlap with other complaints, such as upper respiratory infection or allergy. However, rhinosinusitis is among the most common of all healthcare complaints and accounts for 2% to 10% of all primary care and otolaryngology visits. Approximately 5.1 million ambulatory office visits per year are coded with a diagnosis of acute rhinosinusitis and approximately 86% of these visits result in an oral antibiotic prescription, making acute bacterial rhinosinusitis the fifth most common diagnosis associated with antibiotic therapy. Lower socioeconomic status and smoking are also associated with higher prevalence of rhinosinusitis.

#### Clinical

#### Signs and Symptoms

Acute rhinosinusitis may be suspected based on signs and symptoms. Major factors include facial pain or pressure, congestion or fullness, nasal obstruction, nasal discharge, purulence, or discolored postnasal drainage, hyposmia/anosmia, purulence in nasal cavity on exam, and fever. Minor factors include headache, fatigue, halitosis, dental pain, cough, and ear pain or ear pressure/fullness. Updated guidelines have defined acute rhinosinusitis as sinonasal inflammation lasting less than 4 weeks with sudden onset of symptoms. Symptoms must include purulent nasal drainage and nasal obstruction/congestion or facial pressure/pain, or both. Acute bacterial rhinosinusitis as opposed to viral rhinosinusitis is suspected when symptoms persist beyond 10 days or worsen within 10 days after initial improvement.

#### Differential Diagnosis

In the early phase of the disease (first 10 days), the etiology is presumed to be viral. Thus, viral rhinosinusitis is the main alternative diagnosis in the patient with a history of mild symptoms or symptoms of less than 2 weeks duration. The most common viruses implicated in rhinosinusitis are rhinovirus, influenza, and parainfluenza. Other entities to be considered include rhinitis medicamentosa, allergic rhinitis, vasomotor rhinitis, or rare nasal manifestations of systemic disease such as limited Wegener granulomatosis or sarcoidosis. Other causes of localized symptoms include severe periodontal disease or migraine headaches, which may include throbbing localized headache as well as nasal congestion or rhinorrhea. In the immunocompromised patient, a high index of suspicion for invasive fungal rhinosinusitis is critical.

There is currently no evidence to support use of mucolytics in acute rhinosinusitis.

Occasionally, maxillary puncture is necessary to obtain material for culture and/or to relieve severe symptoms. This may be done via the inferior meatus or canine fossa. A common indication for this is an immunocompromised patient on multiple recent antibiotics with an occluded sinus.

#### Outcome and Follow-Up

Acute rhinosinusitis generally resolves with appropriate medical treatment. Subacute rhinosinusitis progresses from 4 to 12 weeks and usually still resolves. Certain patients may relapse, termed recurrent acute rhinosinusitis, with symptoms again lasting less than 4 weeks and recurring four or more times per year. In patients with recurrent disease, CT scanning and more aggressive therapy, including systemic steroids and/or surgery, may be indicated.

Meningitis is the most common intracranial complication from acute rhinosinusitis, often from sphenoid disease. This is managed with IV antibiotics and usually surgical drainage. Orbital and intracranial complications are discussed in detail in [Chapters 4.1.2 and 4.1.3](#!/content/book/3-s2.0-B9780323882668000041#s0095) .

#### Labs

Unless the patient is toxic or immunosuppressed, or one suspects complications of acute rhinosinusitis (orbital or intracranial extension), blood work such as a CBC with differential is not helpful. Elevated c-reactive protein (CRP) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) help rule in acute rhinosinusitis when positive. Testing for allergy and immune function is considered with recurrent acute rhinosinusitis, or chronic disease.

#### Microbiology

It is generally accepted that viral infection predominates for the first 10 to 14 days and then leads to sinus ostia obstruction. Inspissated mucus leads to bacterial infection. The most common organisms are *Streptococcus pneumoniae* , *H. influenzae* , and *Moraxella catarrhalis* , as well as anaerobes and other *Streptococcus* species. Drug resistance is a growing problem.

#### Treatment Options

Uncomplicated acute rhinosinusitis is observed or treated medically. If symptoms persist beyond 7 to 10 days, bacterial infection is more likely and antibiotics are indicated. The typical duration of therapy is 5 to 10 days. Amoxicillin +/- clavulanate is the first line in treatment of acute bacterial rhinosinusitis. Adult empiric therapy published guidelines recommend (1) amoxicillin/clavulanate (1.75–4.0 g/250 mg per day; or 875 mg twice daily); (2) amoxicillin (1.5–4 g/d; or 500 mg three times daily); or (3) cefuroxime axetil (250 mg twice daily). In β-lactam–allergic patients, guidelines recommend (1) trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole double-strength (twice daily), (2) doxycycline (100 mg twice daily), or (3) macrolide therapy ( [Table 4.6](#!/content/book/3-s2.0-B9780323882668000041#t0035) ). If there is no improvement in 72 hours, or if there has been antibiotic use within the previous 4 to 6 weeks, the antibiotic should be switched; consider a fluoroquinolone, ceftriaxone, or clindamycin. If possible, antibiotic therapy should be culture-directed at this point.

## 9.9

## Diseases of the Adenoids and Palatine Tonsils

### 9.9.1

### Adenotonsillitis

Key Features

- •Adenotonsillitis is most commonly viral in etiology.

- •Bacterial etiology is similar to acute otitis media (OM).

  - •Group A β-hemolytic *S. pneumoniae*

  - • *Moraxella catarrhalis*

  - • *H. influenzae*

- •Infection with anaerobes are associated with suppurative complications.

  - • *Fusobacterium* , *Acitomyces* , and *Porphyromas* species

- •Chronic infection is typically polymicrobial.

- •There is growing evidence for the role of biofilm in chronic infections.

Acute adenotonsillitis most commonly presents in children 5 to 10 years of age and young adults 15 to 25 years of age. The primary consideration in its accurate diagnosis and treatment is prevention of secondary complications particularly associated with group A β-hemolytic *S. pneumoniae* including rheumatic fever and poststreptococcal glomerulonephritis. Suppurative complications avoided by early and appropriate management include peritonsillar abscess and deep neck space infection.

#### Epidemiology

The average incidence of all acute upper respiratory infections is five to seven per child per year. It is estimated that children have one streptococcal infection every 4 to 5 years. Group A *Streptococcus* is isolated in 30% to 37% of children with pharyngitis and asymptomatic carriage of group A *Streptococcus* is approximately 10% to 15% for children aged 14 years or younger.

#### Clinical

#### Signs

- ■Erythremic, exudative palatine tonsils

- ■Tonsilloliths

- ■Trismus

- ■Cervical adenopathy

- ■Palatal petechiae (infectious mononucleosis)

#### Symptoms

- ■Halitosis

- ■Sore throat

- ■Odynophagia

- ■Purulent rhinorrhea

- ■Postnasal drip

- ■Nasal obstruction

- ■Fever

#### Differential Diagnosis

- ■Adenotonsillar hypertrophy

- ■Acute pharyngitis (bacterial or viral)

- ■Peritonsillar abscess

- ■Infectious mononucleosis

- ■Lymphoma, leukemia, or other neoplasm (unilateral/asymmetric tonsillar enlargement)

#### Evaluation

#### Physical Exam

In the head and neck exam, focus on examination of the oral cavity, inspection of palatine tonsils looking for enlargement, erythema, peritonsillar cellulitis, abscess, and exudates. Attention should be paid to symmetry. Palpation of the neck is performed to assess for cervical adenopathy.

#### Imaging

Contrast-enhanced CT if concerned about retropharyngeal or deep neck space infection.

#### Labs

Complete blood count (CBC) with differential, monospot, if clinically indicated.

#### Other Tests

Throat swab for culture and sensitivity and latex agglutination tests for group A β-hemolytic *Streptococcus* .



#### Treatment Options

#### Medical

Analgesics and antipyretics are prescribed. Antibiotics if bacterial infection is suspected.
