[Warfarin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660008) and other vitamin K antagonists (VKAs) have historically been first-line agents for stroke prevention in patients with atrial fibrillation. Their narrow therapeutic window, multiple drug interactions, dietary restrictions, requirement for frequent monitoring, and low safety profile affected routine use of VKAs in practice. *Four direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs)—dabigatran, [rivaroxaban](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659108), [apixaban](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659135), and edoxaban—have been shown to be *at least as effective* as [warfarin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660008) for stroke prevention in patients with atrial fibrillation, with improved safety profiles and ease of use, and are therefore recommended as first-line therapy over [warfarin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660008) in patients with atrial fibrillation* ([Table 12–3](#papacmdt26_ch12tb3)). *Importantly, the DOACs have not been studied in patients with moderate or severe mitral stenosis, and they should not be used for patients with mechanical prosthetic valves.* The term “nonvalvular atrial fibrillation” is no longer used in the American or European guidelines since most patients with valvular heart disease (other than mitral stenosis) have been included in trials of DOACs and shown to be equally as effective in these patients.

### E. Secondary Prevention

Antithrombotic therapy for secondary prevention offered after the initial 3–6 months of treatment should be considered in patients with VTE that is not majorly provoked; it is most compelling for those with unprovoked VTE. For most patients on [apixaban](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659135) or [rivaroxaban](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659108) who continue medication to prevent recurrence, the dose can be reduced to prophylactic intensity after the initial 6 months of therapy ([Table 16–14](#papacmdt26_ch16tb14)). The 2024 EVE study suggested that dose-reduced [apixaban](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659135) (2.5 mg twice daily rather than 5 mg twice daily) after an initial 6 months of therapeutic anticoagulation carried a similar risk of recurrence but did not lower the incidence of bleeding in patients with cancer-associated VTE. In patients without cancer deemed poor candidates for ongoing DOAC or [warfarin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660008) use but who warrant some secondary prevention, low-dose (81–100 mg) [aspirin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660322) may be used; however, this will provide far less reduction in risk of recurrent VTE with similar bleeding risk.

Perioperative management of anticoagulation is reviewed in [Part 3](/content.aspx?legacysectionid=papacmdt26_ch3).

### D. Direct-Acting Oral Anticoagulants

Unlike [warfarin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660008), the DOACs act directly against coagulation factors. [Dabigatran](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659087) is a direct [thrombin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659045) inhibitor (DTI); [rivaroxaban](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659108), [apixaban](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659135), and [edoxaban](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659219) are direct factor Xa inhibitors. DOACs (1) have a predictable dose effect and do not require laboratory monitoring, (2) have anticoagulant activity independent of vitamin K with no need for dietary stasis, and (3) are renally metabolized to varying degrees, requiring restrictions or dose reductions related to kidney function ([Table 16–9](#papacmdt26_ch16tb9)). While the DOACs have fewer drug interactions than [warfarin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660008), if DOACs are given with potentially interacting medications, there is no reliable way to measure the impact on anticoagulant activity of the concomitant administration. There is also no reliable way to measure adherence. DOAC levels are affected by medications that modify P-glycoprotein (P-gp) transport in the gut; [apixaban](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659135) and [rivaroxaban](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659108) are also affected by medications that modify hepatic CYP4A4 metabolism. Medications that decrease DOAC metabolism may increase patients’ bleeding risk, while medications that increase DOAC metabolism may increase thrombotic risk. Concomitant medications should be carefully reviewed; antiepileptic, antifungal, antiarrhythmic, and antiviral medications are categories with frequent interactions. DOACs should not be used with strong CYP3A4 inducers such as [rifampin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660083), [carbamazepine](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659978), and St John’s wort. [nirmatrelvir](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659579)/[ritonavir](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660588) (Paxlovid) contains a strong combined P-gp and CYP3A4 inhibitor, and patients receiving acute VTE dosages of DOACs will need to hold, reduce dose, or transition to LMWH if given Paxlovid ([Table 16–10](#papacmdt26_ch16tb10)). The clinician must carefully consider kidney function, concomitant medications, indication, candidacy for lead-in parenteral therapy (as required for acute VTE treatment with [edoxaban](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659219) and [dabigatran](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659087) only), weight, and anticipated adherence. (See [Table 16–9](#papacmdt26_ch16tb9) for details.) For patients who are morbidly obese (> 120 kg or BMI ≥ 40), standard doses of [apixaban](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659135) or [rivaroxaban](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659108) should be chosen rather than [dabigatran](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659087) or [edoxaban](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659219). DOACs are not recommended for VTE treatment in the acute setting following bariatric surgery due to concerns about absorption but can be considered for ongoing treatment after the initial 4 weeks of therapy; when available, [apixaban](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659135) or [rivaroxaban](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659108) trough levels can be checked to ensure they are within expected ranges. Reversal agents are available for [dabigatran](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659087) and for [apixaban](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659135), [rivaroxaban](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659108), and [edoxaban](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659219) ([Table 16–11](#papacmdt26_ch16tb11)).

**[Dabigatran](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659087)** (studied in the RE-LY trial) is superior to [warfarin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660008) at preventing stroke at the 150-mg twice-daily dose, and it is noninferior at the 110-mg twice-daily dose, although this dose is not approved for treatment of atrial fibrillation in the United States. Both doses result in *less* intracranial hemorrhage than [warfarin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660008) but also in *more* GI bleeding than [warfarin.](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660008) When oral anticoagulation with either [dabigatran](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659087) or [warfarin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660008) was stopped for elective or emergency procedures or surgery in the RE-LY trial, the risk of bleeding was numerically lower with [dabigatran](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659087) than with [warfarin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660008). [Dabigatran](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659087) is 80% renally metabolized. The creatinine clearance should be calculated before initiating therapy. The lower dose of 75 mg twice a day is recommended in the United States for patients with creatinine clearances 15–30 mL/min, although *clinical practice guidelines recommend avoiding any of the DOACs in patients with an estimated creatinine clearance < 30 mL/min* since these patients were excluded from the clinical trials. The exception is [apixaban](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659135); patients with estimated creatinine clearance down to 25 mL/min were included, and patients with creatinine clearance of 25–30 mL/min did well with [apixaban](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659135) versus [warfarin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660008). There is no widely available test to accurately measure the effect of [dabigatran](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659087), although the aPTT is affected by [dabigatran](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659087) and a normal aPTT suggests little if any [dabigatran](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659087) effect. Patients may be converted from [warfarin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660008) to [dabigatran](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659087) by stopping the [warfarin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660008) and beginning [dabigatran](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659087) once the INR is ≤ 2.0, and this is a reasonable approach for transition from [warfarin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660008) to any of the DOACs. Neither [dabigatran](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659087) nor any of the DOACs should be used in patients with mechanical prosthetic heart valves where the medications are less effective and riskier.

**[Rivaroxaban](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659108)** is noninferior to [warfarin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660008) for stroke prevention in atrial fibrillation (in the ROCKET-AF trial). In that trial, [rivaroxaban](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659108) was compared to [warfarin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660008) for stroke prevention for patients with high-risk features of thromboembolism, with half of the patients in the ROCKET-AF trial having a history of stroke. [Rivaroxaban](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659108) is dosed at 20 mg once daily, with a reduced dose (15 mg/day) for patients with creatinine clearances between 15 and 50 mL/min. It should be *administered with food*, since that results in a 40% higher drug absorption. As with [dabigatran](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659087), there is substantially less risk of intracranial hemorrhage with [rivaroxaban](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659108) than [warfarin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660008).

**[Apixaban](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659135)** is more effective than [warfarin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660008) at stroke prevention while having a substantially lower risk of major bleeding (in the ARISTOTLE trial) and a lower risk of all-cause mortality. The [apixaban](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659135) dosage is 5 mg twice daily or 2.5 mg twice daily for patients with two of three high-risk criteria (age 80 years or older, body weight 60 kg or less, and serum creatinine of 1.5 mg/dL or more). [Apixaban](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659135) is associated with less intracranial hemorrhage and is well tolerated. [Apixaban](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659135) was also shown to be superior to [aspirin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660322) (and better tolerated, with comparable rates of bleeding) in the AVERROES trial of patients deemed not suitable for [warfarin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660008). Patients on hemodialysis were excluded from the pivotal DOAC trials; however, [apixaban](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659135) has been studied in a small trial of patients receiving hemodialysis, with pharmacokinetic studies suggesting that a dose of 2.5 mg twice daily in these patients results in equivalent serum concentrations as 5 mg twice daily in patients with normal kidney function.
