## Answer

 ## Aortic stenosis (AS)

**Aortic stenosis** is obstruction to left-ventricular outflow caused by narrowing of the aortic valve. It produces a systolic pressure gradient between the left ventricle and aorta. Over time, the left ventricle typically develops concentric hypertrophy; later, diastolic dysfunction, systolic dysfunction, myocardial fibrosis, and reduced cardiac output may occur. (1)

### Symptoms

Patients may remain asymptomatic for years, even with severe disease. The classic symptoms are:

- **Exertional dyspnea**
- **Angina**
- **Exertional syncope**

Fatigue and reduced exercise tolerance may develop gradually. Orthopnea, pulmonary edema, pulmonary hypertension, right-sided heart failure, and atrial fibrillation are generally late findings. Once symptoms develop—or if the left-ventricular ejection fraction falls below normal—valve replacement is indicated. (2)

### Severity assessment

Severity is assessed using Doppler echocardiography, including transvalvular velocity, pressure gradient, and aortic valve area:

- **Mild:** valve area 1.6–2.0 cm²  
- **Moderate:** 1.0–1.5 cm²  
- **Severe:** <1.0 cm²  
- **Critical:** <0.6 cm²  

A mean systolic gradient >40 mmHg with normal cardiac output or an effective valve area approximately <1.0 cm² generally indicates severe obstruction. Low-flow, low-gradient AS can be diagnostically challenging, particularly when ventricular function is reduced. (3) (1) (4)

### Diagnosis

Transthoracic echocardiography typically demonstrates:

- Thickened and calcified valve leaflets
- Reduced systolic leaflet opening
- Left-ventricular hypertrophy
- Increased transaortic velocity and gradient

Electrocardiography may show left-ventricular hypertrophy and, in advanced disease, ST-segment depression and T-wave inversion, although a normal ECG does not exclude severe AS. Dobutamine stress echocardiography may help assess low-flow, low-gradient AS with reduced ventricular function. CT assessment of aortic-valve calcium may help when severity remains uncertain. (3)

### Treatment

For symptomatic severe AS, valve replacement is generally indicated. Options include:

- **Surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR)**
- **Transcatheter aortic valve implantation/replacement (TAVI/TAVR)**

Choice depends on age, life expectancy, valve anatomy, operative risk, comorbidities, frailty, concurrent cardiac disease, and patient preferences. The provided **Table 272-3** summarizes factors favoring SAVR, TAVI, or palliation. (5) (6)

For asymptomatic severe disease, follow-up echocardiography is listed in **Table 272-2**:

- Every **6–12 months** when maximum velocity is >4 m/s
- Every **1–2 years** for moderate AS
- Every **3–5 years** for mild AS (3)
