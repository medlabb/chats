## 32.7 Treatment of Borderline Personality Disorder

All health care professionals clinically manage patients with borderline personality disorder (BPD) in the routine delivery of medical, surgical, and mental health services. The emotional reactivity, self-destructive impulsivity, relational dysfunction, and stress-induced departures from realistic thinking that define BPD can overwhelm, destabilize, or frustrate the efforts of clinicians to provide standard-of-care interventions. Understanding that these are symptoms of a common, disabling, but treatable disorder can enhance health care providers’ likelihood of adhering to best practices in their domain. However, adequate education and training for the recognition and management of BPD are not widely available, even for mental health clinicians.

Empirically validated manualized psychotherapies for BPD have revolutionized attitudes toward this highly stigmatized, once thought to be untreatable, illness associated with severe morbidity, mortality, and costs to society. These *specialized* therapies for BPD, most notably dialectical behavioral therapy (DBT), mentalization-based treatment (MBT), and transference-focused psychotherapy (TFP), yield comparable effects. No pharmacotherapy has shown significant, consistent superiority over a placebo; therefore, there are no Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved medications for BPD treatment. There is a significant disparity between the short supply of therapists trained in these specialized therapy approaches and the demand created by the high prevalence of patients with BPD seeking treatment, especially in acute mental and general health care environments. The intensive, complex, and lengthy formats of these treatments limit broad implementation outside specialist programs.

Clinical professionals of all disciplines, as well as students and trainees, need pragmatic tools that distill up-to-date knowledge about what works for the treatment of BPD and how that knowledge can be applied in cross-sectional, brief, or acute care situations. To meet this need, this chapter will review common factors from prevailing specialized psychotherapies that clarify BPD’s features in constructive terms. Since most clinicians, even in mental health, will not deliver these specialist therapies, we will also describe good psychiatric management (GPM), a generalist approach that medicalizes BPD as a treatable diagnosis to create objective ways of discussing its symptoms and emphasizes tasks such as diagnostic disclosure, psychoeducation, goal setting, management of safety and comorbidities, and conservative prescribing that most clinicians use for the treatment of other disorders. To further relate GPM’s approach to common clinical scenarios that most medical trainees and practitioners will encounter, GPM’s applications to inpatient psychiatric units, emergency departments (EDs), and general health care settings will be discussed.

Crisis planning should involve the patient as an active participant in creating a crisis plan at the beginning of treatment rather than as a crisis is unfolding. Safety plans for suicidal crises include identifying warning signs for a suicidal crisis, reduction of access to lethal means, coping strategies that the patient can use independently, ideas for social interaction to reduce isolation, and contact information for both supportive interpersonal relationships and professionals. In GPM, the patient and clinician collaboratively develop alternative ways to cope with unbearable emotions, with their intolerance of aloneness and interpersonal hypersensitivity in mind. The patient is thus encouraged to recruit additional social support to be available when they are feeling distressed or alone and also discouraged from engaging in relationships that are likely to be rejecting during crises. Actively involving the patient in the safety planning process, rather than asking them to passively agree to a “safety contract,” promotes a sense of agency and autonomy.

The suicidal or self-injurious patient predictably creates high anxiety in the clinician. Clinicians are therefore strongly encouraged to seek support, supervision, and consultation from colleagues. It is also important for a clinician treating BPD to be open and clear about their limits. No clinician is omniscient, omnipresent, or omnipotent, and it is therefore unreasonable for a patient’s safety to depend on the clinician’s availability. Having a plan that allows for different means of coping and gaining social support is most empowering to the patient, and their ability to rely on themselves to manage their safety over time is a constructive goal for treatment.

Psychopharmacology. Medications are adjunctive to psychosocial interventions for BPD. Medications can be helpful when they are targeted to specific symptoms (e.g., antipsychotics/mood stabilizers for anger, mood instability) or comorbidity (e.g., selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors for depression), but no medication is uniformly or consistently helpful, nor has been approved by the U.S. FDA for the treatment of BPD. Polypharmacy may increase the risk for side effects, promote dependency, and send the message that the patient can expect to improve by taking medications alone, as opposed to mobilizing to build a more satisfactory life. Due to the tendency to accumulate polypharmacy, especially with repeated hospitalizations, “collaborative deprescribing” or judicious and deliberate shared efforts with patients to taper medications when they are not working or reducing doses when possible, can become the focus of a treatment plan while inpatient or in primary care and outpatient psychiatric settings. Benzodiazepines should be avoided as they are habit forming and reinforce the belief of individuals with BPD that their emotions are unbearable and unmanageable.

<small>ALLIANCE.</small>

Effective alliance building often includes (1) displaying concerned attention, (2) active listening, (3) validation of painful experiences, and (4) demonstrating one’s helpfulness through providing effective and practical assistance. GPM principles discourage overemphasis on rescuing a patient and splitting (both where the patient views part of the team as good and the others as bad, as well as where part of the team views the patient as innocent while others view them as problematic). According to GPM, a strong alliance is “real” (two sided), which includes (1) owning one’s mistakes, (2) limit setting around unrealistic expectations, and (3) using self-disclosure of one’s emotional reactions to the patient’s behavior when appropriate. Grounding the terms and expectations of the roles of the patient and clinician by focusing on the diagnoses, treatment plans, and immediate goals at hand can provide focus or organization to the interaction. Making recommendations with a collaborative effort to understand risks and benefits, even if repeated like a broken record, is what clinicians can do in their role. The patient has the choice to follow them or not. The use of case management services to reduce obstacles may assist the patient in reducing barriers to follow through.

<small>MEDICATION MANAGEMENT.</small>

There are a number of potential pitfalls with medication management that most medical and surgical professionals may not have been trained to know. First, it is important for both the care team and the patient to know that there are no FDA-approved medications for BPD, and many medications come with more risks than benefits. Low-dose antipsychotics can be helpful on a short-term basis for psychotic-like symptoms (paranoia, extreme agitation, hallucinations). In order to avoid reinforcing the idea that medications are first-line rescue interventions for distressing symptoms, anxiolytic medications can be prescribed as standing orders rather than as needed. Similarly, while non–habit-forming medications that promote sleep may reduce vulnerability factors, behavioral interventions are sufficient for some patients. If medication is necessary, the clinician can explain that medication for sleep promotion is a short-term intervention rather than a long-term treatment.

Antidepressants and mood stabilizers may be useful in the case of untreated comorbidity (e.g., mania) but are not the intervention of choice for BPD symptoms. If short-term medications have been used during hospitalization or medical treatment, GPM recommends that they be discontinued at discharge to avoid polypharmacy. Patients with BPD are at high risk of misusing analgesic medications, and referrals to structured pain management programs or expedited appointments with the primary care physician should be made as needed. The standard as-needed medications for mild symptoms such as constipation, nausea, indigestion, and headaches can be given if these symptoms are related to the underlying illness that requires hospitalization but minimizing their use where possible is encouraged to mitigate overreliance on medication.

#### The Role of Medication in the Treatment of Personality Disorder

Medications are often helpful, but they always carry some risk of side effects, and the advantages and disadvantages must be carefully weighed to respect the patient’s wishes and to help them be calm and organized enough for further growth in self-awareness. Medication never makes people more self-aware, which requires learning but can help create more calm and stable conditions for doing psychotherapy. In general, the use of medications should be considered temporary to stabilize the patient and then tapered as awareness and self-regulation improve. The complexity of psychopathology in patients with PD requires the psychiatrist to remain vigilant to idiosyncratic and unpredictable reactions, a high prevalence of side effects, a tendency toward impatience for benefits, and the loss of calm judgment resulting in impulsive decisions or prescribing in desperation.

During the initial stage of treating patients with PDs, medications are often used to target specific symptoms with the goals of relieving subjective distress, risky or self-destructive behaviors, and/or conflict with others, thereby paving the way for later stages of treatment that require calmness, safety, and a reduction in defensiveness, to facilitate growth in self-awareness.

Symptomatic pharmacotherapy is not focused on the individual subtypes of PD but rather on the following four symptom domains shared by all subtypes: (i) mood and anxiety dysregulation, related most strongly to Harm Avoidance, (ii) aggression and impulse control, related most strongly to Novelty Seeking, (iii) social and emotional detachment, related most strongly to Reward Dependence, and (iv) psychotic symptoms and cognitive distortions, related most strongly to intellectual reasoning and Persistence.
