The detection of metastases should not preclude surgery to remove the primary tumor in patients with tumor-related symptoms such as gastrointestinal bleeding or obstruction but usually prompts the initiation of chemotherapy unless those symptoms are viewed to be life-threatening. Studies have demonstrated that resection of the primary tumor in the setting of unresectable metastatic disease does not improve overall survival and should be done only on an ad hoc basis.

Patients with MSI-high colon cancer should be treated with a checkpoint inhibitor as initial treatment. Long-term survival is possible in these patients, and some will have a complete response and may not ever have recurrence of their cancer. For the vast majority of patients with MSS colorectal adenocarcinoma who are not candidates for complete resection of metastatic disease, treatment with systemic chemotherapy is associated with marked improvement in overall survival compared with palliative care alone. A patient with metastatic disease lives on average for 2–3 years with systemic chemotherapy, and as many as 25% of patients will live for at least 5 years on and off chemotherapy.

The mainstays of therapy for patients with metastatic colon cancer are the following medications: 5-fluorouracil (5-FU; or [capecitabine](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660663)), [irinotecan](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660590), [oxaliplatin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=658854), and [bevacizumab](/drug.aspx?gbosId=658910). These medications are used in combination all together (e.g., FOLIRINOX-bevacizumab or FOLFOXIRI-bevacizumab) or in partial combination (e.g., FOLFOX-bevacizumab, FOFIRI-bevacizumab). 5-FU is an inhibitor of thymidylate synthase and is used in conjunction with folinic acid ([leucovorin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660041)). It is metabolized by dihydropyrimidine dehydrogenase (DPD), which is deficient in ~1% of adults. Individuals with DPD deficiency can experience increased side effects from 5-FU, especially myelosuppression and mucositis. Testing for DPD gene mutations may identify patients at high risk of DPD deficiency and toxicity from 5-FU. [Irinotecan](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660590) is a topoisomerase I inhibitor and is metabolized by glucuronidation in the liver. Thus, patients with *UGT* mutations can experience decreased clearance and enhanced toxicity, principally myelosuppression. [Oxaliplatin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=658854) is a platinum analogue that is metabolized generally and can cause permanent peripheral neuropathy. [Bevacizumab](/drug.aspx?gbosId=658910) is a monoclonal antibody against vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF). Side effects of [bevacizumab](/drug.aspx?gbosId=658910) include hypertension, nephrotic syndrome, and impaired wound healing. Untreated hypertension from [bevacizumab](/drug.aspx?gbosId=658910) has been associated with posterior reversible leukoencephalopathy syndrome.

Other agents with proven disease-altering effects in the metastatic setting include trifluridine-tipiracil, [regorafenib](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659147), and [fruquintinib](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659705). [Trifluridine](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659254) is a nucleoside analogue that is used with tipiracil, a thymidine phosphorylase inhibitor. [Regorafenib](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659147) is an oral multitargeted tyrosine kinase inhibitor, including VEGF. [Fruquintinib](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659705) is an oral VEFG tyrosine kinase inhibitor.

The main side effects of these two antibodies are the class effects of diarrhea and acneiform rash. [Cetuximab](/drug.aspx?gbosId=658909), which is partially murine, is also associated with allergic reactions.

Patients who harbor *BRAF* V600E mutations may have a particularly virulent form of colon cancer that does not respond as well to standard chemotherapy. These mutations tend to occur more commonly in right-sided tumors and women. They often involve methylation of *MLH1* and can be MMR deficient. The BRAF inhibitor [encorafenib](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659374) is approved for use with [cetuximab](/drug.aspx?gbosId=658909) in patients with *BRAF*-mutant colorectal cancer.

HER2 overexpression can be seen in colorectal cancer, and consideration of anti-HER2–targeted therapy is warranted. [Tucatinib](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659493) (HER2 tyrosine kinase inhibitor) and [trastuzumab](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659501) (anti-HER2 monoclonal antibody) are approved in the United States for patients with HER2-positive colorectal cancers that lack a *RAS* mutation.

Rarely, *RET* fusions and *TRK* fusions can be seen in patients with metastatic colorectal cancer. These patients can have tremendous responses to the appropriate RET and TRK inhibitors with marked prolongation of survival.
