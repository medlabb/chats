In addition to showing NMDA receptor antagonism, amantadine is also a sigma-1 agonist, which may potentially play a role in its effects on parkinsonian symptoms and its antiviral properties. Amantadine has also shown neuroprotective properties via induced expression of glial-cell–derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF) or other antioxidative or anti-inflammatory avenues.

#### Design and Interpretation of Clinical Trials

Amantadine has been used as an antiviral drug for more than 50 years and was initially developed to fight influenza caused by type A influenza viruses. However, widespread drug resistance has reduced its popularity to such an extent that its use is no longer recommended. Reports of antiparkinsonian effects were observed a few years later, and the first clinical study (*n* = 163) using amantadine to treat patients with Parkinson disease was published by Schwab and colleagues in 1969 when they reported a positive outcome. Four years later, in 1973, the FDA approved the use of amantadine to treat Parkinson disease.

Since that time, many studies have compared the effectiveness of amantadine and anticholinergic agents, but to date, there are no conclusive data to establish its efficacy as a first-line agent. A double-blind study compared the effects of amantadine and biperiden in treating extrapyramidal side effects in 42 schizophrenic subjects treated with haloperidol (Haldol). Of note, the treatment effect of amantadine and biperiden was similar. This study supports the notion that amantadine is a useful alternative to anticholinergics.

In another double-blind, placebo-controlled study, amantadine was compared to trihexyphenidyl in treating drug-induced parkinsonism. The study showed that amantadine not only produced far fewer adverse effects but also was equally effective as trihexyphenidyl and is a useful alternative to atropine-like drugs. Some case reports show that amantadine may alleviate symptoms of TD. This is a controversial issue, and there are no controlled studies to replicate these results.

A relatively new form of an extended-release formulation of amantadine is available for the treatment of Parkinson disease. The initial starting dose is 137 mg and can be increased to 274 mg per day. The extended-release formulation of amantadine is effective in decreasing motor fluctuations and has a side-effect profile similar to the regularly available form of amantadine. The new extended-release formulation is taken at bedtime and is slowly released into the bloodstream overnight. As a result, the peak levels of the drug are present when it is time to wake up.

Recent studies have also looked at weight-loss potential, reversal of sexual dysfunction, and improvement in cognitive functioning by amantadine, as well as its potential neuroprotective properties and management of sequelae following cerebrovascular events like stroke or traumatic brain injuries. There is also research currently testing to see if amantadine is effective at treating COVID-19, possibly by disrupting viral replication through the viroporin E channel of the virus or agonistic behavior at sigma-1 receptors. One study assessed the weight loss potential of amantadine in patients who were treated with olanzapine (Zyprexa). Significantly fewer subjects taking amantadine gained weight compared to the placebo group. Amantadine stabilized weight in patients treated with olanzapine but did not improve fasting insulin or lipid levels. Recent studies have also found that amantadine may enhance the therapeutic effects of antidepressants. A preliminary study involving four patients with acute bipolar depression found depressive symptoms improved after 1 week of treatment. More studies are needed to confirm these results. Amantadine has a predilection for the nigrostriatal dopaminergic system compared to the mesolimbic system and enhances the CNS dopaminergic activity. This can lead to delusion, paranoia, alterations and elevation of mood, confusion, and disorientation. A few case reports suggest psychotic decompensation in patients treated with amantadine. The vast majority who are treated for extrapyramidal symptoms show a lack of psychotic relapse. Clinicians should be alerted to the fact that patients treated with amantadine are vulnerable to psychotic decompensation.
