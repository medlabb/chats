## Answer

 **Routine diagnosis of lactose intolerance in young infants should be approached cautiously.** In younger children, lactase deficiency is usually **transient and secondary to mucosal injury from enteric infection**; associated symptoms are generally mild and self-limiting, lasting several days to one or two weeks. After correction of severe dehydration, breastfeeding and the usual infant formula can generally be resumed; with mild dehydration, feeding can continue unchanged. (1)

A positive test for **lactose malabsorption does not by itself establish lactose intolerance**: malabsorption may be asymptomatic, whereas intolerance requires symptoms attributable to lactose ingestion. Testing should therefore include symptom assessment; treatment is indicated for symptomatic malabsorption, not merely malabsorption alone. (2) (3)

Regarding treatment, the selected references support **lactose reduction rather than automatic complete elimination**. Lactase preparations may be used, but their effectiveness is variable and they do not completely hydrolyze all dietary lactose. (2) (4) The provided references do **not** specifically support routine use of stool-reducing-sugar testing in young infants, nor do they provide infant-specific recommendations for starting lactose-free formula or lactase enzyme.

Prematurity is a separate context: infants born at 28–32 weeks may have developmental lactose malabsorption due to low lactase activity, although otherwise healthy infants may avoid malnutrition and diarrhea through colonic carbohydrate salvage. (5)
