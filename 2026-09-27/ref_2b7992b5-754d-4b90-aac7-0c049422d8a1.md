## 32.7 Treatment of Borderline Personality Disorder

All health care professionals clinically manage patients with borderline personality disorder (BPD) in the routine delivery of medical, surgical, and mental health services. The emotional reactivity, self-destructive impulsivity, relational dysfunction, and stress-induced departures from realistic thinking that define BPD can overwhelm, destabilize, or frustrate the efforts of clinicians to provide standard-of-care interventions. Understanding that these are symptoms of a common, disabling, but treatable disorder can enhance health care providers’ likelihood of adhering to best practices in their domain. However, adequate education and training for the recognition and management of BPD are not widely available, even for mental health clinicians.

Empirically validated manualized psychotherapies for BPD have revolutionized attitudes toward this highly stigmatized, once thought to be untreatable, illness associated with severe morbidity, mortality, and costs to society. These *specialized* therapies for BPD, most notably dialectical behavioral therapy (DBT), mentalization-based treatment (MBT), and transference-focused psychotherapy (TFP), yield comparable effects. No pharmacotherapy has shown significant, consistent superiority over a placebo; therefore, there are no Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved medications for BPD treatment. There is a significant disparity between the short supply of therapists trained in these specialized therapy approaches and the demand created by the high prevalence of patients with BPD seeking treatment, especially in acute mental and general health care environments. The intensive, complex, and lengthy formats of these treatments limit broad implementation outside specialist programs.

Clinical professionals of all disciplines, as well as students and trainees, need pragmatic tools that distill up-to-date knowledge about what works for the treatment of BPD and how that knowledge can be applied in cross-sectional, brief, or acute care situations. To meet this need, this chapter will review common factors from prevailing specialized psychotherapies that clarify BPD’s features in constructive terms. Since most clinicians, even in mental health, will not deliver these specialist therapies, we will also describe good psychiatric management (GPM), a generalist approach that medicalizes BPD as a treatable diagnosis to create objective ways of discussing its symptoms and emphasizes tasks such as diagnostic disclosure, psychoeducation, goal setting, management of safety and comorbidities, and conservative prescribing that most clinicians use for the treatment of other disorders. To further relate GPM’s approach to common clinical scenarios that most medical trainees and practitioners will encounter, GPM’s applications to inpatient psychiatric units, emergency departments (EDs), and general health care settings will be discussed.

#### Mentalization-Based Treatment of Borderline Personality Disorder

Bateman and Fonagy have developed a manualized treatment for patients with BPD with a focus on increasing mentalization. Their approach was initially studied in a partial hospitalization setting with patients randomized to a Mentalization-Based Treatment (MBT) versus a treatment-as-usual group. Patients in the MBT group showed a statistically significant reduction in depressive symptoms, suicidal and self-injurious behaviors, number of inpatient hospitalization days, and better social and interpersonal functioning. The improvement began after 6 months of treatment and was maintained following termination of treatment at 18 months. Follow-up evaluations suggested that the patients who received the MBT intervention continued to experience significant improvement relative to the control group after the treatment had ended. MBT has since been adapted to the outpatient setting consisting of one group and one individual psychotherapy session per week and can be administered by any mental health practitioner trained in MBT and supervised by experts.

According to MBT theory, mentalization is a capacity that is typically gradually acquired in the first few years of life in the setting of secure child and caregiver attachments. It entails “making sense of the actions of oneself and others on the basis of intentional mental states, such as desires, feelings, and beliefs.” Prior to the development of capacity for mentalization, children are thought to experience the world in terms of psychic equivalence. In this experience, the internal and external worlds are equivalent. In pretend mode, the mental state exists independent of external reality but separate from the rest of the child’s mental state. A playful, attentive, and sensitive caregiver who respects the thoughts, feelings, and wishes of the child as significant, while also not being on the same order as physical reality helps the child modify the psychic equivalent and pretend modes of functioning. This style of parenting eventually allows the child to develop the capacity for organizing and recognizing their internal states as internal states.

## 32.14 Mentalization-Based Treatment

### DEFINITION

Mentalization-based treatment (MBT) is an evidence-based treatment for borderline personality disorder (BPD). Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have demonstrated their effectiveness in reducing the core symptoms of BPD and it is adapted for the treatment of other conditions, including antisocial personality disorder (ASPD) and avoidant personality disorder, substance abuse, depression, and eating disorders, and complex post-traumatic stress disorder.

This chapter will outline the theoretical basis of MBT and the core treatment model in relation to BPD.

#### What Is Mentalizing?

*Mentalizing* is the social cognitive ability to understand actions of other people and oneself in terms of mental states, including thoughts, feelings, wishes, and desires. It is a very human capability that underpins everyday interactions. In nontechnical language, it is attentiveness to thinking and feeling in oneself and others. It is beyond question that mental states influence behavior. Beliefs, wishes, feelings, and thoughts, whether within or outside people’s awareness, always influence what people do. Mentalizing involves a whole spectrum of capacities: critically, this includes the ability to experience one’s *own* behavior as coherently organized by mental states and to differentiate oneself psychologically from others. These capacities are reduced in individuals with a personality disorder, who lose cognitive and emotional coherence, particularly at moments of interpersonal (relational) stress, which challenge one’s mentalizing capacities. In the authors’ view, many symptoms characteristic of BPD emerge in association with a distortion or reduction in mentalizing.

The general reduction in mentalizing capacities leads to fear of social isolation and experience of loneliness; the loss of integrated and balanced mentalizing results in a reduction in joint intentionality and a sense of togetherness, which leaves people with BPD feeling isolated from others and unable to engage in and feel part of everyday social interaction. The key to constructive prosocial interaction is the capacity to coordinate perspectives between oneself and others and thus appreciate the distinction between the subjective (one’s own view) and the objective (actual physical reality “out there”), and then intertwine these with knowledge of another individual’s mental state. Joining of minds—the so-called “we-mode”—is the crucial ingredient of social mentalizing and friendship making. The route to this, often obstructed for people with BPD, is the experience of joint attention, which defines a commonly regarded object while also acknowledging that different individuals may have different perspectives on it.

GPM is a principle-driven generalist approach to BPD grounded in the understanding of BPD as a problem of interpersonal hypersensitivity. GPM recognizes that most mental health care providers will not be trained intensively in psychotherapy, and few will have the specialized training and supervision that most evidence-based treatments for BPD require. Instead, it relies on the use of common factors in psychotherapy that most clinicians already know and use: diagnostic disclosure, psychoeducation, goal setting, multimodal treatment, crisis planning, a structured approach to psychopharmacology, managing comorbidities, and most importantly, functional rehabilitation. It is rooted in principles of the treatment of BPD shared by many other specialist and generalist treatments, substantiated by evidence from clinical trials and studies.

GPM’s interpersonal coherence model ([Fig. 32.7–2](#ff32-7-2)) provides a helpful roadmap to navigating the fluctuating states of an individual with BPD. It is another variant of a healing paradigm that allows the patient and clinician to discuss symptomatic events. Due to their interpersonal hypersensitivity, individuals with BPD are most stable and collaborative when they feel connected to someone who provides care in a way that makes them feel worthy. Their sense of self-worth is often rooted in the status of their exclusive relationships, leaving them intensely vulnerable and fearful of abandonment. In order to avoid abandonment, patients with BPD present as compliant and idealizing in a way that optimizes their existing capacities for collaboration. When interpersonal stressors invariably arise, patients enter a threatened state demonstrating anger amidst abrupt transitions between idealization and devaluation. In threatened states, people with BPD act aggressively toward caregivers and other important figures and frequently express this distress and anger toward themselves in the form of self-harm. Although threatened, patients are still desperately attempting to reinstate connection with important others.

Anger, cycling between idealization and devaluation, and self-harming behaviors predictably evoke fear, confusion, and anger in others, and often precipitate the very abandonment that people with BPD fear. As others withdraw, the patient with BPD enters a state of aloneness. In alone states, people with BPD lack the interpersonal anchor they rely on and become disoriented both cognitively and behaviorally. Here, patients with BPD are paranoid, disconnected from reality, recklessly impulsive and disregarding of consequences, and descend into the most unstable state of despair. Without the holding or grounding influences of others, people with BPD lose reasons to live and become more dangerously suicidal. At this point, concerned others may intervene unilaterally, putting patients in the hospital when suicidal, or prison when recklessly impulsive, in order to create a holding environment that reconstitutes either the connected or threatened state once again. The revival of interpersonal contact stabilizes the patient with BPD temporarily and while it may be necessary for short-term safety, without attention to the repeated patterns of interpersonal hypersensitivity underlying symptomatic states, this pattern will continue. Clinicians can expect that an individual with BPD will transition through the different states of this model. They can use the GPM model to understand how BPD symptoms manifest themselves in each state as well as how external factors, including the provider’s own attitude and actions influence the patient and their reactions to care. Over time, this model can become a shared and explicit tool for both provider and patient.

Managing Co-occurring Disorders. Personality pathology should be prioritized in cases where the comorbid disorder is unlikely to remit otherwise or does not preclude learning. These disorders include major depressive disorder (MDD), non-manic bipolar disorder, panic disorder, bulimia, and post-traumatic stress disorder related to trauma that occurs in adult life. Recent findings suggest the presence of a substance use disorder does not impact the efficacy of brief GPM. However, comorbidities that interfere with active learning, including psychosis, manic states, or anorexia nervosa, should be prioritized before use of BPD treatment.

Duration and Intensity. GPM is distinguished by its relative flexibility about the length of treatment recommended and the intensity of the treatment as reflected in frequency of visits. In this regard, GPM differs from the other empirically validated treatments that require a set number of visits weekly and/or an expected duration of treatment. Greater flexibility allows for treatment of patients with BPD symptoms with a range of severity of illness and schedules. This means that relatively stable patients who are not in crisis, for example, may be seen sporadically, while those patients with prominent active difficulties can be seen more frequently as determined by the therapist. In situations where treatment is offered to patients who may not have a set schedule, such as college students who are away for vacations or summer break, this flexibility can allow for good enough treatment.

Treatment should continue only if the patient and therapist feel that progress toward stated goals is being made. The patient and provider should actively evaluate the benefit of the intervention. The idea that any medical intervention should only be continued if it is working frames the treatment as useful if improvement occurs, rather than solely required due to crises or desperate need. Patients vary in their readiness for and responsiveness to treatments, so if this occurs with generalist treatment, a referral for a different, more intensive treatment where available should be considered.

Inpatient Management of BPD. Patients with BPD constitute a large proportion of all psychiatric admissions (20% to 25%). These patients tend to spend more time in inpatient settings than many other disorders (14.3 weeks of hospitalization on average, compared to 2.4 weeks for patients with MDD) and have nearly five times as many separate hospitalizations. Given these facts, it is important that inpatient units are prepared to treat this patient population, but they are often not provided guidance on how to do so since most evidence-based treatments for BPD are not designed for implementation at this level of care. Hospitalization prevents unsafe action but often does not address the relationship between the patient’s BPD and their presenting problem. Hospitalization can become addictive when patients view it as necessary to their basic survival, and it serves a secondary function of separating the patient from interpersonal stressors and unwanted responsibilities in the patient’s life outside the hospital. To mitigate the harmful cycle of recurrent hospitalizations, inpatient units can implement the following basic principles: diagnostic disclosure and psychoeducation, structure, focusing on the patient’s life stressors, and aftercare planning. <small>ALLIANCE.</small>

Effective alliance building often includes (1) displaying concerned attention, (2) active listening, (3) validation of painful experiences, and (4) demonstrating one’s helpfulness through providing effective and practical assistance. GPM principles discourage overemphasis on rescuing a patient and splitting (both where the patient views part of the team as good and the others as bad, as well as where part of the team views the patient as innocent while others view them as problematic). According to GPM, a strong alliance is “real” (two sided), which includes (1) owning one’s mistakes, (2) limit setting around unrealistic expectations, and (3) using self-disclosure of one’s emotional reactions to the patient’s behavior when appropriate. Grounding the terms and expectations of the roles of the patient and clinician by focusing on the diagnoses, treatment plans, and immediate goals at hand can provide focus or organization to the interaction. Making recommendations with a collaborative effort to understand risks and benefits, even if repeated like a broken record, is what clinicians can do in their role. The patient has the choice to follow them or not. The use of case management services to reduce obstacles may assist the patient in reducing barriers to follow through.

<small>MEDICATION MANAGEMENT.</small>

There are a number of potential pitfalls with medication management that most medical and surgical professionals may not have been trained to know. First, it is important for both the care team and the patient to know that there are no FDA-approved medications for BPD, and many medications come with more risks than benefits. Low-dose antipsychotics can be helpful on a short-term basis for psychotic-like symptoms (paranoia, extreme agitation, hallucinations). In order to avoid reinforcing the idea that medications are first-line rescue interventions for distressing symptoms, anxiolytic medications can be prescribed as standing orders rather than as needed. Similarly, while non–habit-forming medications that promote sleep may reduce vulnerability factors, behavioral interventions are sufficient for some patients. If medication is necessary, the clinician can explain that medication for sleep promotion is a short-term intervention rather than a long-term treatment.

Antidepressants and mood stabilizers may be useful in the case of untreated comorbidity (e.g., mania) but are not the intervention of choice for BPD symptoms. If short-term medications have been used during hospitalization or medical treatment, GPM recommends that they be discontinued at discharge to avoid polypharmacy. Patients with BPD are at high risk of misusing analgesic medications, and referrals to structured pain management programs or expedited appointments with the primary care physician should be made as needed. The standard as-needed medications for mild symptoms such as constipation, nausea, indigestion, and headaches can be given if these symptoms are related to the underlying illness that requires hospitalization but minimizing their use where possible is encouraged to mitigate overreliance on medication.
