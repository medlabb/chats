*Others.* There is some evidence to suggest that trazodone may be helpful in decreasing irritability, anxiety and restlessness, and affective disturbance, especially in patients with FTD. Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors and memantine have been shown to have little benefit in the treatment of agitation and psychosis. Pimavanserin, which is a selective 5-HT<sub>2A</sub> receptor inverse agonist and antagonist, demonstrated potential efficacy in the treatment of psychotic symptoms in Alzheimer dementia in a randomized placebo-controlled study, with there being a larger treatment effect shown in patients with more severe psychotic symptoms at baseline. The benefit of treatment compared to placebo was significant at 6 weeks but not at 12 weeks. A Cochrane review showed potential benefits of methylphenidate in the improvement of apathy in dementia, as well as possible improvement of cognition and instrumental activities of daily living. There is some evidence to support the use of prazosin for the treatment of behavioral symptoms in patients with agitation or aggression, and there is currently an ongoing multicenter trial evaluating the use of prazosin in adults with Alzheimer dementia. Treatment with the combination dextromethorphan-quinidine resulted in a significant decrease in agitation and aggression in one randomized placebo-controlled trial in patients with probable Alzheimer dementia. A 2020 systematic review and meta-analysis examining the use of cannabinoids, including tetrahydrocannabinol, dronabinol, and nabilone, for the treatment of neuropsychiatric symptoms was supportive of the efficacy of these agents and found them to be well tolerated. Minimal evidence supports the use of beta-blockers or lithium (Eskalith), and both classes of agents can have significant adverse events that preclude recommending them for use in a frail older adults population. There is minimal evidence that buspirone (Buspar) may be efficacious in the treatment of agitation or anxiety in dementia.

Depression. Several antidepressants have been studied for the treatment of depressive symptoms in patients with Alzheimer disease. SSRIs have been the most studied, as there are clinical concerns regarding the tolerability and risk profile of tricyclics and monoamine oxidase inhibitors. A 2018 Cochrane review was not supportive of the efficacy of antidepressants for the treatment of depression in dementia. Similarly, a 2011 review of seven studies with 330 participants found that while two studies found antidepressants to have a benefit over placebo, overall, efficacy was only suggested and not confirmed. There is no data clearly favoring any specific antidepressant; however, because of its very long half-life in the older adults, fluoxetine (Prozac) is not generally recommended. There are no controlled trials of electroconvulsive therapy in the depression of dementia.

Psychotic symptoms are common in Parkinson disease, with an estimated prevalence of around 60%, with visual hallucinations being the most common. Psychosis is more common as the disease progresses and is associated with comorbidities, including cognitive impairment, mood disorders, and sleep disorders. The first step in addressing psychotic symptoms is to rule out any concurrent processes that could cause worsened cognition or delirium (e.g., infections; anticholinergic medications). These syndromes may respond to lowering anti-Parkinson drug doses. If not, or if motor disability worsens to an unacceptable degree, atypical antipsychotic drugs with less impact on nigrostriatal dopaminergic pathways can be helpful. Pimavanserin [Nuplazid], a 5HTa inverse agonist approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) to treat Parkinson disease psychosis, has been found to be effective and well tolerated. It is unique in its status as an antipsychotic without dopamine-receptor–blocking properties, and given this different mechanism of action, the time to response is significantly longer—on the order of 4 to 6 weeks. Clozapine (Clozaril) and quetiapine (Seroquel) are the most extensively studied antipsychotics, and though clozapine is consistently efficacious, quetiapine is usually prescribed first because of the risk of agranulocytosis and the requirement for frequent blood monitoring with clozapine. Positive response to clozapine can occur at low doses and in timeframes as short as one week. Motor deterioration in a significant percentage of patients has been observed with olanzapine (Zyprexa), risperidone (Risperdal), ziprasidone (Geodon), and aripiprazole (Abilify). Cholinesterase inhibitors may also be beneficial in reducing hallucinations, though the data supporting this assertion is sparse.

Major neurocognitive disorder due to Parkinson disease is more commonly seen in those with more advanced disease and more advanced age. The affected cognitive domains include attention and visuospatial functions, with language functions being relatively preserved. Behavioral and neuropsychiatric symptoms, including those discussed above, are nearly always present. The currently available evidence supports the use of cholinesterase inhibitors in these patients, with a positive impact on global assessment, cognitive function, behavioral disturbance, and activities of daily living rating scales. The rivastigmine (Exelon) transdermal patch is often better tolerated than oral formulations, though exacerbation of tremor remains a concern. Response to memantine (Namenda) in this disorder has been disappointing.

In Alzheimer disease, depression affects up to one-third of patients, especially in milder stages, and may be the major presenting problem. Despite their widespread use, antidepressants lack clear evidence for this patient population. They are also associated with significant adverse effects, including falls, hyponatremia, cardiotoxicity, and increased bleeding risk. Trials of antidepressants may be best reserved for those with historical responses to antidepressants or those with moderate or severe depression who have not responded to behavioral interventions. As always, risks and benefits should be carefully weighed. Apathy is even more common than depression in major neurocognitive disorder due to Alzheimer disease, but similarly, antidepressants and other commonly used agents haven’t proven to be effective. Methylphenidate may be beneficial, but the quality of current evidence is low. With the progression of dementia, classical presentations of depression become less frequent, while agitation, psychosis, and aggressive behavior with or without dysphoria become more common. Mixed behavioral syndromes are the rule in middle and later-stage dementia. Assaultive or threatening behavior demands thorough medical and socioenvironmental assessment to identify precipitating and aggravating factors, but pharmacologic interventions are often required. Second-generation antipsychotics are commonly used off-label in these cases, but their use has been controversial since black box labeling by the FDA. Recent reviews conclude that the use of these agents confers modest benefits for short-term treatment of aggression and psychosis as well as a small but significant benefit on caregiver burden. This is offset, however, by the substantial risk of serious adverse events, including 1.6 to 1.7 times increased mortality risk. Trials of valproate for these dementia-related behaviors have been disappointing. Existing evidence for the use of SSRIs for the treatment of agitation associated with Alzheimer disease is promising but limited. Further high-quality research is also needed on the use of cognitive enhancers for behavioral and psychological symptoms of dementia. Delirium induced by drugs or acute medical illness is a risk at all stages and in all types of dementias, as brain compensatory mechanisms are impaired.
