##### Interventional Treatment

Interventional treatment is an option for selected patients with acute ischemic stroke, but not TIA. Current guidelines regarding the timing of therapy in relation to stroke onset are summarized in [Table 13-5](#greenberg11_ch13tb5).

1. **Intravenous thrombolysis—**Intravenous administration of [alteplase](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660465) within 4.5 hours of the onset of symptoms (or within 9 hours if imaging studies demonstrate diffusion-perfusion mismatch) can reduce disability and mortality from acute ischemic stroke. Secondary prevention (ie, prevention of a subsequent cerebrovascular event in patients with prior TIA or ischemic stroke) involves measures similar to those employed in primary prevention, with the following exceptions.




##### Statins

Treatment with a statin (eg, atorvastatin 80 mg orally daily) is recommended for all patients with prior TIA or ischemic stroke.


##### Blood Pressure Control

Angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitors (or perhaps angiotensin receptor blockers) and diuretics appear to be most effective in reducing the risk of recurrent stroke.


##### Antiplatelet Drugs

Patients with prior non-cardioembolic TIA or ischemic stroke should receive [aspirin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660322) (81-325 mg/d), with or without a 21-day course of [clopidogrel](/drug.aspx?gbosId=658818) (600 mg, then 75 mg/d for 20 days); [aspirin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660322)/[dipyridamole](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660297) (25/200 mg twice daily); or [clopidogrel](/drug.aspx?gbosId=658818) (75 mg/d) alone. For patients whose TIA or stroke occurred while taking [aspirin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660322), it is unclear if increasing the dose or substituting another antiplatelet drug confers additional benefit. There is no evidence that anticoagulation or the combination of antiplatelet therapy and anticoagulation is effective in this setting.


##### Anticoagulation

Patients with prior TIA or ischemic stroke and **valvular atrial fibrillation** (atrial fibrillation with moderate to severe mitral stenosis or a mechanical heart valve) or **mechanical aortic or mitral valve replacement** without atrial fibrillation should be given long-term [warfarin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660008) treatment targeted to an INR of 2.5 ± 0.5. Low-dose [aspirin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660322) is added for patients with mechanical valves who are at low risk for bleeding complications. **Nonvalvular atrial fibrillation** should be treated with [apixaban](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659135), [dabigatran](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659087), [rivaroxaban](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659108), [edoxaban](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659219), or [warfarin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660008) (INR 2.5 ± 0.5). Short-term (~3 months) anticoagulation with [warfarin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660008) is indicated for prior TIA or ischemic stroke with **acute myocardial infarction** or **cardiomyopathy** when either is complicated by mural thrombus. [Warfarin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660008) for 3-6 months followed by long-term low-dose [aspirin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660322) is recommended for recipients of **bioprosthetic valves**. Either antiplatelet drugs or anticoagulation with [warfarin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660008) can be used in **rheumatic valvular disease** without atrial fibrillation.


##### Surgical Treatment

**Carotid endarterectomy** or **stenting** for secondary prevention of TIA or stroke is as described earlier for treatment of TIA or acute ischemic stroke.

##### Infective Endocarditis

Infective (bacterial or fungal) endocarditis is associated with a 1-year mortality of ~30% and a 20-40% incidence of stroke, typically within the middle cerebral artery distribution. Factors that predispose to infectious endocarditis include intravenous drug use, hemodialysis, intravenous catheterization, valvular heart disease, and prosthetic heart valves. *Staphylococcus aureus*, *Streptococcus viridans*, coagulase-negative *Staphylococcus*, and *Enterococcus* species are the most common organisms. Fungal endocarditis is rare, is usually caused by *Candida* or *Aspergillus*, and has a worse prognosis.

The risk of embolization is highest with mitral valve involvement, large (>10-15 mm) valvular vegetations, and fastidious gram-negative organisms. Infectious endocarditis can cause cardioembolic stroke or intracerebral or subarachnoid hemorrhage from rupture of a **mycotic aneurysm**, which are most common before or soon after the onset of antibiotic treatment.

Signs of infective endocarditis (**[Figure 13-17](#greenberg11_ch13fg17)**) include heart murmur, petechiae, subungual splinter hemorrhages, Osler nodes (painful red or purple digital nodules), Janeway lesions (red macules on the palms or soles), Roth spots (retinal red spots with white centers), and clubbing of the fingers or toes. Diagnosis is by culturing the responsible organism from the blood and imaging vegetations with echocardiography. Treatment is with antibiotics and, for heart failure, uncontrolled infection, or recurrent embolization, valve repair or replacement surgery. Anticoagulation, thrombolytics, and antiplatelet agents should be avoided for at least 2 weeks after endocarditis-related TIA or stroke because of the risk of intracranial hemorrhage.
