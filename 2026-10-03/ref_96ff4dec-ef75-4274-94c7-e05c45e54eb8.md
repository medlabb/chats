The most promising results have come from a version of CBT developed by David M. Clark and colleagues. Much of this is consistent with modern theories of CBT discussed above. One of the latest advances in this treatment is an online internet administration of much of the program, using videos, chats, and other interactive activities. Results seem to suggest excellent outcomes with less time spent by the therapist (most of which is online), though considerable patient investment in time on their own. Indeed, multiple studies of internet-based CBT have been conducted and reviewed in meta-analyses, suggesting good efficacy.

#### Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD)

The key feature of GAD is the presence of excessive worry accompanied by anxiety and physiologic symptoms of tension, irritability, and sleep disturbance. Pathologic worry, as occurs in GAD, is distinguished from normal worry in that the frequency, intensity, and duration of the worry, in addition to the attendant anxiety, greatly exceed the actual probability or impact of the feared event and that the worry is experienced as difficult to control. GAD is the least focused of anxiety disorders and has the greatest overlap with depressive disorders. GAD is one of the only psychiatric disorders with a specific cognitive criterion A variable in the DSM-5: excessive, uncontrollable worry. Individuals with GAD tend to worry about minor matters (a sometimes-distinguishing feature of GAD) as well as major ones (health, children’s future, etc.). In addition to worry, individuals with GAD tend to have physiologic responses that are less autonomic and more related to stress (e.g., sleep disturbance, muscle tension, irritability).

CBT packages for GAD have typically included training in relaxation and cognitive therapy. In addition, some packages have included some form of exposure therapy to the worry content and problem-solving. Relaxation alone, cognitive therapy alone, and their combination (with and without the further addition of exposure) have all been found to be effective in reducing worry and anxiety compared to waitlist. In addition, CBT has been shown to be more effective than supportive control conditions. One meta-analysis found that CBT and relaxation did not yield different results for GAD. Several meta-analyses and reviews have found CBT superior to waitlist and placebo conditions. One meta-analysis found that CBT effects were somewhat smaller than those for medications; another more recent review found CBT to be superior. The general results of these trials have been positive, but there is a consensus that improvements in treatment efficacy are necessary, perhaps more than in CBT for other anxiety disorders. Several attempts have been made to improve treatment outcomes for GAD. One study added more treatment sessions for individuals predicted to have poorer outcome, with nonsignificant improvement in outcome compared to standard treatment. Others attempted to modify or add to existing models of CBT. Refinement of the CBT model includes a greater focus on meta-worry (worry about worry) or a greater focus on the intolerance of uncertainty and the separation of worry into worry about current problems (addressed by problem-solving) and worry about future unknown events (addressed by increasing skills in tolerating uncertainty, including imaginal exposure). Additional areas and techniques to integrate with current CBT models include interpersonal treatment strategies, emotion regulation strategies, mindfulness, and experiential avoidance strategies, additional focus on achieving positive well-being, and schema-focused treatments. Most data to date have not shown that these techniques provide improved outcomes compared to standard CBT packages, though one study focusing on emotion regulation found larger effect sizes. As with the other disorders, internet-based treatments for GAD are also showing efficacy and great promise.

#### Other Antianxiety Medications

Beta-adrenergic antagonists, also called beta-blockers, are primarily useful as needed for performance anxiety. They block the peripheral effects of adrenaline and noradrenaline and thus reduce symptoms such as tachycardia and tremors, which patients with performance anxiety often tend to experience. Propranolol and atenolol are the commonly prescribed agents with some variability in the dosing. The dose range for propranolol is usually between 20 and 40 mg, and that for atenolol is between 25 and 50 mg. It has been suggested that targeting a heart rate reduction by 10 beats/minute and evaluating the patient’s subjective feedback on performance is a useful strategy to determine the optimum dose. While published literature indicates a marked reduction of somatic anxiety symptoms, clinical impression suggests a less robust benefit. Beta-blockers used short-term as needed are generally well tolerated, but occasional side effects include dizziness, vertigo, or sleep disturbance. Their use in patients with certain cardiac or pulmonary diseases, angle-closure glaucoma, or diabetes is usually contraindicated.

Buspirone, an azapirone, is effective in the treatment of chronic anxiety states, primarily GAD. Buspirone is usually prescribed in two or three divided doses at a dose range of 20 to 60 mg. The time course of response to buspirone is similar to that of antidepressants, and it may take time to reach full therapeutic benefit. Hydroxyzine is an antihistamine with preliminary evidence of benefit in the treatment of GAD. While initial symptom relief can be experienced within the first 15 to 20 minutes of administration, it can take up to several weeks to achieve an optimal therapeutic effect. Hydroxyzine can be used for both short-term and long-term anxiety symptoms but may lead to mild side effects, including dry mouth, sedation, and tremors. It may be a good alternative to BZDs for some patients, although tolerance to the anxiolytic effects of hydroxyzine may occur rather quickly.

#### Treatment Resistant Anxiety Disorders

Partial or nonresponse to pharmacotherapy is not uncommon with anxiety disorders. In such cases, confirmation of the diagnosis (including ruling out potentially contributing medical conditions, such as thyroid disorders), evaluating the impact of potential comorbidities (e.g., substance use), and pharmacokinetic interactions, as well as validating adequate trials (i.e., adequate dose and duration) are prudent first steps. In many patients, treatment resistance occurs even when pharmacotherapy is used in combination with CBT. Literature on treatment-resistant anxiety remains sparse, with few well-conducted clinical trials. Augmentation agents commonly used include anticonvulsants, AAPs, and BDZ. A common strategy in practice for addressing treatment-resistant GAD is the augmentation of SSRIs with pregabalin or BZDs. Despite lack of controlled trials and mixed evidence, in clinical practice, gabapentin is often used as an adjunct for refractory anxiety disorders (GAD and PD), particularly in cases of comorbid alcohol use disorder. The literature on the benefit of AAPs is mixed. Several small case series/open trials of olanzapine and aripiprazole indicate their utility of use as augmentation while data with quetiapine augmentation is mixed. With all AAPs, the risk of adverse metabolic effects needs evaluation and long-term monitoring. For treatment-resistant PD, there is evidence for adding clonazepam to an SSRI if a patient is not amenable to or suitable for CBT.

### TREATMENT OF ANXIETY DISORDERS

Both cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) and antidepressant medications are effective for treating youth anxiety disorders (e.g., GAD and SAD). Expert reviews of the literature and meta-analyses repeatedly affirm the evidence base for both interventions, whether administered alone or in combination.

The Child/Adolescent Anxiety Multimodal Study (CAMS) remains the largest double-blind, placebo-controlled trial for child and adolescent anxiety disorders. The CAMS compared the relative efficacy of CBT, sertraline, combination therapy (CBT plus sertraline), and placebo for n = 488 youth with GAD, separation anxiety disorder, and/or SAD. All active treatments were superior to placebo, and combination therapy led to significantly higher response rates than CBT or sertraline (81% vs. 60% vs. 55%, respectively). Remission rates (loss of principal diagnosis, loss of all anxiety diagnoses) ranged from 20% to 68% and followed the same patterns as response rates based on treatment condition. Factors associated with better response and remission rates in the CAMS included the absence of comorbid internalizing disorders, absence of SAD at baseline, lower baseline anxiety symptom severity, lower caregiver strain, nonminority status, and younger age. A recent paper using CAMS data goes further to suggest that youth with mild–moderate anxiety benefit most from CBT monotherapy, whereas those with more severe illnesses may be best served by beginning with combined treatment.

Beyond acute and short-term follow-up of anxious youth, there has been considerable interest in understanding how these youngsters fare over time, particularly when they have had the benefit of evidence-based treatment. To address this question, youth who participated in the CAMS trial were followed as part of a prospective longitudinal study as they entered early adulthood (Child/Adolescent Anxiety Multimodal Treatment Extended Long-term Study; CAMELS). On average, youth were recontacted approximately 6 years following their CAMS treatment and followed for another 4 years. Findings from CAMELS suggest that roughly 20% of youth treated in CAMS remained consistently remitted over time as they entered early adulthood. Roughly half exhibited a fluctuating course marked by intermittent relapse. The remaining 30% were chronically ill, with anxiety disorder evident at every time point. These findings speak to the persistent nature of anxiety and the need to consider models of care that integrate chronic disease approaches and use surveillance and booster sessions to maintain wellness.

#### Psychotherapy

Over 50 years of evidence support the effectiveness of exposure-based CBT for youth anxiety. Although there is variability across treatment protocols, they are generally linked by their focus on psychoeducation, emotion identification, somatic management techniques, cognitive restructuring, exposure tasks, and relapse prevention. Traditional individual child treatment typically involves 10 to 20 weekly treatment sessions, and numerous studies support the use of additional formats, including group, family, brief intervention, and telehealth administration.
