## Answer

 **Xerostomia** is the sensation of dry mouth, perceived when salivary flow is reduced by approximately 50%. Saliva is important for lubrication, cleansing, antimicrobial protection, buffering, chewing, speaking, and denture use. Reduced salivation increases the risk of **dental caries, periodontal disease, and difficulty chewing or swallowing**. (1)

**Common causes** include:

- Medications—especially anticholinergic drugs, but also alpha- and beta-blockers, calcium-channel blockers, and diuretics  
- Sjögren’s disease  
- Chronic parotitis or salivary duct obstruction  
- Diabetes mellitus  
- HIV/AIDS  
- Radiation involving the salivary glands  
- Other causes listed in the differential include psychotherapeutic or antihypertensive drugs, psychogenic causes, trauma, amyloidosis, and autoimmune thyroid disease. (1) (2)

**Management** includes:

- Eliminate or limit drying medications when possible  
- Maintain preventive dental care and fluoride supplementation  
- Use oral liquids, commercial saliva substitutes, or gels  
- Sugar-free mints or chewing gum may stimulate salivation in mild dysfunction  
- Pilocarpine or cevimeline may increase secretions when sufficient salivary tissue remains. (1)

Sjögren’s disease should be considered when xerostomia occurs with dry eyes, salivary-gland enlargement, or systemic manifestations. Evaluation may include medication review, salivary-flow testing or ultrasound, eye testing, autoantibody testing, and sometimes labial salivary-gland biopsy. (2) The diagnostic approach is summarized in **Figure 373-3**, and causes of xerostomia are listed in **Table 373-2**. (3) (2)

In Sjögren’s disease, oral findings may include difficulty swallowing dry food, burning mouth, increased dental caries, dry sticky mucosa, an atrophic deeply fissured tongue, and reduced or cloudy saliva. A dry, deeply fissured tongue is illustrated in **Figure 373-1**. (4)
