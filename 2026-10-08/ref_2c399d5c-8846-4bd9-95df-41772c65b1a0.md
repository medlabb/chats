# e1-08: Polymyxins

The polymyxins (**colistin** and **polymyxin B**) are basic polypeptides that are bactericidal for certain gram-negative aerobic rods, including *Pseudomonas*. Because of poor distribution into tissues and substantial toxicity (primarily nephrotoxicity and neurotoxicity), systemic use of these agents has been limited to infections caused by multidrug-resistant gram-negative organisms that are sensitive only to the polymyxins. Colistin is used for pan-resistant *Acinetobacter baumanii* and *P aeruginosa* and CRE infections. However, safer alternatives are available, such as ceftolozane-tazobactam for multidrug-resistant *P aeruginosa* and ceftazidime-avibactam or meropenem-vaborbactam for CRE infections. The safe, effective use of the polymyxins, including confusion between polymyxin B and colistin, absence of pharmaceutical standards, uncertainties regarding susceptibility testing and breakpoints, pharmacokinetics in special patient populations, and the role of combination therapy limits their utility. As such, the IDSA does not recommend polymyxins for the treatment of CRE infections, although colistin may be used as an alternative treatment for uncomplicated CRE cystitis. Baseline renal impairment and older age strongly predict AKI. Higher doses of colistin are associated with increased microbiologic success and decreased 7-day mortality; however, they are also associated with worsened kidney function. While not proven to improve clinical outcomes, some clinicians recommend capping the dose at 9 million units/daily of colistin methanesulfonate and adding a second medication such as a carbapenem.

# e1-06: Tetracycline Derivatives

**[Tigecycline,](/drug.aspx?gbosId=658949)** a glycylcycline [tetracycline](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660066) derivative, is  available as a parenteral antibacterial for the treatment of nosocomial infection. It is active against most gram-positive bacteria, including methicillin-resistant staphylococci, VRE, and many multidrug-resistant aerobic gram-negative bacilli, including *Acinetobacter*, *Enterobacter*, *Citrobacter*, and ESBL-producing *E coli* and *Klebsiella*. However, [tigecycline](/drug.aspx?gbosId=658949) lacks clinically significant activity against *Pseudomonas* and *Proteus* spp. [Tigecycline](/drug.aspx?gbosId=658949) demonstrates excellent anaerobic activity against *B fragilis* and gram-positive anaerobes. At standard dosing, a loading dose of 100 mg is administered intravenously with maintenance at 50 mg every 12 hours. The IDSA recommends an increased dose (200 mg as a loading dose, then 100 mg every 12 hours) when treating multidrug-resistant gram-negative infections. The medication distributes into deep compartments with a large volume of distribution and low serum levels. Considering the low achievable serum levels associated with [tigecycline](/drug.aspx?gbosId=658949), this agent should *not* be used in patients with bacteremia or sepsis. The medication is primarily eliminated via biliary/fecal excretion with a half-life of 30–40 hours. Dose adjustment to 25 mg every 12 hours is recommended in Child-Pugh class C liver disease. [Tigecycline](/drug.aspx?gbosId=658949) has a similar adverse effect profile as the [tetracyclines](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660066); upper GI side effects are particularly common. While approved for complicated skin and soft tissue infection and intra-abdominal infection, the spectrum of activity of [tigecycline](/drug.aspx?gbosId=658949) has resulted in its use in the treatment of certain resistant gram-negative pathogens, including ESBL- producing organisms. [Tigecycline](/drug.aspx?gbosId=658949) has been associated with *increased* mortality in ventilator-associated pneumonia when compared with [imipenem](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660434). Consequently, its use in sepsis and ventilator-associated pneumonia is not recommended. *[Tigecycline](/drug.aspx?gbosId=658949) has a boxed warning highlighting its increased all-cause mortality.* [Tigecycline](/drug.aspx?gbosId=658949) is also not indicated for treatment of diabetic foot infection or hospital-acquired pneumonia.

**[Omadacycline](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659405)** is an aminomethylcycline [tetracycline](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660066) derivative, approved for treatment of bacterial community-acquired pneumonia and acute skin and skin structure infections. Available as both a tablet and in an intravenous formulation, [omadacycline](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659405) has activity against *S aureus, S pneumoniae*, viridans group streptococci, *E coli*, *H influenzae*, *M catarrhalis*, atypical bacteria (eg, *Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Legionella pneumophilia*, nontuberculous *Mycobacteria)*, and anaerobes. [Omadacycline](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659405) is also active against the biothreat pathogens *Bacillus anthracis* and *Yersinia pestis*. Similar to [tigecycline](/drug.aspx?gbosId=658949), [omadacycline](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659405) also retains activity against drug-resistant bacteria, such as MRSA, VRE, and ESBL-producing Enterobacterales. In clinical trials, [omadacycline](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659405) was noninferior to [linezolid](/drug.aspx?gbosId=658749) for the treatment of bacterial skin and skin structure infections and noninferior to [moxifloxacin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=658882) for the treatment of community-acquired pneumonia. *[Omadacycline](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659405) has a warning for higher rates of mortality (2%) compared with [moxifloxacin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=658882) (1%) in the treatment of community-acquired pneumonia*; it is recommended that patients at a high risk (eg, older than 65 years with comorbidities) be monitored while receiving [omadacycline](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659405) for this indication. [Omadacycline](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659405) has shown promise in treating certain nontuberculous mycobacterial (NTM) infections, particularly *M abscessus*. Its oral dosage form makes it a promising option in those who require prolonged therapy, and it may be particularly useful in cases where other options are ineffective due to resistance or intolerance. The adverse effects of [omadacycline](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659405) are similar to other medications in the [tetracycline](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660066) class. Treatment with [omadacycline](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659405) requires a fixed loading dose followed by a fixed, once-daily maintenance dose. If administered orally, patients should fast for at least 4 hours prior to and 2 hours after taking [omadacycline](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659405) and that administration is separated by at least 4 hours from multivalent cations (eg, milk, antacids, [multivitamins](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660325)). One potential benefit of [omadacycline](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659405) over other antibacterials with similar spectrum of activity is that [omadacycline](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659405) does not appreciably induce *C difficile* proliferation.

## CARBAPENEMS & CARBAPENEMS IN COMBINATION WITH β-LACTAMASE INHIBITORS

This class of medications is structurally related to β-lactam antibiotics with a broad spectrum of activity that includes most gram-negative rods (including *P aeruginosa*), gram-positive organisms, and anaerobes. Carbapenems are inactive against *Burkholderia cepacia, S maltophilia, E faecium*, MRSA, and methicillin-resistant *Staphylococcus epidermidis.* There are four carbapenem antibiotics: **[imipenem](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660434), [meropenem](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660592), doripenem**, and **[ertapenem.](/drug.aspx?gbosId=658830)**

[Meropenem](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660592), doripenem, and [imipenem](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660434) have a similar spectrum of activity and pharmacology. Of particular importance, *the carbapenems are the most reliable agents in the treatment of infection secondary to ESBL-producing  E coli and Klebsiella spp*. The emergence of CRE throughout the world is of particular concern because limited options are available to treat these infections, which are associated with high morbidity and mortality. All four carbapenems have been associated with neurotoxicity, particularly seizures. [Meropenem](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660592) and doripenem are less likely to be associated with seizures when compared with [imipenem](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660434), although the risk of seizures is low with [imipenem](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660434) if dosage is appropriately adjusted for kidney dysfunction. [Meropenem](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660592) and doripenem are associated with less nausea and vomiting than [imipenem](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660434), a feature of importance when high doses must be used, as in the treatment of *Pseudomonas* infection in patients with cystic fibrosis. The usual dose for [meropenem](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660592) is 1–2 g intravenously every  8 hours. Dosage adjustment is required in kidney dysfunction. Doripenem (500 mg–1 g intravenously every 8 hours) has been used in the treatment of intra-abdominal infection and pyelonephritis, but is no longer available in some countries. In the treatment of ventilator-associated pneumonia, doripenem (administered for 7 days) was associated with increased mortality when compared with [imipenem](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660434) (administered for 10 days). [Imipenem](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660434) is coformulated with cilastatin, an enzyme that enhances the pharmacokinetics and minimizes the nephrotoxic effects of [imipenem](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660434). The dose and dosing interval of [imipenem](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660434)/cilastatin vary depending on the pathogen susceptibility profile and kidney function.

[Ertapenem](/drug.aspx?gbosId=658830) is similar to the other carbapenems in its activity against aerobic gram-positive and anaerobic organisms, but it is inactive against *Pseudomonas* and *Acinetobacter.* Because of its long half-life (4 hours), it can be administered once daily. The usual dose is 1 g intravenously every 24 hours, and adjustments are needed for kidney dysfunction.

In patients hospitalized for a prolonged period with high severity of illness or with presumed infection caused by a multidrug-resistant organism, empiric use of carbapenems is reasonable. However, [ertapenem](/drug.aspx?gbosId=658830) should not be used if *Pseudomonas* and *Acinetobacter* are suspected. *Pseudomonas* may rapidly develop resistance to carbapenems; however, combination anti-pseudomonal therapy does not decrease the rate of resistance. The use of [imipenem](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660434) or [meropenem](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660592) alone is as effective as combination therapy in patients with febrile neutropenia and certain polymicrobial infections such as peritonitis and pelvic infections.

### Treatment

The initial treatment of HAP and VAP is based on risk factors for MRSA and multiple drug-resistant pathogens ([Table 9–11](#papacmdt26_ch9tb11)) as well as local antibiograms and mortality risk and is thus empiric ([Table 9–12](#papacmdt26_ch9tb12)). The predictive capability of sets of risk factors for drug-resistant organisms in nosocomial pneumonia vary locally; strongest predictive factors include prior isolation of drug-resistant organisms, a high local prevalence of drug-resistant organisms, and antibiotic exposure within prior 90 days. Each hospital should generate antibiograms to guide the optimal choice of antibiotics with the goals of reducing exposure to unnecessary antibiotics and the development of antibiotic resistance. Because of the high mortality rate, therapy should be started as soon as HAP or VAP is suspected. After results of cultures are available, it may be possible to narrow initially broad therapy to more specific agents. Endotracheal aspiration cultures have significant negative predictive value but limited positive predictive value in the diagnosis of specific infectious causes of HAP/VAP. If an invasive diagnostic approach to suspected VAP using quantitative culture of bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL), protected specimen brush (PSB), or blind bronchial sampling (BBS) is used, antibiotics can be withheld when results are below a diagnostic threshold (BAL <10<sup>4</sup> CFU/mL, PSB or BBS <10<sup>3</sup> CFU/mL). Duration of antibiotic therapy is 7 days, consistent with clinical response, but should be individualized based on the pathogen, complications (empyema, necrotizing pneumonia), severity of illness, response to therapy, and comorbid conditions.
