## Treatment



The major goals of vitiligo treatment are stabilization of the depigmentation process, repigmentation, and maintenance of repigmentation 39a 39b . Immunomodulatory therapies are utilized to limit disease progression, with repigmentation requiring repopulation of epidermal melanocytes from neighboring pigmented hair follicles, surrounding uninvolved epidermis, and/or lesional interfollicular melanocyte precursors.

The therapeutic regimen depends on the extent, location, and activity of disease as well as the patient’s age, skin type, and motivation for treatment. In general, a period of at least 6 months is needed to determine whether a particular treatment is effective. The face, neck, mid extremities, and trunk tend to have the best response to therapy, while the distal extremities and lips are the most resistant to treatment.Repigmentation initially appears in a perifollicular pattern (unless the affected site is hairless or has depigmented hairs) and/or at the periphery of the lesions ( Fig. 66.9 ). After therapeutic repigmentation, the rate of recurrent depigmentation of vitiligo lesions is ~40% 40 .

### Photo(chemo)therapy



#### Narrowband UVB



Narrowband UVB (NB-UVB) has become first-line therapy for adults and children ≥6 years of age with generalized vitiligo 47 , especially if it involves ≥15% BSA or cosmetically sensitive areas that typically respond to treatment (see Ch. 134 ). Several studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of NB-UVB monotherapy 48 , which is superior to broadband UVB for the treatment of vitiligo.

The Vitiligo Working Group recommends initiating NB-UVB phototherapy at a dose of 200 mJ/cm 2 for all skin phototypes 49 . The dose is then increased by 10%–20% at each subsequent treatment until mild, asymptomatic lesional erythema is achieved, up to a maximum dose of 1500 mJ/cm 2 for the face and 3000 mJ/cm 2 for the body. Phototherapy is administered two to three times per week on non-consecutive days. If there is no evidence of repigmentation after 50 mildly erythrogenic exposures, then therapy should be discontinued. Short-term side effects include painful erythema, pruritus, and xerosis; to date, long-term side effects (e.g. cutaneous carcinogenesis) have not been reported. The advantages of NB-UVB over psoralen plus UVA (PUVA) therapy include shorter treatment times, no gastrointestinal side effects, reduced phototoxic reactions, less accentuation of the contrast between depigmented and normally pigmented skin, and no need for post-treatment photoprotection. In addition, NB-UVB can be used in children, pregnant or lactating women, and individuals with hepatic or kidney dysfunction. For reliable patients, home NB-UVB units represent a convenient option 50 .

#### Psoralen plus UVA (PUVA)



PUVA consists of psoralens (usually 8-methoxypsoralen) combined with UVA light (see Ch. 134 ). Psoralens can be administered orally (oral PUVA) or applied topically (topical PUVA), followed by exposure to either artificial UVA light or natural sunlight (PUVASOL). Oral PUVA is no longer recommended for vitiligo therapy considering the greater efficacy, ease of administration, and lack of systemic side effects of NB-UVB 51 . Topical PUVA or PUVASOL can be useful for localized lesions 52 , although there is a risk of phototoxicity with blistering or koebnerization.A low concentration (≤0.1%) of psoralen should be used, which requires dilution of commercially available preparations, followed by an initial UVA dose of 0.25 J/cm 2 and subsequent increases in 10%–20% increments until mild erythema is achieved in the treated sites.

#### Excimer laser and lamp



The excimer laser and lamp deliver UVB light at a peak wavelength of 308 nm, which is similar to the 311 nm peak of NB-UVB. Compared with NB-UVB, excimer devices have equal to superior efficacy for vitiligo, typically requiring a shorter duration of treatment and avoiding darkening of unaffected skin 52 . However, excimer therapy is primarily indicated for segmental vitiligo and other forms involving <10% BSA.

Areas of vitiligo are treated two to three times weekly, typically for a total of 25 to 50 sessions; in general, the repigmentation rate depends on the total number of sessions, not their frequency 53 . Repigmentation may be enhanced by the concomitant use of treatments such as TCIs, topical corticosteroids, topical JAK inhibitors, or short-term oral corticosteroid mini-pulses (see next section).

### Depigmentation



Depigmentation represents a treatment option for patients who have widespread, treatment-resistant vitiligo with limited areas of normally pigmented skin in exposed sites. The patients must be carefully chosen, i.e. adults who recognize that their appearance will be altered significantly and who understand that depigmentation requires lifelong strict photoprotection (e.g. sunscreens, clothing, umbrellas). The most commonly used agent is 20% monobenzyl ether of hydroquinone (MBEH), applied once to twice daily to the affected areas for 9–12 months or longer 77 . MBEH is a potent irritant and allergen, and an open application test can be performed before more widespread application. It typically takes 1–3 months to initiate a response, and a loss of pigment can occur at distant sites. Although depigmentation from MBEH is considered permanent, repigmentation (especially perifollicular in areas with pigmented hairs) can be seen following a sunburn or intense sun exposure. Monomethyl ether of hydroquinone (MMEH) in a 20% cream can be used as an alternative to MBEH. Side effects include contact dermatitis, exogenousochronosis, and leukomelanoderma en confetti. Depigmentation via Q-switched ruby laser therapy was reported to achieve faster depigmentation than that achieved with a bleaching agent 78 , and this laser has also been used in combination with topical 4-methoxyphenol to induce depigmentation 79 . Lastly, depigmentation with the Q-switched alexandrite laser has been described 80 .

### Psychological support



The impact of vitiligo on quality of life is severe in many affected individuals, and it is critical for physicians to recognize this aspect of the condition and address their patients’ psychological needs. Although a “magic” treatment is not yet available, there is always something beneficial that can be done for vitiligo patients. They first need to know what their skin disorder is. Explaining the nature of the disease process and the potential and limits of available therapies is important and more productive than a fatalistic attitude that there is no cure and vitiligo is “only” a cosmetic disorder. Even helping patients to conceal the condition so that it is not visible can be part of the management plan. The use of support groups and, if indicated, psychological counseling are important supplementary therapies.
