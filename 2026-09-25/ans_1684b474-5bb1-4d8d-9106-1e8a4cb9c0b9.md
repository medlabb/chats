## Answer

 **Head trauma in a child requires immediate assessment for airway, breathing, circulation, neurologic status, and cervical-spine injury.** The goals are to prevent secondary brain injury by maintaining adequate **oxygenation, ventilation, perfusion, and cerebral perfusion pressure**. Assess consciousness using AVPU and assign a pediatric **Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS)**; check pupils and limb movement before sedatives or paralytics. Children with **GCS ≤8 require endotracheal intubation and supportive mechanical ventilation**, with spine precautions. (1)

### Initial management of moderate or severe injury
- Children with **moderate TBI (GCS 9–12)** or **severe TBI (GCS 3–8)** should receive ICU monitoring. (2)
- Perform a full trauma survey and obtain **cranial CT after resuscitation and cardiopulmonary stabilization** to identify hemorrhage, swelling, skull fracture, or a lesion requiring emergency surgery. (3)
- Maintain normal oxygen and carbon dioxide levels, mean arterial pressure, temperature, and volume status. Treat hypotension with IV fluid boluses; maintain **euvolemia**, avoid hypotonic fluids, and use **normal saline** as the preferred fluid. Pressors may be needed. (2)
- Obtain urgent **neurosurgical consultation**. Focal lesions such as epidural or subdural hematomas may require immediate surgical evacuation. (1)
- If surgery is not immediately required in severe TBI, insert an **intracranial-pressure (ICP) monitor**. Repeat CT is indicated if the neurologic examination changes or intracranial hypertension is refractory. (2)

### Raised ICP or cerebral herniation
Signs of deterioration or herniation include worsening consciousness, pupillary dilation or asymmetry, hypertension, bradycardia, irregular respirations, or extensor posturing; this is a **medical emergency**. (4)(5)

First-tier measures include:
- Elevate the head of the bed and keep the head midline.
- Provide controlled mechanical ventilation, analgesia, and sedation.
- Drain cerebrospinal fluid if a ventricular drain is present.
- Treat ICP above approximately **20 mm Hg** with hypertonic saline or mannitol. Typical regimens described are **3% hypertonic saline 2–5 mL/kg over 10–20 minutes** or **mannitol 0.25–1 g/kg IV over 20 minutes**; avoid serum osmolality above 320 mOsm/L. (5)

For acute herniation, the cited treatment includes oxygen at an inspired fraction of 1.0, carefully titrated hyperventilation, and either **3% hypertonic saline 5–10 mL/kg IV** or **mannitol 0.25–1 g/kg IV**. (5) Brief hyperventilation may be used as an immediate measure, but prophylactic, vigorous, or prolonged hyperventilation is not recommended because it can reduce cerebral perfusion and oxygenation. (1)

If ICP remains refractory, reassess for hypercarbia, hypoxemia, fever, hypotension, hypoglycemia, pain, seizures, or an evolving surgical lesion; repeat imaging should be considered. Second-tier options may include decompressive craniectomy, pentobarbital infusion, carefully monitored mild hypothermia, or further hyperventilation, although evidence for a specific second-tier treatment is limited. (5)

### Supportive care
Maintain euvolemia with isotonic fluids, monitor glucose, avoid severe hyperglycemia, provide early enteral nutrition, and generally avoid corticosteroids unless adrenal insufficiency is documented. Anticonvulsant prophylaxis with **fosphenytoin or levetiracetam** is recommended after severe acute TBI to prevent early post-traumatic seizures. (6)

For **concussion without severe neurologic injury**, management includes education, 24–48 hours of relative physical and cognitive rest, followed by gradual symptom-limited activity. Each return-to-play stage should last at least 24 hours, and activity should be reduced if symptoms worsen; persistent symptoms in a child for more than one month warrant specialist assessment. (7)
