## Answer

 ## **Depersonalization/Derealization Disorder**

“Derealization disorder” is generally described within **depersonalization/derealization disorder**, the DSM-5 term for a condition involving persistent or recurrent experiences of detachment or unreality. (1)

### **Core experiences**

- **Derealization:** The world may seem strange, foreign, unreal, dreamlike, distant, foggy, two-dimensional, muffled, or lacking vivid color. Objects may feel unfamiliar, and faces may appear changed or frightening. (2)
- **Depersonalization:** A person may feel detached from their body, thoughts, emotions, or identity; feel like an outside observer or an automaton; experience emotional or sensory numbing; or report out-of-body sensations. (2)
- Other experiences can include altered body size or shape, distorted time perception, déjà vu or jamais vu, and visual distortions such as objects appearing unusually near, far, large, or small. (2)

A key diagnostic feature is that **reality testing is generally intact**: the person experiences things as unreal or “as if” unreal but does not literally believe that they have become dead or that perceptual changes are objectively true. (1) (3)

### **Diagnosis and differential diagnosis**

Diagnosis requires a detailed psychiatric history, particularly when mood or anxiety disorders are also present. The symptoms must not occur exclusively during another disorder, and they must not occur exclusively during a culturally accepted religious, spiritual, or ritual practice. (3) (4)

Similar symptoms may occur with PTSD, acute stress disorder, bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, dissociative identity disorder, dissociative amnesia, depression, anxiety disorders, seizure disorders, intoxication or withdrawal, medication effects, migraine, sleep disorders, vertigo, metabolic illness, or delirium. (4)

The distinction from psychotic disorders is especially important: psychosis involves features such as delusional explanations, psychotic thought processes, ideas of influence or reference, or Schneiderian symptoms, whereas depersonalization/derealization is primarily an “as if” perceptual and experiential disturbance. (3)

### **Course**

The disorder may be episodic, chronic, or episodic initially and later become chronic. The reported pattern is approximately one-third episodic with remission and relapse, one-third chronic, and one-third episodic initially but eventually chronic. It can cause substantial occupational, social, and personal impairment. (3)

### **Treatment information in the references**

A disorder-specific cognitive-behavioral approach focuses on interpreting symptoms as less threatening, reducing symptom monitoring, and decreasing avoidance and safety behaviors. A small uncontrolled trial reported reductions in depersonalization/derealization, depression, and anxiety. (5)

Supportive treatment may include psychoeducation, reassurance, and techniques to modulate arousal. The references describe medication responses as generally limited; some patients may have partial benefit from benzodiazepines, serotonin reuptake inhibitors, or stimulants, while there is no empirical evidence supporting antipsychotics for the core disorder. (5)

If symptoms begin after age 40, occur with focal neurological findings, sudden head pressure, a history of head injury or seizures, or possible toxicity or medical illness, the references recommend considering medical, neurological, and toxicological evaluation. (4)
