### DEPERSONALIZATION/DEREALIZATION DISORDER

As described in the section on Nosology, the DSM-IV-TR diagnosis of depersonalization disorder was renamed depersonalization/derealization disorder in DSM-5. This was based on a review that found no differences between patients with predominant depersonalization and those with predominant derealization, confirming the ICD-10/11 conceptualization of either symptom as characteristic of the disorder.

For many years, the ubiquity of depersonalization/derealization as a psychiatric symptom obscured its broader recognition as a disorder. Consequently, patients with chronic and primary depersonalization/derealization are commonly misdiagnosed as having solely mood or anxiety disorders. These disorders may be comorbid with chronic depersonalization/derealization and/or may develop in response to untreated depersonalization/derealization disorder symptoms. Recent systematic research by Daphne Simeon and Anthony David on two large cohorts of well-characterized patients with depersonalization/derealization disorder yielded strikingly consistent findings in the United States and Europe. Clinical features, course, prognosis, and cognitive and neurobiologic correlates clearly distinguish this disorder from other psychiatric disorders in which depersonalization/derealization symptoms occur. Also, valid and reliable instruments exist for screening and diagnosis, such as the Cambridge Depersonalization/Derealization Scale developed by Mauricio Sierra and Germán Berrios (see section on Nosology and [Table 20–2](CH20.xhtml#tt20-2)).

#### Etiology

Discrete Behavioral States Theory and Depersonalization/Derealization Disorder. Individuals with depersonalization/derealization disorder describe highly distressing, continuous or relapsing/remitting DBSs characterized by alienation from somatic, perceptual, cognitive, and affective processes. Unlike individuals with psychotic or delusional disorders, reality testing remains intact: for example, when queried these individuals acknowledge that they “feel” terrifyingly dead but they do not literally believe that they are dead. Individuals with the disorder usually have limited ability to modulate or moderate these states. Some describe states characterized by numbness and detachment. Typically, these individuals’ emotionless, robotic outward presentation belies their substantial subjective distress at experiencing chronically anhedonic states of being. Conversely, many individuals with depersonalization/derealization disorder present with states of overt agitation and desperation about experiencing disconnection from themselves and/or the world.

Psychodynamic. Traditional psychodynamic formulations have viewed depersonalization/derealization as a disintegration of the ego or as an response in defense of the ego. These explanations stress the role of overwhelming painful experiences or conflictual impulses as triggering events. The high rates of depersonalization/derealization symptoms in normal adolescents and in patients conceptualized as having borderline or narcissistic personality organizations are cited as evidence that ego immaturity, ego deficits, and/or self-self-system lability and/or deficits predispose to these conditions. More recently, attention has been drawn to the similarities between depersonalization/derealization and obsessive-compulsive symptoms. Patients with depersonalization/derealization disorder often display obsessive-like behaviors with respect to their symptoms. The split between an observing and a participating self is likened to the division between intellect and emotion in obsessive patients. Both groups respond to serotonin reuptake inhibitors, although the therapeutic response for patients with depersonalization/derealization disorder is usually much less robust.

#### Diagnosis and Clinical Features

In clinical samples, the disorder is equally common in men and women but is often missed. Under-diagnosis is facilitated by several aspects of the disorder. Patients experiencing depersonalization/derealization symptoms have difficulty putting their ineffable experiences into words and may have problems with alexithymia. They often fear that they may be seen as “crazy” if they **do** describe their symptoms and may be reluctant to speak about them. They may describe their severe suffering in ways that do not adequately convey their distress to an examiner; for example, repeatedly stating that, “I feel dead,” “Nothing seems real,” or “I’m standing outside of myself.” Individuals with depersonalization/derealization disorder typically endure an intensely unpleasant, and often disabling, subjective experience. Many say that they would gladly exchange the depersonalization/derealization for physical pain that would at least reconnect them with their body. Others say they do not contemplate dying because they already are dead. Some patients may appear remarkably detached and unemotional, due to profound dissociation of affects. As the patient’s complaints do not match their affective presentation, clinicians may not take the severity of this disorder as seriously as they should.

There are several distinct components to experiences of depersonalization and derealization. Individuals may be reluctant or have difficulty describing their symptoms, and clinicians should familiarize themselves with the range of depersonalization/derealization symptoms for direct inquiry. *Depersonalization* may include a sense of bodily changes, duality of self as observer and actor, being cut off from others, and being cut off from one’s own emotions. These individuals may suffer from teleopsia (objects appear further away), pelopsia (objects appear nearer than they are), metamorphopsia, micropsia, and/or macropsia. They may experience their body changing in size and shape; disintegration of time sense with little ability to assess how recently or remotely events occurred; *déjà vu*, *déjà vécu*, and *jamais vu*; numbing of emotions and perceptions, including the inability to perceive hunger or thirst; and feeling robotic, detached, and/or without thoughts or feelings. The individual may report out-of-body experiences or, as in the case example above, a highly dysphoric feeling of partial disconnection from oneself. On the other hand, in *derealization,* the world appears strange, foreign, or dreamlike. Objects may appear as if viewed from a great distance and as if they were two-dimensional, without depth or substance, or as if observed through a fog, mist, or hazy, frosted glass. Sounds come from a distance, muffled and distorted. Objects feel strange to the touch. Colors dim and lose their vitality. The faces of others change, becoming unfamiliar and frightening.

To establish a differential diagnosis, a thorough and detailed psychiatric history needs to be obtained, especially in the presence of comorbid mood and anxiety disorders. Current diagnostic criteria require that the chronic depersonalization/derealization not occur exclusively in the presence of other disorders. It is common to obtain a history whereby an earlier and frightening episode of severe anxiety or depression—which conceivably may have acted as an internal stressor and destabilized a person’s sense of self—may precipitate chronic depersonalization/derealization. Subsequently, the latter continues even after the initial anxiety or mood episode remits. In another common scenario, depersonalization/derealization disorder tends to be more refractory, often continuous for periods of many years or decades, while the comorbid disorders go into periods of remission, whether with treatment or spontaneously. Conversely, patients who are severely and chronically ill with depersonalization/derealization disorder may become unremittingly depressed in response to their primary dissociative disorder, leading to misdiagnosis of a primary affective disorder. As discussed above, significant depersonalization/derealization symptoms may be found in dissociative identity disorder, often with CDS total scores several times higher than the cutoff score of ≥70 (e.g., 150 to 220) considered diagnostic for depersonalization/derealization disorder. Individuals with generalized dissociative amnesia, as well as patients with the childhood autobiographical memory deficits (CAMD)-form of dissociative amnesia, may experience depersonalization/derealization as a manifestation of the dissociative disorder and/or other comorbidities (e.g., PTSD, depression, obsessive-compulsive disorder, conversion disorder). from other dissociative disorders.

As a defining differential-diagnostic point to discriminate depersonalization/derealization disorder from psychotic disorder, patients with the former condition generally have intact reality testing about perceptual alterations. Also, the presence of a psychotic thought process, overvalued ideas, ideas of influence/reference, and Schneiderian symptoms distinguish depersonalization/derealization disorder from psychotic spectrum disorders such as schizophrenia, prodromal schizophrenia, or schizotypal personality disorder. The diagnosis of depersonalization/derealization disorder is based on the “as if” experiential nature of depersonalization/derealization, without distorted, delusional, dereistic cognitive explanations. This is similar to the assessment of a psychotic process accompanying obsessive-compulsive symptoms. A study comparing perceptual alterations and magical ideation in individuals with depersonalization/derealization disorder and healthy volunteers found that people with the disorder only had elevated perceptual, but not cognitive, distortions.

#### Course and Prognosis

Depersonalization or derealization symptoms after traumatic experiences or intoxications commonly remit spontaneously. Depersonalization/derealization accompanying mood, psychotic, or other anxiety disorders commonly remit only with definitive treatment of these conditions.

One-third of individuals with depersonalization/derealization disorder have an episodic, relapsing, and remitting course; one-third a chronic course; and one-third an episodic course that becomes chronic. Many patients with chronic depersonalization/derealization disorder are characterized by severe impairment in occupational, social, and personal functioning. In most studies, mean age of onset appears be in late adolescence or early adulthood. Most patients with depersonalization/derealization disorder are initially treated for the secondary anxiety and mood disorder symptoms. The primary nature of the depersonalization/derealization disorder is often only recognized later, if at all. These individuals, like those with dissociative identity disorder, may spend many unproductive years in the mental health system before receiving a correct diagnosis, “failing” to respond to standard treatments and often characterized as “treatment resistant.” As in the case of individuals with dissociative identity disorder, expert clinical opinion suggests that persons with untreated depersonalization/derealization disorder may, after years of unproductive treatment, suffer demoralization and clinical deterioration. This may lead to a chronic and disabling course with impairment in family, social, and occupational functioning.

#### Differential Diagnosis

The variety of conditions comorbid with depersonalization/derealization disorder complicates the differential diagnosis. These include major depressive disorder (73%); any anxiety disorder (64%); obsessive-compulsive disorder (21%); avoidant personality disorder (23%); and borderline personality disorder (21%). Symptoms of depersonalization/derealization may be associated with PTSD, ASD, bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, dissociative identity disorder, and dissociative amnesia, among others. Depersonalization/derealization symptoms may also result from a medical or neurologic condition, particularly seizure disorders, intoxication or withdrawal from illicit drugs, or as a side effect of medications (e.g., over-the-counter cold medications containing dextromethorphan; prescription or over-the-counter medications with anticholinergic potency). A thorough medical and neurologic assessment is not routinely needed, except for patients whose symptoms or course appear atypical, or if there is reasonable suspicion or evidence of medical or neurologic disorders or toxicity. Expert consensus suggests consideration of medical, neurological, and toxicologic evaluation when the depersonalization/derealization begins after age 40; there are focal neurologic findings; symptoms of numbness or tingling in extremities or sudden onset of symptoms of head pressure; history of head injury, seizure, or brain disorder; or infectious diseases or other medical conditions that can affect the CNS (e.g., systemic lupus, Lyme). Other medical conditions that can generate depersonalization/derealization include migraine, sleep apnea, vertigo, Ménière disease, metabolic diseases, and delirium. In general, depersonalization/derealization symptoms with medical etiology have a predominantly sensory character without the elaborated descriptions and personalized meanings common to psychiatric etiologies.

Work up should include standard laboratory tests, specialized labs (e.g., for autoimmune disorders), and urine and/or serum drug screens. Sleep-deprived EEG, and sometimes ambulatory or inpatient telemetric EEG, may be necessary to rule out focal seizures with impaired awareness (partial complex seizures). Brain imaging may be necessary to assess for structural abnormalities or other processes, as well as specialized assessments (e.g., vestibular testing, polysomnogram).

Drug-related depersonalization/derealization is typically transient, but persistent depersonalization/derealization may follow an episode of intoxication with a variety of substances, notably marijuana, hallucinogens, MDMA (Ecstasy), ketamine, and more recently salvia.<sup>1</sup> When depersonalization/derealization disorder is precipitated by drug use, there is no difference in the clinical course from depersonalization/derealization disorder without this precipitant.

In recent years, a disorder-specific cognitive-behavioral therapy approach to depersonalization/derealization disorder has been developed and tested, based on the cognitive-behavioral model outlined earlier. In this type of treatment, the focus is on developing techniques to cognitively reinterpret symptoms as less threatening; to reduce symptom monitoring; and to diminish avoidance and safety behaviors. In an uncontrolled, prospective treatment trial of 21 patients utilizing this approach, significant reductions in ratings of depersonalization/derealization, depression, and anxiety were found. In addition, by the end of the 12-week treatment, 29% of participants no longer met diagnostic criteria for the disorder. Recently, a small, uncontrolled study of a staged psychoeducational, mindfulness, and CBT group therapy intervention for adolescents with depersonalization/derealization disorder found that, at 6 months follow-up, about two-thirds of the patients decreased CDS scores below cutoff criteria.

A proportion of severely impaired patients with depersonalization/derealization disorder may require long-term supportive treatment. The clinician will need to be acutely aware of the patient’s interpersonal sensitivity, distress, and sense of hopelessness about the condition. Some supportive psychotherapy elements are very important for the effective treatment of all patients with the disorder. These include psychoeducation, such as giving the condition a name and reassuring the patient that it will not evolve into a psychotic disorder and that there is no evidence of permanent brain damage. Also, psychoeducation and supportive interventions can help the patient lift the barrier of secrecy based on shame about symptoms in general, alleviate guilt or shame associated with a drug-induced onset, and provide hope for the future. In this educational/supportive/cognitive-behavioral model, the clinician challenges the patient’s experience of the “physicality” of the experience. This can include emphasizing that depersonalization/derealization disorder is a psychological disorder, as physical as it may feel; providing a sense of control over the symptoms that may be perceived as continuous in intensity; using techniques that can help modulate the level of arousal and indirectly soften the symptoms; and reassuring women who are considering pregnancy that the heritability, as far as it is known, appears to be limited. There are no adequate data on the utility of either hypnosis or EMDR in treating chronic depersonalization/derealization. One study found that these patients had relatively low hypnotic capacity on standardized hypnotizability measures, suggesting that this may not be a helpful intervention for patients with depersonalization/derealization disorder.

Pharmacotherapy. Extensive retrospective treatment histories have not provided support for major symptom improvement with any of the known and widely used classes of psychiatric medications, although benzodiazepines, serotonin reuptake inhibitors, and stimulants appear to be partially efficacious for some patients. In past decades typical antipsychotics were widely used for chronic depersonalization/derealization, often as first-line treatment, as many clinicians viewed depersonalization/derealization symptoms as indicative of a psychotic-spectrum condition. There is no empirical evidence for the efficacy of typical or atypical antipsychotic medications in depersonalization/derealization disorder. They should only be used to target overwhelming anxiety or mood disturbance associated with the depersonalization/derealization. Common side effects of these medications may *exacerbate* an experience of emotional deadness and lack of emotional response to oneself or the world. Thus, in some individuals with depersonalization/derealization disorder these medications may *increase* the target symptoms. Typically, the numbing/deadening neuroleptic side effects set in gradually as dosage increases. Thus, neither patient nor clinician appreciates the source of symptoms worsening—sometimes leading to even higher neuroleptic dosage.
