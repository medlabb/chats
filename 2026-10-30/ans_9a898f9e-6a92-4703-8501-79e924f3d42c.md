## Answer

 ## Management of hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state (HHS)

HHS is treated in the hospital with **intravenous fluids, electrolyte replacement, insulin, frequent monitoring, and treatment of the precipitating illness**. (1) The overall approach is summarized in **algorithm 1**. (2)

### 1. Initial assessment
- Assess **vital signs, cardiovascular and respiratory status, hydration, and mental status**. (2)
- Determine a Glasgow Coma Scale score in patients with stupor or coma; endotracheal intubation is usually required when the score is ≤8. (2)
- Identify and treat precipitating events, including infection. (2)

### 2. Fluid resuscitation
- Begin **isotonic saline or isotonic buffered crystalloid**, such as Lactated Ringer solution, to correct hypovolemia and hyperosmolality. (3; 4)
- In hypovolemic shock, infuse isotonic fluid as rapidly as possible. In hypovolemia without shock or heart/kidney failure, an initial rate of approximately **500–1000 mL/hour for the first few hours** is described. (4)
- Aim to correct the fluid deficit over **24–48 hours**, avoiding a rapid fall in osmolality. (4)
- After the initial period:
  - Continue isotonic fluid at approximately **250–500 mL/hour** if corrected serum sodium is <135 mEq/L.
  - If corrected serum sodium is ≥135 mEq/L, generally switch to **0.45% saline at 250–500 mL/hour**. (4)
- Add **5% or 10% dextrose** when serum glucose falls below **250 mg/dL (13.9 mmol/L)**. (4)

Treatment targets include:
- Glucose decline no faster than approximately **90–120 mg/dL per hour**
- Sodium decline no faster than **10 mmol/L in 24 hours**
- Plasma osmolality decline of approximately **3–8 mOsm/kg per hour**. (4)

### 3. Potassium replacement
- Most patients require intravenous potassium replacement because total-body potassium depletion may be substantial despite a normal or elevated serum potassium. (3)
- If serum potassium is **<3.5 mEq/L**, give fluids and potassium and **delay insulin** until potassium is >3.5 mEq/L. (5)
- If potassium is >5.0 mEq/L or urine output is low, delay potassium replacement until potassium is ≤5.0 mEq/L and urine output is at least 50 mL/hour. (3)
- Routine phosphate replacement is generally not recommended; phosphate is indicated for severe hypophosphatemia <1 mg/dL. (6)

### 4. Insulin
- Once serum potassium is ≥3.5 mEq/L, start **low-dose intravenous insulin**. (2; 5)
- For predominant HHS without significant acidosis or ketonemia, use continuous regular insulin at **0.05 units/kg/hour**. (3; 5)
- If mixed HHS/diabetic ketoacidosis is present, use **0.1 units/kg/hour**. (3; 5)
- If glucose does not fall by at least 50–70 mg/dL during the first hour after confirming insulin delivery, the infusion rate may be doubled hourly until an appropriate decline is achieved. (5)
- Regular intravenous insulin is generally preferred over rapid-acting analogs because of similar efficacy and lower cost. (3; 5)

### 5. Monitoring
- Measure serum glucose **hourly initially**, until stable. (7)
- Measure electrolytes, BUN, phosphorus, and creatinine every **2–4 hours**, depending on severity and response. (7)
- Calculate plasma osmolality every **2–4 hours** using the measured sodium and glucose concentrations. (7)
- Monitor closely for cerebral edema; overly rapid reduction in plasma osmolality should be avoided. (4)

### 6. Transition to subcutaneous insulin
When HHS has resolved, glucose is approximately **<250–300 mg/dL**, and the patient can eat, transition to a multiple-dose basal-bolus subcutaneous regimen. Continue IV insulin for **1–2 hours after rapid-acting subcutaneous insulin** is started, with basal insulin administered before stopping the infusion. (6)
