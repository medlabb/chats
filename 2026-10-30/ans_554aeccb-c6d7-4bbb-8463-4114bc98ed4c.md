 ## Heart failure

**Heart failure (HF)** is a clinical syndrome in which the heart cannot maintain adequate cardiac output at normal filling pressures and/or ventricular relaxation is impaired, causing elevated filling pressures. Low cardiac output may cause **fatigue and exercise intolerance**, while elevated filling pressures may cause **dyspnea and peripheral edema**. (1)

### Common clinical findings
Findings suggesting elevated filling pressures or reduced cardiac output include:

- Pulmonary rales, rhonchi, or wheezing  
- Peripheral edema or ascites  
- Jugular venous distension and a positive hepatojugular reflux  
- An **S3**, often associated with left ventricular systolic dysfunction  
- An **S4**, suggesting diastolic dysfunction  
- A displaced point of maximal impulse (2)

### Cardiomyopathy patterns
- **Dilated cardiomyopathy** commonly causes impaired systolic function, or **HFrEF**; causes include myocardial infarction and infectious myocarditis.  
- Ventricular hypertrophy impairs relaxation and elevates filling pressures, producing **HFpEF**; long-standing hypertension is a common cause.  
- **Restrictive cardiomyopathy** also causes impaired relaxation and HFpEF and may result from fibrosis or amyloidosis.  
- **High-output HF** occurs when increased cardiac output still fails to meet metabolic and perfusion demands; possible causes include obesity, anemia, hyperthyroidism, vitamin B1 deficiency, arteriovenous shunts, and liver disease. (3)

### Hemodynamic profiles
Patients may be categorized by cardiac output and filling pressures:

- **Low cardiac output**: cardiac index ≤2.2 L/min/m², with possible fatigue, dizziness, reduced urine output, and cool extremities.  
- **Elevated filling pressures**: fluid may accumulate in the lungs, gastrointestinal tract, and extremities.  
- **Warm and wet**: congestion with adequate perfusion; many patients with acute decompensation fall into this group.  
- **Cold and wet**: congestion with poor perfusion; inotropic support may be required.  
- **Cold and dry**: poor perfusion despite diuresis and may require advanced therapies. The hemodynamic profiles are illustrated in **Fig. 5.1**. (4)

### Pathophysiology
In HF, reduced contractility limits the ability of increased preload to augment stroke volume. This contributes to elevated filling pressures and fluid retention. Diuresis can reduce left ventricular end-diastolic pressure and congestion; afterload reduction and inotropic therapy can improve forward flow in selected decompensated patients. ### Prognosis and advanced care
Reported poor prognostic indicators include impaired functional status, low EF, pulmonary vascular or right ventricular remodeling, cardiorenal or hepatic syndromes, and arrhythmias. Palliative care can improve symptom burden and support end-of-life planning; it is described as a class I recommendation for stage D HFrEF. (6)
