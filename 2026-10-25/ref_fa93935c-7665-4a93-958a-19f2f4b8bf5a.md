SinceC. tetanispores cannot be eliminated from the environment, immunization and proper treatment of wounds and traumatic injuries are crucial for tetanus prevention.
All patients with tetanus should receive active immunization with a full series, starting at the time of diagnosis of illness. (See'Vaccination once stabilized'above.)
Subsequent tetanus doses, in the form of tetanus and diphtheria toxoid or tetanus, diphtheria, acellular pertussis, are recommended at 10-year intervals throughout adulthood in many countries including the United States [66]. Tetanus toxoid alone should be given only to those patients with documented allergy or untoward reactions to diphtheria toxoid (see"Tetanus-diphtheria toxoid vaccination in adults"). Immunization of individuals who are pregnant or of childbearing age reduces neonatal tetanus mortality by approximately 94 percent [20]. Improving hygiene during home births in resource-limited settings is also likely to play an important role in preventing neonatal tetanus.

## Neutralization of unbound toxin with antitoxin
The use of passive immunization (ie, antitoxin) to neutralize unbound toxin is associated with improved survival, and it is considered to be standard of care [47]. We suggest IM humantetanus immune globulin(HTIG) as the antitoxin of choice to neutralize unbound toxin. If HTIG is not available, human immunoglobulin or equine antitoxin are reasonable alternatives.
The recommended dose of HTIG is a single dose of 500 units IM [48,49]. Antecedent skin testing is not needed before administering the human preparation. HTIG should be administered as soon as the diagnosis of tetanus is considered, with part of the dose infiltrated around the wound [48,49]. HTIG should be administered at different sites than tetanus toxoid vaccine.
If IV human immunoglobulin is used, the suggested dose is 200 to 400 mg/kg. Given the long half-life of HTIG (approximately 28 days), repeat dosing is not necessary. When equine antitoxin is used, an intradermal test dose of 0.1 mL in a 1:10 dilution should be administered prior to giving the full dose to evaluate for hypersensitivity reactions [2].
After administration of antitoxin, symptoms continue to worsen for a period of hours to days due to the toxin that has already entered the nervous system. Since tetanus toxin is irreversibly bound to tissues, antitoxin can only neutralize unbound toxin. Neutralization of remaining toxin limits further binding and additional toxin effects on the central nervous system.
Evidence supporting antitoxin is limited. Supporting data for antitoxin come from a single randomized trial published in 1960 demonstrating that a dose of 200,000 international units equine antitoxin reduced mortality (49 versus 76 percent) [50] and observations of reduced incidence of tetanus in wounded soldiers who received equine antitoxin. Animal-derived antibodies are generally considered more allergenic than HTIG, but both appear to be effective [51]. In a blinded, randomized trial of 215 adults with tetanus in Vietnam that compared IM equine antitoxin (21,000 international units) with HTIG (3000 international units), there were no differences in mechanical ventilation rate, hospital/ICU length of stay, mortality, or adverse events [51].
- Intrathecal antitoxin is of unclear benefit. In general, there is no role for intrathecal antitoxin in addition to IM antitoxin. As an example, in the trial of 215 patients from Vietnam described above, the rate of mechanical ventilation was similar among those who received IM antitoxin with or without intrathecal antitoxin (43 versus 50 percent, respectively; relative risk 0.87, 95% CI 0.66-1.13) [51]. In another trial of 120 patients from Brazil evaluating the use of intrathecal immunoglobulin in addition to IM therapy, patients receiving intrathecal therapy had a shorter duration of spasms, shorter hospital stay, and a decreased requirement for respiratory assistance; however, mortality was not significantly affected [52].
