### C. Hyperbilirubinemia

**Unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia** may result from overproduction of bilirubin because of hemolysis; impaired hepatic uptake of bilirubin due to certain drugs; or impaired conjugation of bilirubin by glucuronide, due to mild decreases in uridine diphosphate (UDP) glucuronyl transferase (Gilbert syndrome), or moderate decreases (type II) or absence (type I) of UDP glucuronyl transferase (Crigler-Najjar syndrome) ([Table 18–2](#papacmdt26_ch18tb2)). Hemolysis alone rarely elevates the serum bilirubin level to more than 7 mg/dL (119.7 mcmol/L). Stool and urine color are normal. There is mild jaundice and increased serum indirect (unconjugated) bilirubin, but bilirubin is not present in the urine. Splenomegaly occurs with chronic hemolytic disorders except sickle disease.

# 22-04: Prevention of Rhesus Alloimmunization

The antibody anti-Rh<sub>o</sub>(D) causes severe hemolytic disease of the newborn. About 15% of people of European and North American descent and much lower proportions of people from Africa and Asia are [Rh<sub>o</sub>(D)–negative.](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660285) If an [Rh<sub>o</sub>(D)–negative](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660285) woman carries an [Rh<sub>o</sub>(D)–positive](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660285) fetus, antibodies against [Rh<sub>o</sub>(D)](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660285) may develop in the mother when fetal red cells enter her circulation during small fetomaternal bleeding episodes in the early third trimester or during delivery, abortion, ectopic pregnancy, placental abruption, or other instances of antepartum bleeding. This antibody, once produced, remains in the mother's circulation and poses the threat of hemolytic disease for subsequent Rh-positive fetuses.

Passive immunization against hemolytic disease of the newborn is achieved with [Rh<sub>o</sub>(D)](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660285) immune globulin, a purified concentrate of antibodies against [Rh<sub>o</sub>(D)](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660285) antigen. The [Rh<sub>o</sub>(D)](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660285) immune globulin (one vial of 300 mcg intramuscularly) is given to a [Rh<sub>o</sub>(D)](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660285) negative mother within 72 hours after delivery. Administration after spontaneous or induced abortion or ectopic pregnancy before 12 weeks’ gestation may not be beneficial. The provider and patient should exercise shared decision making. Administration is recommended at 12 weeks’ gestation and beyond. The antibodies in the immune globulin destroy fetal Rh-positive cells so that the mother will not produce anti-Rh<sub>o</sub>(D). During her next Rh-positive gestation, erythroblastosis will be prevented. An additional safety measure is the routine administration of the immune globulin at the 28th week of pregnancy to all [Rh<sub>o</sub>(D)](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660285) negative mothers. The passive antibody titer that results is too low to significantly affect an Rh-positive fetus. The maternal clearance of the globulin is slow enough that protection will continue for 12 weeks. Once a woman is alloimmunized, [Rh<sub>o</sub>(D)](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660285) immune globulin is no longer helpful and should not be given.

## GENERAL CONSIDERATIONS

The upper limit of normal for ALT is 29–33 U/L in men and 19–25 U/L in women. Elevated aminotransferases have multiple etiologies (see [Table 18–4](#papacmdt26_ch18tb4)). Persistent mild elevations (< 5 times normal) have myriad causes but are caused most often by metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) (formerly non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, NAFLD). Severe elevations (> 15 times normal and often > 1000 U/L [20 mckat/L]) are the hallmark of hepatocellular necrosis or inflammation caused by a different spectrum of disorders. Cholestasis is defined by an elevated alkaline phosphatase level (> 120 IU/L) most commonly caused by biliary obstruction or cholestatic liver disease. Total serum bilirubin is normally 0.2–1.2 mg/dL (3.42–20.52 mcmol/L) with higher mean levels in men than women. Jaundice (icterus) may be caused by increases in serum unconjugated or conjugated bilirubin in the serum ([Table 18–1](#papacmdt26_ch18tb1)). Jaundice may not be clinically recognizable until serum bilirubin levels are ≥ 3 mg/dL (51.3 mcmol/L).
