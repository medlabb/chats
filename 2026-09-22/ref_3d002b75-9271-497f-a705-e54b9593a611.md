### Harlequin Color Change



Harlequin color change is a transient phenomenon observed in the immediate neonatal period and common in low birth weight infants. The dependent side of the body becomes intensely red, and the upper side pales, with a sharp midline demarcation. The peak frequency of attacks in one series occurred on the second, third, and fourth days, but episodes were observed during the first 3 weeks of life. These episodes are of no pathologic significance. They have been attributed to a temporary imbalance in the autonomic regulatory mechanism of the cutaneous vessels; there are no accompanying vital sign changes or long-term consequences reported.

## Skin



Inspect the skin for color, rashes, texture, and birthmarks. Although acrocyanosis is commonly seen in the first day of life, central cyanosis, best observed on the tongue, requires urgent investigation. If there is any doubt, the newborn’s oxygen saturation should be checked with a pulse oximeter. A blue discoloration of the skin, often with petechiae, can affect the presenting part in a face or breech presentation or the head and neck if the umbilical cord was wrapped around the infant’s neck. This can be distinguished from central cyanosis because the tongue remains pink. The infant may be plethoric from polycythemia or unduly pale from anemia or shock. Polycythemic infants (central hematocrit >65%) sometimes appear cyanotic because of the high concentration of reduced hemoglobin in their blood, even though they are adequately oxygenated. If there is a concern for polycythemia or anemia, the hemoglobin concentration or hematocrit should be measured. Harlequin color change is a rare but dramatic manifestation of infant vasomotor instability, presenting with a longitudinal sharply demarcated reddening down one half of the body and a blanching down the other side. This lasts for a few minutes but can be understandably disturbing to parents and staff. Jaundice within the first 24 hours of birth, unless mild, is most likely to be hemolytic and requires urgent investigation and treatment. It may also be a feature of congenital infection. Manage according to guidelines.

The following newborn skin conditions are benign and self-limited and do not need any treatment. Cracked, peeling skin is common, especially in post-term infants, and does not need investigation or interventions unless platelike or deep, suggesting ichthyosis. Congenital dermal melanocytoses (also termed cerulean spots) are blue-black macular discolorations at the base of the spine or on the buttocks and are present at birth. They occasionally also occur on the legs or other parts of the body. They are most common in Black or Asian infants and fade slowly over the first few years of life. They are of no clinical significance but are occasionally misdiagnosed as bruises: therefore their presence and distribution at birth should be recorded. Neonatal urticaria (erythema toxicum neonatorum) is a common rash that usually starts on the second or third day of life. It consists of small whitish papulesat the center of an erythematous base. Eosinophils are present on microscopy. The lesions resolve over 1 to 3 days with fresh crops appearing for a few days, and all lesions resolve around the fifth day. Neonatal pustular melanosis begins at 37 to 38 weeks of gestation and therefore may evolve in utero. It is more common in Black infants. If pustules are present at birth, there is minimal to no surrounding erythema, and the top of the tiny pustule is readily removed by wiping to reveal a melanotic freckle which takes several months to fade. The pus contains neutrophils, which may lead to a concern for bacterial infection, from which it is distinguished by absence of erythema around the lesions. Milia are benign white cysts on the nose and cheeks from retention of keratin and sebaceous material in the pilaceous follicles.

Café au lait spots are common, but more than three lesions or one large lesion may indicate an underlying disorder such as neurofibromatosis type 1 or McCune-Albright syndrome. A family history which includes learning disabilities, attention-deficit disorder, and seizures may be helpful in further diagnosis. If several café au lait macules are present, examine the infant’s axillae and inguinal regions for freckling, suggestive of neurofibromatosis 1.

Several vascular anomalies can be present at birth, some of which may be of long-term significance. The classification system has changed recently to more accurately reflect two distinct pathologic groups: malformations (errors in vasculogenesis), which may be associated with other anomalies (e.g., Sturge-Weber and Klippel-Trénaunay syndromes), and tumors (proliferating endothelium). 8

Vascular malformations are present at birth. The macular stain of infancy or nevus simplex, previously called “capillary hemangiomas” or “stork bites,” are pink macules caused by distention of dermal capillaries and are seen on the upper eyelids, the midforehead, or the nape of the neck. They occur in 40% to 60% of infants and gradually fade during the first year, although those on the neck may persist but become covered with hair.
