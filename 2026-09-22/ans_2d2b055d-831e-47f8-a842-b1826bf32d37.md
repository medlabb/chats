 # Immune thrombocytopenia (ITP): treatment guideline

## 1. Treatment principles

The therapeutic objective is a **safe platelet count sufficient to prevent clinically important bleeding**, not normalization of the platelet count. Bleeding risk correlates imperfectly with the platelet count and is greatest with **prior bleeding, platelet count <10,000/µL, and age >60 years**. (1)

Management should be individualized according to:

- Bleeding severity and anatomical site
- Platelet count and its trajectory
- Prior bleeding
- Age and comorbidities that increase bleeding risk
- Need for antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy
- Planned surgery or invasive procedures
- Treatment toxicity, patient preferences, and quality of life (rf_68) (2)

The treatment pathways are summarized in the referenced **algorithms 1 and 2**, with response times in **table 2**. (rf_68) (2)

## 2. Before treatment

ITP is a presumptive diagnosis after excluding other causes of thrombocytopenia. The evaluation should include:

- History, physical examination, CBC review, and peripheral blood-smear review
- Review of medications, beverages, and foods that may cause drug-induced thrombocytopenia
- Consideration of inherited platelet disorders, liver disease/hypersplenism, consumptive coagulopathies, microangiopathic disorders, myelodysplastic syndromes, and marrow disorders
- Testing for **HIV and hepatitis C virus**
- Evaluation for secondary ITP when clinically indicated, including **Helicobacter pylori**, systemic lupus erythematosus, lymphoproliferative disorders, immune disorders, large granular lymphocyte disorders, chronic inflammatory disorders, or CLL (rf_a8) (2)

Bone marrow examination should generally be reserved for patients with suspected marrow disease rather than performed routinely solely to confirm ITP. (rf_a8)

Before rituximab or splenectomy, provide appropriate vaccinations; hepatitis B serologies should be obtained before rituximab. (2)

# 3. Adults: initial treatment

## A. No bleeding or minor bleeding

For most adults with minor bleeding or a platelet count **<20,000/µL**, particularly <10,000/µL, treatment is favored over observation. Treatment at higher platelet counts may be appropriate when bleeding risk is increased, for example with:

- Prior clinically significant bleeding
- Peptic ulcer disease or liver disease
- High-risk activities or fall risk
- Concurrent anticoagulant or antiplatelet therapy
- Chronic kidney disease or other clinically important comorbidity (rf_68) (3) (2)

Observation may be reasonable in a young, asymptomatic patient with a lower platelet count after shared decision-making and reliable follow-up. The ASH-referenced threshold is to generally treat below **30,000/µL**, while observation may be appropriate in selected patients; treatment is generally not recommended solely on the basis of a count above 30,000/µL without additional risk factors. (3)

## B. First-line therapies

### Glucocorticoids

Choice of corticosteroid is individualized:

- **Dexamethasone:** 40 mg orally or intravenously once daily for 4 days; no taper. This produces faster responses and fewer bleeding events.
- **Methylprednisolone:** 1 g IV once daily for 3 days; no taper.
- **Prednisone:** 1 mg/kg/day orally, with a range of 0.5–2 mg/kg/day, for 1–2 weeks followed by a gradual taper, typically completed in <6 weeks. (rf_68)

### IVIG

Typical adult dosing is **1 g/kg daily for 1–2 days**; one dose is often sufficient. (rf_68)

IVIG is useful when a rapid platelet increase is needed or corticosteroids are contraindicated or poorly tolerated. Chronic IVIG use should generally be avoided because of transient efficacy, cost, administration burden, and adverse effects. (2)

### Anti-D immune globulin

Anti-D is an alternative to IVIG in appropriately selected patients. The provided references do not give a complete adult eligibility and dosing framework; pediatric eligibility criteria in the selected references include Rh-positive, DAT-negative patients and exclusion of hemolytic anemia, Rh-negative blood type, and prior splenectomy. (4)

# 4. Critical or severe bleeding in adults

## A. Critical bleeding

Critical bleeding involves a critical anatomical site or hemodynamic or respiratory compromise. Management includes:

1. **Immediate platelet transfusion in all patients**
2. **Glucocorticoids plus IVIG**, rather than either alone
3. Other hemostatic therapies as clinically appropriate (rf_68)

The selected references do not provide a complete adult dosing protocol for platelet transfusion, adjunctive antifibrinolytics, or other hemostatic agents.

## B. Severe but noncritical bleeding

For severe bleeding—defined in the selected reference as a hemoglobin decrease ≥2 g/dL or bleeding requiring ≥2 units of transfusion, without critical-site involvement—glucocorticoids alone are suggested rather than IVIG alone or combined glucocorticoids plus IVIG. Some clinicians may favor IVIG because of faster action or its side-effect profile. (rf_68)

# 5. Surgery and invasive procedures

The proceduralist determines the required platelet threshold. Management may include:

- Platelet transfusion, particularly for urgent procedures with substantial bleeding risk
- Glucocorticoids and/or IVIG
- A properly timed TPO receptor agonist for elective procedures (rf_68) (3)

For an urgent procedure with significant bleeding risk, platelet transfusion should be combined with ITP-directed therapy. If there is sufficient time, glucocorticoids and/or IVIG may be used without transfusion. In elective surgery, a properly timed TPO receptor agonist may be considered, particularly when corticosteroids or IVIG have not produced an adequate response. (3)

Advance coordination is required with surgery, anesthesia, and hematology because platelet thresholds—particularly for neuraxial anesthesia—may differ between clinicians. (3)

# 6. Adult second-line treatment

Second-line treatment is commonly considered at a lower platelet threshold than first-line treatment, often **<20,000/µL**, with greater urgency when bleeding risk is increased or antithrombotic therapy is necessary. (2)

The principal options are:

1. **Splenectomy**
2. **Rituximab**
3. **Thrombopoietin receptor agonist (TPO-RA)**
4. **Fostamatinib** (2)

Selection should be shared with the patient and based on treatment goals, duration of response, administration burden, toxicities, comorbidities, and preferences.

## A. Splenectomy

Splenectomy may suit a patient seeking a potentially curative procedure and wishing to avoid long-term medication. The cited response rate is **80–90%**. Risks include immunosuppression and venous thromboembolism. Younger age and splenic sequestration on radiolabeled liver-spleen scanning predict efficacy, although such scanning is not routinely used in the United States. (2)

The laparoscopic approach has lower surgical morbidity and mortality than open surgery. The references recommend waiting **at least 12 months** when possible to allow late remissions and administering preoperative immunizations. (2)

## B. Rituximab

Rituximab may suit patients wishing to avoid surgery and prolonged medication. Reported efficacy is **40–60%**, with a median response duration of approximately one year; retreatment may be required, and long-term efficacy is similar to placebo. (2)

Before treatment:

- Provide appropriate immunizations
- Obtain hepatitis B serologies

A commonly used regimen is **rituximab 375 mg/m² IV once weekly for 4 weeks**, although the optimal dose is stated to be unknown. (2)

## C. TPO receptor agonists

TPO-RAs may suit patients wishing to avoid surgery and immunosuppression and who accept ongoing daily or weekly treatment. Oral or subcutaneous administration may be preferred by some patients. Important adverse effects include:

- Thromboembolism
- Reversible bone-marrow fibrosis
- Thrombocytosis

Close platelet-count monitoring is required to avoid excessive thrombocytosis. (2)

The selected references do not provide a complete adult TPO-RA dosing, comparative efficacy, switching, tapering, or discontinuation guideline.

## D. Fostamatinib

Fostamatinib is listed as a principal second-line option, but the selected references do not provide dosing, efficacy, patient-selection criteria, monitoring, or adverse-effect guidance. (2)

# 7. Response, relapse, and monitoring

Patients should be monitored regularly with platelet counts and clinical assessment for bleeding. They should be instructed to contact their clinician for new or worsening bleeding. (rf_68)

The selected references emphasize that treatment decisions should be based on bleeding control and maintenance of a safe platelet count rather than achievement of a normal platelet count. (rf_68) (1)

For active ITP, platelet counts should be checked before and after COVID-19 vaccination; vaccination benefits outweigh the risk of exacerbation for most patients. (rf_68)

# 8. Children

Pediatric management is chiefly based on bleeding severity, activity level, quality of life, family preferences, and reliability of follow-up. (4) (5)

## A. Watchful waiting

Most children with no bleeding, mild bleeding, or low-risk moderate bleeding—grades 0–3a on the modified Buchanan and Adix bleeding score—can be managed with:

- Clinical observation
- Serial platelet counts
- Monitoring for clinical bleeding
- Activity restriction
- Avoidance of antiplatelet and anticoagulant medications

Pharmacologic therapy may be reasonable when quality of life is significantly impaired or reliable follow-up cannot be assured. (4)

## B. Moderate- to high-risk bleeding

For grade 3b or higher bleeding, pharmacologic treatment is suggested. Oral prednisone or dexamethasone is generally used first-line. (4)

Common regimens include:

- **Prednisone:** 2–4 mg/kg/day orally, maximum 120 mg/day, for 5–7 days
- **Dexamethasone:** 0.6 mg/kg/day, maximum 40 mg/day, for 4 days (rf_42)

Repeated or prolonged corticosteroid therapy beyond 2–4 weeks is strongly discouraged because of toxicity. (rf_42)

When a rapid rise is needed, such as before surgery, IVIG or IV anti-D may be used. Anti-D should not be used in Rh-negative patients, patients with a positive DAT, hemolytic anemia, or prior splenectomy. (4)

## C. Life-threatening pediatric bleeding

Recommended combination treatment includes:

- Platelet transfusion
- High-dose IV methylprednisolone
- IVIG
- Possible IV anti-D when eligible (5)

Specific regimens in the selected reference are:

- **Platelets:** 10–30 mL/kg bolus followed by continuous infusion; assess the platelet count approximately 15 minutes after the bolus and thereafter frequently. The stated goal is >50,000/µL, with some advocating >100,000/µL for intracranial hemorrhage.
- **Methylprednisolone:** 30 mg/kg/day IV, maximum 1 g/day, for 2–3 days
- **IVIG:** 1 g/kg once; may be repeated daily for a total of 3 days if required
- **Anti-D:** 75 micrograms/kg once in eligible Rh-positive, DAT-negative patients (5)

A high-dose parenteral TPO-RA, such as subcutaneous romiplostim, may be added to increase and prolong the platelet response after the acute bleeding event, although it has little immediate effect. (6)

For persistent or recurrent pediatric disease, second-line agents include TPO-RAs—eltrombopag, romiplostim, or avatrombopag—and rituximab. (6)
