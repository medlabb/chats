### Tic DISORDERS

Tic disorders are common in youth, occurring in 15% of the general population, and are more common in youth with other psychiatric disorders. In general, when tic disorders do not affect function, pharmacologic intervention is not recommended. However, when tics interfere with function, as is frequently the case with Tourette syndrome, pharmacotherapy may be indicated. Currently, there are three medications with FDA approval for tic suppression in Tourette’s, and these include haloperidol (age >12 years), pimozide (>12 years of age), and aripiprazole (>6 years of age). However, secondary to the risks associated with dopamine antagonists and mixed dopamine serotonin receptor antagonists, α<sub>2</sub>-agonists (e.g., clonidine and guanfacine) are generally used as the first-line pharmacologic interventions. Additional trials are underway with VMAT2 inhibitors (e.g., tetrabenazine), which deplete vesicular dopamine and have been used in other hyperkinetic disorders.

Psychotropic medications play a critical role in treating many psychiatric disorders in children and adolescents. However, using these medications effectively and appropriately requires an understanding of pediatric pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics, in addition to the nuances of response that are revealed by numerous double-blind, placebo-controlled trials. In this chapter, we review the developmental pharmacology that is relevant to psychotropic medication use in pediatric populations and the evidence for multiple psychotropics across internalizing and externalizing disorders.

#### Pharmacotherapy

The decision to treat tics and/or comorbid symptoms with medication should be based on a comprehensive evaluation. Patients with symptoms that significantly interfere with adaptation to family, school or work life, peer relationships, or developmental progress should be treated, especially if psychotherapeutic options are either unavailable or not optimally beneficial. Patients who suffer significant emotional distress or inadvertently cause distress to significant others in their lives may also be candidates for treatment, even when symptoms are relatively mild. Most patients with very mild symptoms need only monitoring, education, guidance, and support. Moderate to severe symptoms should usually be treated aggressively.

The clinical workup before initiating pharmacologic treatment should include a complete physical examination and neurologic screening, psychiatric evaluation, vital signs including height, weight, and body mass index (BMI), an Abnormal Involuntary Movement Scale (AIMS) screening, basic laboratory screening (including a complete blood count with differential, urinalysis, and a comprehensive metabolic panel including liver and thyroid function tests, fasting blood glucose, and metabolic screen including cholesterol and triglycerides), and an electrocardiogram (ECG).

Medication should be initiated at the lowest possible dose, and dose titration should be gradual in general. Most maximum doses will be low (compared to doses needed for other indications for these same medications) in TD patients. When possible, a single medication (monotherapy) should be used initially. At times more than one agent will need to be prescribed simultaneously (targeted combined pharmacotherapy) when monotherapy has not been effective or has resulted in limiting adverse effects or when target symptoms include more than one disorder.

Determining an adequate duration for a medication trial for patients with tics can be quite challenging because the natural history of TD is characterized by the waxing and waning of symptoms over time. A 1-month baseline observation period before treatment is initiated is recommended, if feasible. In general, it is best to wait at least several weeks after changing a dose before making any conclusions about a therapeutic response. External stressors must always be considered as potential determinants of increased symptomatology during medication trials. Comorbid conditions, such as ADHD and OCD, which significantly affect the individual, should be targeted aggressively because it is well established that these conditions are often more impairing than tics, and they respond to treatment.

α-Adrenergic Agonists. The α-adrenergic agents are recommended as a first-line pharmacologic treatment for most patients with mild to moderate TD, especially in those with comorbid ADHD, in whom they are more effective. Clonidine (Catapres), an α<sub>2</sub>-adrenergic (presynaptic) agonist, has been used for over three decades for the treatment of TD. When given in low doses, it is postulated that presynaptic noradrenergic effects mediate the observed clinical improvement in motor and vocal tics. Clonidine activates presynaptic autoreceptors in the locus coeruleus to reduce norepinephrine release and turnover in the cerebral cortex. In addition, clonidine also reduces the disinhibition, impulsivity, inattention, and hyperactivity often present in young TD patients. Data supporting its use have been mixed, but there have been well-designed studies that demonstrate clinical effectiveness.

#### Psychoeducational Approaches

Educating patients and their families is essential. Patients and their parents frequently experience relief at the time of diagnosis. There should be ongoing opportunities for discussion of patients’ and families’ questions and concerns. Referral to the Tourette Association of America and to its local chapters is recommended for all newly diagnosed patients and their families to maintain continuing education and support.

#### Behavioral Therapies

Behavioral treatment is a primary intervention for many tic disorder patients. Simple behavioral approaches can be used with the majority of TD patients to “de-stimulate” and contain the symptoms. Specific paradigms are indicated for obsessions, compulsions, and tics. Relaxation techniques, such as deep breathing, guided imagery, mindfulness, and the use of relaxation tapes, can be useful for the anxious TD patient.

Habit-reversal therapy or training (HRT) forms the essence of therapeutic intervention in a cognitive behavioral treatment known as comprehensive behavioral intervention for tics (CBIT) in youth and adults. The superiority of CBIT over psychoeducation and supportive therapy alone is well established; CBIT is considered the first-line treatment for mild to moderate tics when intervention is warranted. The primary components of CBIT include awareness training, competing response training, relaxation training, contingency management, social support, and relapse prevention. Awareness training teaches patients to identify the premonitory urges or sensations that typically precede tics, as well as to recognize the situations in which tics are likely to occur. The second component of CBIT, teaching the patient a competing response to the targeted tic, has been developed to prevent the emergence of the tic. Parents are taught to provide appropriate support and encouragement to the child in practicing the technique at home. Tic substitution (substituting a more socially tolerable tic for a less socially tolerable one) can be useful.

Due to barriers to accessibility of behavioral interventions for tics, Singer and co-workers examined home-based parent-administered HRT. Their study found the home-based intervention to produce tic reduction comparable to in-person behavior intervention with a therapist. However, there were more dropouts from the parent-administered therapy group. Various interactive online treatment programs based on CBIT are now available for youth with tic disorders.

Behavioral interventions are also critical for the management of oppositional, defiant, or explosive outbursts, which are not uncommon in clinically referred youth with tic disorders. Scahill and colleagues demonstrated that parent management training is helpful in improving disruptive and explosive behavior problems in children with tic disorders, and Sukhodolsky and team have developed anger control training (ACT) for adolescents with TD and disruptive behavior. In children and adolescents, cognitive-behavioral paradigms are also the cornerstone of the treatment of mood and anxiety disorders, which often co-occur in clinically referred individuals with TD.

#### Psychotherapy

Individual, group, and family therapies are supportive adjuncts to behavioral interventions and pharmacotherapy. Individual supportive therapy is indicated for patients having difficulty adjusting to the disorder, to their peers or family, or with school or occupational functioning. It is particularly useful when there is evidence of moderate stress or anxiety or another comorbid psychiatric disorder responsive to this form of psychotherapy (e.g., mild depression). It can help to restore or maintain self-esteem and promote mastery and coping. Furthermore, supportive psychotherapeutic interventions may facilitate the normal developmental tasks of friendship development, school adjustment, and day-to-day stress management.

Family therapy is extremely useful in dealing with the many complex interpersonal and family issues that arise for children with TD. At a minimum, both parents should be involved in the initial evaluation. Ideally, family members can be sources of support and nurturance. Ongoing family therapy is indicated when family development (i.e., growth and maturation) has slowed or halted because of the focus on the patient with the tic disorder. It may also help with maladaptive reactions from or to siblings or with specific symptoms that are affecting the entire family.

Group therapy is another important adjunct treatment. Support and education for parents and family members of patients with TD can be found through the Tourette Association of America, which has created a well-organized network of state and community support groups. Informal, unstructured activities can be arranged through local chapters of the Tourette Association of America. Formal, structured support is also possible through social skills groups.

#### School and Occupational Interventions

Learning problems and classroom difficulties occur commonly in tic disorder patients. Specific developmental disorders and ADHD- or OCD-related symptoms may interfere with academic performance.

Clinicians should be available to provide consultation, guidance, and education to teachers or employers of patients with tic disorders. Useful special educational interventions include creating a moderate, task-oriented structure in the classroom, preferential seating, one-to-one support, and individualized educational or work plans. Flexibility is a key element in educational and occupational intervention. To promote tic control, there may be a need for optional “time outs” from class or work settings. Time limits may have to be extended or eliminated for exams. Some patients may also benefit from writing aids, such as laptops. Laptop and tablet computers may allow patients with handwriting difficulties to take notes in class without disturbing others. Adaptive, as contrasted to regular physical education, can be particularly helpful for children with TD. This form of physical education can be arranged through special education departments in local schools. Programs can be tailored to each child’s needs, strengths, and challenges.

Clonidine should generally be started at 0.025 mg daily to twice daily for prepubertal children and increased by 0.025 mg every 5 to 14 days. Prepubertal patients will generally need dosing three or four times daily. Adolescents or adults can be started on 0.05 mg daily and increased by 0.05-mg increments to twice-daily dosing. The total daily dose typically is 0.05 mg to 0.45 mg (i.e., up to 8.0 μg/kg). Common side effects include sedation, bradycardia, orthostatic hypotension, headache, dry mouth, stomachache, midsleep awakening, and irritability. Sedation, the most common and sometimes limiting side effect, usually abates over several weeks; when it does not, dose reduction may be helpful. Hypotensive effects are minimal in this dose range, but blood pressure, pulse, and ECG should be monitored at baseline and follow-up visits. More concerning is the risk of rebound increases in blood pressure, anxiety, and tics if abrupt discontinuation of clonidine occurs. A transdermal form (Catapres TTS-1, TTS-2) is available and may be an alternative when patients require very frequent dosing and/or cannot tolerate the sedative effects. A long-acting oral formulation is also available (Kapvay) and designed to be administered twice daily.

Guanfacine (Tenex), an alternative α<sub>2</sub>-adrenergic agonist, may also be efficacious for hyperactivity, impulsivity, and tics. Guanfacine activates postsynaptic prefrontal α-adrenergic cortical receptors. Prepubertal patients are typically started on 0.25 mg daily and increased by 0.25-mg increments every 5 to 14 days given twice daily; older patients and adults are typically started on 0.5 mg daily and increased by 0.5-mg increments to about 3.0 mg daily. Adverse effects, generally less significant and more tolerable than with clonidine, include sedation, fatigue, midsleep awakening/insomnia, irritability, stomach upset, constipation, and hypotension/lightheadedness. A long-acting, once-daily extended-release form (Intuniv) is also available. While previous uncontrolled studies and randomized control trials have supported the clinical use of guanfacine, a recent small, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled pilot study conducted by Murphy and colleagues found that there was no difference in efficacy between extended-release guanfacine and placebo.

Neuroleptic Agents. Since the late 1960s, when haloperidol was first introduced as a treatment for TD patients, haloperidol (Haldol) and pimozide (Orap) have been the only Food and Drug Administration (FDA)–labeled, formally approved agents for the treatment of TD. These typical neuroleptic medications block postsynaptic D<sub>2</sub> (dopamine) receptors in the basal ganglia, decreasing dopaminergic input from the substantia nigra and ventral tegmentum and thus reducing tics. These medications are generally effective in tic reduction; however, considerable variation exists in clinicians’ and patients’ thresholds for neuroleptic use. The decision to use a neuroleptic may be guided by both symptom severity and quality-of-life concerns. Significant adverse effects, including acute dystonic reactions, oculogyric crises, torticollis, Parkinsonism, akathisia, tardive dyskinesia, weight gain, sedation, dry mouth, blurred vision, galactorrhea, gynecomastia, constipation, urinary retention, social phobia, loss of drive/energy/personality, and electrocardiographic changes including tachycardia and QTc prolongation, limit their use to second- or third-line treatments. In general, neuroleptic agents are not recommended for mild tics. Of the typical neuroleptics, haloperidol and pimozide have been the most investigated, with double-blind, placebo-controlled studies supporting their use.

The newer, atypical neuroleptics, or second-generation antipsychotic agents, are recommended as a first-line treatment when a neuroleptic is indicated for moderate to severe tics. These agents, which block both D<sub>2</sub> dopamine and 5-HT<sub>2</sub> serotonin receptors, have the potential advantage of fewer extrapyramidal adverse effects (EPSs) (acute dystonic reactions, oculogyric crises, torticollis, Parkinsonism, akathisia, tardive dyskinesia). Their dual pharmacologic activity appears to protect against the neurologic adverse effects associated with typical neuroleptic agents. Among them, risperidone (Risperdal) has been found to be efficacious for tics. Risperidone is a 5-HT2 receptor antagonist at low doses and a D2 antagonist at high doses. It acts at the α-2, D3, D4, and H1 receptors with moderate to high affinity as well. Multiple trials, including randomized, controlled trials, support risperidone’s efficacy. It has been shown to be superior to placebo and equally effective as pimozide and clonidine for tic reduction. Doses are typically in the 1 to 4 mg range. Common adverse effects include appetite increase, weight gain, lipid and glucose metabolism abnormalities, and sedation. Sleep disturbance, social phobia, galactorrhea, gynecomastia, menstrual irregularities, and erectile dysfunction have also been reported.

Aripiprazole (Abilify) is a novel atypical neuroleptic with partial agonist-antagonist effects on the dopamine and serotonin system. It is a partial D2, D3, and D4 receptor agonist as well as a partial 5-HT1A and 5-HT2C receptor agonist. Several published placebo-controlled studies and open-label trials have reported effectiveness and tolerability in TD. A meta-analysis of ten studies with a total of 302 patients conducted by Liu and peers confirms that aripiprazole is safe and effective for the treatment of tics in children and adolescents. The most common adverse effects include weight gain, akathisia, and sedation. Aripiprazole became the most recent and only the third agent to be FDA-approved for the treatment of tics in 2014.

Additional atypical options with some evidence base for efficacy in TD include ziprasidone (Geodon), olanzapine (Zyprexa), and quetiapine (Seroquel). Results of one placebo-controlled trial of ziprasidone indicated that it was as efficacious as risperidone for tic reduction. Weight gain is typically less likely than that with other neuroleptic agents. Ziprasidone carries the added risk of altered cardiac conduction, especially QTc prolongation. An ECG is recommended before initiating treatment, during dose adjustments, on reaching the maintenance dose, and annually thereafter. Other adverse effects include somnolence, sedation, and akathisia. Evidence for the effectiveness of olanzapine is supported by open-label and case-report studies, but its significant association with weight gain, the highest among the atypical neuroleptics, limits its use. Only two open-label studies demonstrate quetiapine’s effectiveness for treating tics. Sedation and weight gain are the most common adverse effects.

Benzodiazepines. Benzodiazepines have not been well investigated in tic treatment but may hold some promise (e.g., clonazepam [Klonopin]). Stimulants. Stimulants are first-line agents for the treatment of ADHD. There has been controversy over the role of stimulants in TD and whether their use causes or exacerbates tics; however, recent studies have demonstrated that TD patients with clinically significant ADHD symptoms may be candidates for stimulants. In the first systematic investigation of methylphenidate use by Gadow and colleagues, behavior improved, and tics did not worsen at moderate doses of 0.1 to 0.5 mg/kg methylphenidate. When group data are analyzed, there is no significant increase in tics when stimulants are used in patients with tic disorders; however, individual patients may experience at least a transient increase in tics. Interestingly, in some studies, stimulant use is associated with a decrease in tics.

Treatment of Mark’s anxiety was indicated because it appeared to be pervasive and was causing distress.
