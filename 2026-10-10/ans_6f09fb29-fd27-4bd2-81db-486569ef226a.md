## Answer

 **Cerebral venous sinus thrombosis (CVST)** is thrombosis of the cerebral veins or venous sinuses. Predisposing factors include bacterial meningitis, subdural or epidural abscess, infections of the face, paranasal sinuses, middle ear, or mastoid, dehydration, hypercoagulable states, and antiphospholipid antibodies. Thrombosis may extend between sinuses and can be associated with cortical-vein thrombosis, small parenchymal hemorrhages, or infarction. (1)

### Clinical features
- **Superior sagittal sinus:** headache, fever, nausea/vomiting, confusion, seizures, rapid progression to stupor or coma, and possible leg weakness, bilateral Babinski signs, or hemiparesis. (2)
- **Cavernous sinus:** fever, headache, frontal or retro-orbital pain, diplopia, ptosis, proptosis, chemosis, and ophthalmoplegia involving cranial nerves III, IV, and VI; papilledema may occur. (2)
- **Transverse sinus:** headache and earache; otitis media, sixth-nerve palsy, or retro-orbital/facial pain may occur. (2)
- **Sigmoid sinus/internal jugular vein:** neck pain. (2)

### Diagnosis
MRI may show an absent flow void in the affected sinus. Confirmation is obtained with contrast-enhanced MR venography, CT venography, or the venous phase of cerebral angiography. Intracerebral hemorrhage may suggest intracerebral or meningeal venous thrombophlebitis, but definitive diagnosis requires the venous phase of cerebral angiography. (3)

### Treatment
- **Septic CVST:** antibiotics, hydration, and removal of infected tissue and thrombus in septic lateral or cavernous sinus thrombosis. Antibiotics are generally continued for about 6 weeks or until radiographic resolution. (5)
- **Aseptic CVST:** anticoagulation with unfractionated or low-molecular-weight heparin is recommended. Anticoagulation may also be used in septic CVST complicating bacterial meningitis when neurological deterioration progresses despite antimicrobial therapy and intravenous fluids. A small intracerebral hemorrhage is not an absolute contraindication to heparin. (5)
