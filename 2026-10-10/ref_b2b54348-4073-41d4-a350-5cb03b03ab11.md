## APPROACH TO DIAGNOSIS

**Stroke** is a syndrome with four key features:

1. **Sudden onset—**The sudden onset of symptoms is documented by the history.
2. **Focal involvement of the central nervous system—**The site of involvement is suggested by symptoms, pinpointed more precisely by neurologic examination, and confirmed by CT or MRI.
3. **Lack of rapid resolution—**The duration of neurologic deficits is documented by the history. The classic definition requires that deficits persist for ≥24 hours to distinguish stroke from transient ischemic attack (discussed later). However, any such time point is arbitrary, and transient ischemic attacks usually resolve within 1 hour.
4. **Vascular cause—**A vascular cause may be suggested by the acute onset of symptoms and often from the patient’s age, the presence of risk factors for stroke, and the occurrence of symptoms and signs referable to the territory of a particular cerebral blood vessel. Investigative studies can often identify a more specific etiology, such as arterial thrombosis, cardiogenic embolus, or clotting disorder.


### ACUTE ONSET

Strokes begin abruptly. Neurologic deficits may be maximal at onset or may progress over seconds to hours. A stroke that is actively progressing in severity as a direct consequence of the underlying vascular disorder (but not because of associated cerebral edema), or has done so in recent minutes, is termed **stroke in evolution** or **progressing stroke** (**[Figure 13-1](#greenberg11_ch13fg1)**). Focal cerebral deficits that develop slowly (over weeks to months) suggest a cause other than stroke, such as tumor or inflammatory or degenerative disease.

### DURATION OF DEFICITS

Stroke produces neurologic deficits that persist. When symptoms and signs resolve completely after brief periods (usually within 1 hour) without evidence of cerebral infarction, the term **transient ischemic attack** (**TIA**) is used (see [Figure 13-1](#greenberg11_ch13fg1)). About 15% of strokes are preceded by TIAs. Recurrent TIAs with identical clinical features (stereotypic TIAs) are usually caused by thrombosis or embolism arising from the same site within the cerebral circulation. TIAs that differ in character from event to event suggest recurrent emboli from distant (eg, cardiac) or multiple sites. Although TIAs do not themselves produce lasting neurologic dysfunction, they are important to recognize because 3-10% of patients with TIAs will have a stroke within 2 days and 9-17% will have a stroke within 90 days—and because this risk may be reduced with treatment.


### VASCULAR ORIGIN

Although hypoglycemia, other metabolic disturbances, trauma, and seizures can produce focal central neurologic deficits that begin abruptly and last for at least 24 hours, the term *stroke* is used only when such events are caused by vascular disease.

The underlying pathologic process in stroke can be either **ischemia** or **hemorrhage**, both usually arising from an arterial lesion, and accounting for ~90% and ~10% of strokes, respectively. Among ischemic strokes, large artery occlusion, small artery occlusion, cardiac embolism, and unknown causes are each thought to be responsible for ~25% of cases worldwide.


### HISTORY


##### Predisposing Factors

The history should document risk factors (see [Table 13-1](#greenberg11_ch13tb1)) such as TIAs, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, ischemic or valvular heart disease, cardiac arrhythmia, cigarette smoking, and oral contraceptive use; other vascular, cardiac, or hematologic disorders associated with ischemic stroke, intracerebral hemorrhage, or global cerebral ischemia; and therapeutic (eg, antihypertensive) or recreational (eg, sympathomimetic) drugs that can precipitate cerebrovascular symptoms.

The history should establish the time of onset of symptoms, whether similar symptoms have occurred before, and whether the clinical course is that of TIA, stroke in evolution, or completed deficit from stroke, intracerebral hemorrhage, or global cerebral ischemia (see [Figure 13-1](#greenberg11_ch13fg1)). ##### Associated Symptoms at Onset

1. **Headache** is especially severe in intracerebral hemorrhage and is also present at onset in many patients with ischemic stroke.
2. **Seizures** can accompany the onset of thrombotic or embolic stroke or lobar intracerebral hemorrhage, or appear up to years later.


### PHYSICAL EXAMINATION


##### General Physical Examination

The general physical examination should focus on searching for an underlying systemic (especially treatable) cause of cerebrovascular disease.

1. **Blood pressure** should be measured to detect hypertension—a major risk factor for both ischemic stroke and intracerebral hemorrhage.
2. **Comparison of blood pressure and pulse on the two sides** can reveal differences related to atherosclerotic disease of the aortic arch or coarctation of the aorta, which predispose to embolic stroke.
3. **Ophthalmoscopic examination** of the retina can provide evidence of embolization in the anterior circulation, in the form of visible embolic material in retinal blood vessels, or papilledema from intracranial hemorrhage.
4. **Neck examination** may reveal the absence of carotid pulses or the presence of carotid bruits. ### FOCAL INVOLVEMENT

Stroke produces focal symptoms and signs that correlate with the area of the brain supplied by the affected blood vessel.

In **ischemic stroke**, occlusion of a blood vessel interrupts the flow of blood to a specific vascular territory of the brain, interfering with neurologic functions dependent on that territory and producing a more or less stereotyped pattern of deficits.

**Hemorrhage** produces a less predictable pattern of focal involvement because complications such as increased intracranial pressure, cerebral edema, compression of brain tissue and blood vessels, or dispersion of blood through the subarachnoid space or cerebral ventricles can impair brain function at sites remote from the hemorrhage.

**Global cerebral ischemia** (usually from cardiac arrest) and **subarachnoid hemorrhage** (discussed in [Chapter 6](/content.aspx?legacysectionid=greenberg11_ch6), Headache & Facial Pain) affect the brain in more diffuse fashion and produce global cerebral dysfunction; the term *stroke* is not usually applied in these cases.

In most cases of stroke, the history and neurologic examination provide enough information to localize the lesion to **one side of the brain** (eg, to the side opposite a hemiparesis or hemisensory deficit or to the left side if aphasia is present) and to the **anterior or posterior cerebral circulation**.


#### ANTERIOR (CAROTID) CIRCULATION

The anterior cerebral circulation supplies most of the **cerebral cortex** and **subcortical white matter**, **basal ganglia**, and **internal capsule**. It consists of the internal carotid artery and its branches: the **anterior choroidal**, **anterior cerebral**, and **middle cerebral arteries**. The middle cerebral artery in turn gives rise to deep, penetrating **lenticulostriate** branches (**[Figure 13-2](#greenberg11_ch13fg2)**). ##### Neurologic Examination

Patients with cerebrovascular disorders may or may not have abnormal neurologic findings. A normal examination is expected, for example, after a TIA has resolved. Where deficits are found, the goal is to define the anatomic site of the lesion, which may suggest the cause and optimal management of stroke. For example, evidence of anterior circulation involvement may lead to angiographic evaluation for possible surgical correction of an internal carotid lesion, whereas signs that suggest vertebrobasilar or lacunar infarction will dictate a different course of action.

1. **Cognitive deficits** such as aphasia, unilateral neglect, or constructional apraxia suggest a cortical lesion in the anterior circulation and exclude vertebrobasilar or lacunar stroke. Coma implies brainstem, bihemispheric, or global cerebral ischemia, or intracerebral hemorrhage.
2. **Visual field abnormalities** exclude lacunar infarction, but hemianopia can occur with occlusion of either the middle or posterior cerebral artery, which supply the visual radiation and visual cortex, respectively. Isolated hemianopia suggests posterior cerebral artery stroke, because middle cerebral artery stroke should produce additional (motor and somatosensory) deficits.
3. **Ocular palsy**, **nystagmus**, **or internuclear ophthalmoplegia** assigns the lesion to the brainstem, suggesting posterior cerebral circulation ischemia or hemorrhage.
4. **Hemiparesis** can be due to lesions in cerebral cortical regions supplied by the anterior circulation, descending motor pathways in the brainstem supplied by the vertebrobasilar system, or lacunes at subcortical or brainstem sites. Hemiparesis affecting the face, hand, and arm more than the leg is characteristic of middle cerebral artery lesions. ### INVESTIGATIVE STUDIES


##### Blood Tests

1. **Blood Glucose—**Hypoglycemia and hyperglycemia can both present with focal neurologic signs and masquerade as stroke, or adversely affect stroke outcome. Hypoglycemia requires immediate administration of glucose to avoid permanent brain injury. Hyperglycemia (hyperosmolar nonketotic hyperglycemia or diabetic ketoacidosis) also requires prompt specific treatment.
2. **Complete Blood Count—**This can identify possible causes of stroke (eg, sickle cell disease, polycythemia, coagulopathy) or demonstrate leukocytosis from concurrent infection. Some studies suggest that a platelet count <100,000/μL contraindicates thrombolytic therapy for ischemic stroke.
3. **Coagulation Studies—**Coagulation defects due to anticoagulant drugs or liver dysfunction may cause intracerebral hemorrhage, or affect eligibility for thrombolytic therapy in ischemic stroke. The prothrombin time (PT) and internationalized normal ratio (INR) are useful for detecting the effects of [warfarin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660008) and liver disease, but other tests (eg, [thrombin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659045) time or ecarin clotting time) may be required to detect anticoagulation by direct [thrombin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659045) ([dabigatran](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659087)) or factor Xa ([rivaroxaban](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659108), [apixaban](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659135), [edoxaban](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659219)) inhibitors.
4. **Inflammatory Markers—**An increased erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) is seen in giant cell arteritis and other systemic vasculitides.
5. **Serologic Assays—**A positive serum treponemal assay (FTA-ABS or MHA-TP) establishes, and a negative assay excludes, past or present syphilis infection. Positive CSF serology (VDRL) indicates untreated or inadequately treated neurosyphilis and suggests syphilitic arteritis as the cause of stroke. Antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies in blood or CSF are seen in certain forms of small vessel vasculitis associated with ischemic stroke.

##### Electrocardiogram (ECG)

An ECG should be obtained routinely to detect unrecognized myocardial infarction or cardiac arrhythmias, such as atrial fibrillation, which predispose to stroke.


##### Lumbar Puncture

Lumbar puncture should be performed only in selected cases, to exclude subarachnoid hemorrhage (manifested by xanthochromia and red blood cells) or to document meningovascular syphilis (reactive CSF VDRL) or certain forms of vasculitis as the cause of stroke.


##### Brain Imaging

1. **Noncontrast CT scan** or **MRI** (**[Figure 13-3](#greenberg11_ch13fg3)**) should be obtained routinely (and always prior to thrombolytic therapy) to distinguish between infarction and hemorrhage as the cause of stroke, to exclude other lesions (eg, tumor or abscess) that can mimic stroke, and to localize the lesion. Noncontrast CT is usually preferred for initial diagnosis because it is widely and rapidly available. However, its sensitivity within the first 6 hours is limited, and MRI may be superior for demonstrating early infarcts and brainstem or cerebellar infarcts and for detecting thrombotic occlusion of venous sinuses.
2. **CT perfusion** and **perfusion-diffusion MRI** can detect diffusion-perfusion mismatch corresponding to the ischemic penumbra—brain tissue at risk of infarction but potentially salvageable by thrombolysis or thrombectomy.
