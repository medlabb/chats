### Modes of Surfactant Administration



The most well-studied and only FDA-approved method of giving surfactant is via the endotracheal tube. As mechanical ventilation is known to increase the risk of injury to the lungs, the INSURE technique (INtubate, SURfactant, Extubate) gained popularity because it has demonstrated a decreased need for reintubation and mechanical ventilation. 72 A meta-analysis demonstrated no significant difference in composite outcomes of BPD or death or other associated morbidities, but suggested INSURE was favorable compared to noninvasive respiratory support alone. 73 Because even short periods of invasive ventilation can induce lung injury and intubation attempts can damage the airway, multiple studies have examined alternative noninvasive methods of surfactant administration including aerosolization or nebulization, nasopharyngeal or pharyngeal instillation, laryngeal mask administration, and intratracheal administration via a thin catheter. ## Continuous Positive Airway Pressure and Surfactant Administration



Intubation with surfactant administration, followed by extubation (INSURE, INtubation-SURfactant-Extubation) is a method of surfactant administration for premature infants supported with CPAP. It is typically indicated in infants demonstrating normal spontaneous respiratory effort with an FiO 2 greater than 0.3 without other indications for invasive mechanical ventilation. A meta-analysis of studies involving INSURE failure and the need for subsequent mechanical ventilation found extremely low birth weight, lower gestational age (especially <26 weeks), and RDS severity to be factors increasing the risk of subsequent need for intubation and mechanical ventilation. 29

Less invasive surfactant administration (LISA) describes administering surfactant to spontaneously breathing premature infants through a small catheter or feeding tube, without traditional endotracheal intubation, and thus avoidance of invasive positive pressure ventilation. Several studies have investigated whether INSURE or LISA change important long-term outcomes compared to CPAP with or without rescue intubation and mechanical ventilation; multiple meta-analyses demonstrated a reduction in death and/or BPD in premature infants who had received LISA. Recent data demonstrate a reduced incidence of BPD in survivors when CPAP was supported by minimally invasive surfactant therapy. 29a Other less invasive forms of surfactant administration currently being studied include administration via laryngeal mask airway (LMA) directly into the pharynx, and via nebulization. 30 Although nebulization seems appealing as a truly noninvasive method of surfactant administration, it has been met with many practical challenges, including difficulties with aerosolizing surfactant, long administration times, and poor surfactant delivery, with inadequate and uneven distribution into the airways and distal lung tissue. 8 , 31

Less-invasive surfactant administration (LISA) or minimally invasive surfactant therapy (MIST) combine the benefits of early surfactant administration while continuing nCPAP and avoiding intubation among preterm infants. These methods involve either placement of a small gastric catheter into the trachea via Magill forceps or insertion of a semirigid vascular catheter under direct laryngoscopy. Meta-analyses and systematic reviews demonstrate decreased risk of BPD or death at 36 weeks’ corrected gestational age, among other morbidities, when compared with INSURE or no surfactant therapy. 74 75 76 However, some of these outcomes should be interpreted with caution as a limited number of patients at the most immature gestational ages were included.

Laryngeal mask airways (LMA) provide the advantage of not requiring direct laryngoscopy, which requires a skilled clinician, and insertion can result in hypoxemia, bradycardia, increased intracranial pressure, and pain. Multiple randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have evaluated surfactant administration via LMA. Although one study demonstrated superiority of LMA to avoid intubation compared to CPAP or INSURE technique, this loses significance when pooled with other studies, and no significant differences in morbidities or mortality were identified. 77 , 78

Inhalation of aerosolized surfactants is, in fact, the only truly noninvasive method of administering surfactant. Most studies are small, but pooled data suggest that the risk of intubation within 72 hours is lower among infants who received nebulized surfactant without significant differences in morbidities or mortality. 79 However, the optimal dose of nebulized surfactant needed to provide adequate effect has not yet been determined.

The threshold for treatment of premature neonates with RDS with surfactant is continually under debate as more emphasis is being placed on noninvasive modes of respiratory support. As such, less invasive routes of surfactant administration via thin catheter or laryngeal mask airway are gaining support. 74 75 76 77 78 The recent RDS NeXT (RDS Neonatal Expert Taskforce) workshop created a consensus statement based on survey and modified Delphi method. Consensus is that surfactant is most effective if administered within 1–2 hours after birth for invasively ventilated neonates with RDS. 5a The fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO 2 ) at which surfactant should be considered is less clear but ranges from 0.3 for an invasively ventilated neonate to more than 0.4 for a noninvasively ventilated neonate. The route of surfactant administration should be determined based on the provider’s experience, skill level, available equipment, and institutional practice.
