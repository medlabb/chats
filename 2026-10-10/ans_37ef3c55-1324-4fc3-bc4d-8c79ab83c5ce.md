 ## Stroke

**Stroke** is a syndrome characterized by:

- **Sudden onset** of symptoms  
- **Focal central nervous system involvement**
- **Persistent neurologic deficits**
- A **vascular cause** (1)

The underlying process is usually either:

- **Ischemia**—approximately 90% of strokes  
- **Hemorrhage**—approximately 10% of strokes (2)

### Clinical patterns

- **Thrombotic stroke:** often progresses stepwise and may be preceded by identical transient ischemic attacks (TIAs).
- **Embolic stroke:** often reaches maximal severity within about 5 minutes and may be associated with arrhythmia, valvular disease, cardiomegaly, or endocarditis.
- **Intracerebral hemorrhage:** commonly causes impaired consciousness disproportionate to focal deficits.
- **Global cerebral ischemia:** usually occurs after cardiac arrest. (3)

Stroke deficits may be **completed and persistent** or may worsen over time, termed **stroke in evolution**. In contrast, a **TIA** resolves completely—usually within 1 hour—without evidence of cerebral infarction. (4) About 15% of strokes are preceded by TIAs, and TIA is associated with an increased short-term risk of stroke. (2)

### Possible findings

Symptoms depend on the affected vascular territory and may include:

- Aphasia, unilateral neglect, or visual-field deficits
- Facial or limb weakness or sensory loss
- Hemiparesis
- Ocular palsy, nystagmus, or internuclear ophthalmoplegia
- Ataxia or crossed face–body deficits (5, 6)

### Initial evaluation

Evaluation includes determining the time and course of symptom onset, assessing vascular risk factors, and performing a general and neurologic examination. (3, 5)

**Noncontrast CT or MRI should be obtained routinely and before thrombolytic therapy** to distinguish infarction from hemorrhage, exclude mimics, and localize the lesion. Noncontrast CT is generally preferred initially because it is rapid and widely available, although MRI may be more sensitive for early, brainstem, and cerebellar infarcts. (7)
