## Therapies that may be effective
The following therapies may be effective for some individuals and are options for patients with moderate to severe symptoms. Choice of therapy will depend on what symptoms predominate. Importantly, there are no data supporting any single universal, highly effective treatment option for the common cold and we do not favor any one of the following treatments over others.
### Analgesics
Available data suggest thatacetaminophenand nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) are roughly equivalent at relieving some symptoms (eg, headache, ear pain, muscle and joint pains, malaise, and sneezing) associated with common cold, and that short courses of standard doses in this setting are generally safe and well-tolerated [3-5]. In one randomized trial involving nearly 400 patients with upper respiratory tract infections, acetaminophen and NSAIDs were more effective than placebo in reducing headache, achiness, and feverish discomfort [4]. NSAIDs may be helpful in decreasing some cold symptoms (headache, ear pain, muscle and joint pain), but a meta-analysis found that NSAIDS did not improve cough or nasal discharge and did not significantly reduce the total symptom score or duration of colds [5].
### Antihistamine/decongestant combinations
The combination of antihistamines and decongestants may be more beneficial than either component alone [6-8]. Antihistamine use alone in patients with the common cold is of minimal benefit and frequently results in troublesome side effects. (See'Antihistamines'below.)
In a systematic review of combination products, antihistamine-decongestant products were shown to be effective in reducing symptoms compared with control interventions (odds ratio of therapy failure 0.31; 95% CI 0.20 to 0.48; number needed to treat for benefit = 4) [6]. More patients experienced adverse effects (eg, drowsiness, dry mouth, insomnia, and dizziness) with antihistamine-decongestant products, although the difference was not statistically significant (31 versus 13 percent; OR 1.58, 95% CI 0.78 to 3.21).
### Intranasal/inhaled cromolyn sodium
Various formulations of intranasal or inhalationalcromolynsodium are available over the counter. Cromolyn sodium administered intranasally and/or by inhalation may improve cold symptoms. A randomized study of 118 adult patients with symptoms of rhinorrhea, throat pain, or cough for less than 24 hours compared the use of sodium cromoglycate dry powder (20 mg per inhalation in spincaps), sodium cromoglycate aqueous nasal spray (5.2 mg per dose), or matching placebos every two hours during waking hours on days 1 and 2, and four times daily on days 3 to 7 [9]. Symptoms resolved faster and were less severe in patients treated with sodium cromoglycate than with placebo. Side effects were mild and did not differ among the three treatment groups.
### Intranasal ipratropium bromide
Symptoms of rhinorrhea and sneezing may be improved by the use of intranasalipratropiumbromide, though nasal congestion is not affected. A systematic review of seven trials comparing ipratropium and placebo (2144 participants) found improvement in rhinorrhea with ipratropium, although the possibility of inadequate blinding raises the potential for bias [10]. The systematic review found a twofold increase in side effects (nasal dryness, blood-tinged mucus, and epistaxis) in patients assigned to ipratropium.

## Therapies with minimal or uncertain benefits
We generally start treatment with therapies that may be effective. Treatments for which the balance of benefits to harms seems small or uncertain may be reasonable options in some patients who are not able to tolerate the more effective therapies.
### Dextromethorphan
Evidence supporting the use ofdextromethorphanfor acute cough due to the common cold is limited and generally poor in quality, with various studies showing mixed results [9,10]. In studies which demonstrate a statistically significant benefit, the benefit is found to be small (12 to 36 percent reduction in cough events) [9]. Given the lack of consistent benefit in published studies, the minor nature of the relief seen in positive trials, and potential for side effects from misuse, we do not routinely use dextromethorphan for acute cough due to the common cold [9,11,12].
### Decongestants
Topical and oral decongestants, such aspseudoephedrine, may offer mild relief of nasal congestion associated with the common cold when used alone [7,13,14]. One meta-analysis found a small improvement in subjective nasal congestion with decongestant monotherapy compared with placebo in adults with the common cold, although the magnitude of effect was small (standard mean difference 0.49, 95% CI 0.07-0.92; 94 participants; two studies) and most studies used pseudoephedrine [7]. An earlier meta-analysis had suggested a net 6 percent decrease in subjective symptoms after a single dose of decongestant compared with use of a placebo [14].
- **Oral**– Commonly used oral decongestants includepseudoephedrineandphenylephrine. Pseudoephedrine appears to modestly improve subjective nasal congestion compared with placebo [7], but in the United States, there are limitations on the sale of medications containing pseudoephedrine because it can be used to manufacture methamphetamine [15]. Phenylephrine is available over-the-counter and often used instead, but multiple studies have found it is no more effective than placebo, even at doses up to 40 mg [11,16,17]. The dose commonly used in most cold products is 10 mg.

- **Topical**– Topical decongestant use should be limited to two to three days because rebound rhinitis can occur after 72 hours of use. The use of topical decongestants may occasionally be complicated by nosebleeds, agitation, insomnia, and worsened hypertensive control in patients with preexisting hypertension. (See"An overview of rhinitis", section on 'Nasal decongestant sprays'.)

### Saline nasal spray
Salinenasal sprays may help nasal symptoms of the common cold. A 2015 systematic review of saline nasal irrigation for acute upper respiratory infections concluded that there may be symptomatic benefits, but there was limited evidence to support this conclusion, as the available trials were small and had a high risk of bias [18].
In a subsequent randomized trial that included approximately 6000 participants, those who were instructed to use nasalsalinesprays at the first sign of respiratory infection reported fewer days of illness over a six-month period compared with those who received usual care without nasal spray (6.4 versus 8.2 days, incidence rate ratio [IRR] 0.81, 99% CI 0.74-0.88) [19].

## Ineffective therapies
Evidence does not support the use of these therapies for treatment of the common cold.
### Antibiotic therapy
The common cold is caused by viruses and there is no indication for antibiotic therapy in the absence of evidence of secondary bacterial infections. Treatment with antibiotics for uncomplicated upper respiratory tract infections causes more harm than benefit [33]. A systematic review of randomized trials in patients (including children) with upper respiratory symptoms for fewer than seven days found that the persistence of symptoms was identical in groups who received antibiotics or placebo (risk ratio [RR] 0.95, 95% CI 0.59-1.51) [34]. Adults who received antibiotics had a significantly greater risk of adverse effects (RR 2.62, 1.32-5.18).
Despite this evidence, antibiotics continue to be prescribed inappropriately in many practices. In a retrospective cohort study of over 180,000 patients 66 years or older with nonbacterial upper respiratory infections, the majority of which were common colds, 46 percent received an antibiotic prescription [35]. Improved rapid diagnostic tests and practitioner education initiatives are needed to reduce such overutilization.
### Antihistamines
Antihistamine used alone in patients with the common cold is of minimal benefit and frequently results in troublesome side effects. First-generation antihistamines, such asdiphenhydramine, may alleviate rhinorrhea and sneezing, but their use is limited by side effects such as sedation and drying of the eyes, nose, and mouth [20]. A systematic review of 18 trials concluded that antihistamines improved the severity of symptoms slightly more often than placebo for the first one to two days of treatment but offered no benefit after 6 to 10 days of treatment [8]. The sedating antihistamines may have small symptomatic benefits, but these were clinically non-significant and outweighed by the frequency of side effects.
### Antiviral therapies
There is no clinical role for antiviral agents in the management of the common cold, in contrast to other, potentially more severe viral infections (eg, SARS-CoV-2 infection, influenza, RSV or adenovirus in immunocompromised patients). The results of studies of the use of directed antiviral therapy in patients with the common cold thus far are largely inconclusive because of recurring methodologic weaknesses. An etiologic agent is identified in only a minority of study subjects; the common cold has a wide array of viral causes, and few antiviral therapies have proven efficacy. Thus, the results of published studies of the efficacy of antiviral agents for the common cold are challenging to directly apply to the clinical care of most patients.
As an example, the combination of antiviral and NSAIDs was tested in a trial involving 150 healthy adults experimentally inoculated with rhinovirus, where patients were randomly assigned to treatment with intranasal interferon (IFN)-alpha-2b (active against rhinovirus) pluschlorpheniramineandibuprofen, intranasal placebo plus chlorpheniramine and ibuprofen, or intranasal and oral placebos [36]. Those receiving the intranasal IFN had a greater reduction in symptom scores as well as reduced volumes of nasal mucus production, and reduced virus concentrations in their nasal mucus. However, these results are not generalizable to the majority of colds, which are non-rhinovirus in origin.

### Vitamins and herbal remedies

- **Vitamin C**–Vitamin Cis often touted as a natural remedy for the common cold, and while the available data are numerous, they are of varied quality and frequently in opposition to each other. A 2013 meta-analysis of 29 trials (n = 11,306) showed a small reduction in the duration of cold symptoms in adults regularly taking vitamin C supplements (at least 200 mg/day), as well as a reduction in severity of symptoms when colds did occur [37]. A subsequent meta-analysis in 2023 showed that Vitamin C usage may reduce the severity of cold symptoms by 15 percent (95% CI 9-21 percent) [38].

- **Echinacea**– Echinacea has not been found to have efficacy for treatment of upper respiratory tract infections (including the common cold). This is discussed separately. (See"Clinical use of echinacea", section on 'Treatment'.)

### Other

- **Codeine**– Althoughcodeineis effective in suppressing chronic cough, trials in patients with acute cough due to the common cold have found no consistent benefit of codeine compared with placebo [21].

- **Glucocorticoids**– We do not use either intranasal or oral glucocorticoids for the treatment of the common cold.

**Intranasal glucocorticoids**- Multiple small trials indicate that intranasal glucocorticoids are not effective in reducing the duration or severity of symptoms associated with the common cold [39,40]

**Oral glucocorticoids**- Oral glucocorticoids have not been well-studied for the common cold [40]; we do not use them due to the lack of proven benefit and potential for adverse effects. In one small study of 47 males with rhinovirus, those who received oralprednisonehad similar symptom scores compared with those who received placebo, but viral titers were higher in the group receiving prednisone [41].

- **Humidified air**– We do not routinely recommend humidified air in the treatment of the common cold. For patients who would like to use a humidifier, we generally prefer cool mist rather than heated humidification to avoid the risk of thermal injury. However, only heated humidification has been examined in clinical studies and does not have a clear benefit in the treatment of the common cold.

- A 2017 systematic review (six trials, 387 participants) with two trials providing data for pooling did not find unequivocal evidence that warm vapor inhalation reduced symptoms [42]. Results were inconsistent regarding its effect on nasal airway resistance, and one study showed no difference in viral shedding between treatment and placebo groups. Minor side effects (eg, nasal discomfort or irritation) were infrequently reported. Another randomized trial (performed in a "real-world" clinical setting) that was not included in the systematic review also found no symptomatic benefit for the use of heated, humidified air and an infrequent (2 percent) incidence of mild thermal injury [3].
