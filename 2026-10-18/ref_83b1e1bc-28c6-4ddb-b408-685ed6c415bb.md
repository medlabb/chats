- The AAP recommends against dextromethorphan-containing medications for the treatment of cough associated with viral respiratory infections [3,54].

- Caregivers frequently seek interventions to suppress cough. They should be educated that cough clears secretions from the respiratory tract, and suppression of cough may result in retention of secretions and potentially harmful airway obstruction [3,12].

- **Expectorants and mucolytics**– OTC expectorants (eg,guaifenesin) and mucolytics (eg,acetylcysteine, bromhexine, letosteine) have no proven benefit in children [10,12,43]. By itself, guaifenesin is relatively safe, causing only mild gastrointestinal irritation. However, in OTC medications, guaifenesin is usually combined with other ingredients that may be harmful in children [55]. The adverse effects of mucolytics include bronchospasm, gastrointestinal disturbance, and fever [12].

As with all medications, OTC cough and cold remedies should be stored out of the reach of children. Data from the Pediatric Cough and Cold Safety Surveillance System (2009 to 2014) indicate that approximately two-thirds of adverse events related to OTC cough and cold medications among children <12 years are related to accidental unsupervised ingestion [49]. (See'Management'above and"Over-the-counter cough and cold preparations: Approach to pediatric poisoning".)

Caregivers should be educated about appropriate doses of acetaminophen and ibuprofen based on the child's weight. (See"Fever in infants and children: Pathophysiology and management", section on 'Management of fever'.)
If an analgesic is used, it is important to counsel caregivers against the concomitant use of a combination OTC cough and cold medication because these often contain an analgesic. The concurrent use of multiple medications that contain the same ingredient (eg,acetaminophen) can lead to an overdose of that ingredient. (See"Clinical manifestations and diagnosis of acetaminophen (paracetamol) poisoning in children and adolescents", section on 'Inappropriate dosing by a caregiver'.)
### Nasal symptoms
Nasal symptoms include rhinitis and nasal congestion/obstruction. Nasal obstruction can interfere with adequate fluid intake and may be the most bothersome symptom in infants and young children [10]. Nasal irrigation withsalinecan improve nasal symptoms of the common cold  (table 2) [59].
- For nasal symptoms that do not improve with supportive care, our advice for symptomatic therapies varies according to age:

- **<12 years**– For children <12 years of age with nasal symptoms from the common cold, we advise caregivers not to use OTC decongestants or decongestant/antihistamine combinations because the evidence of benefit is limited, and there are associated adverse effects [60]. Instead, we generally advise caregivers to increase the frequency of nasal suction, sprays, or irrigation.

- **≥12 years**– For children ≥12 years of age with nasal symptoms from the common cold, we advise caregivers to increase the frequency of nasal irrigation or use an OTC decongestant (oral or topical). # SUMMARY AND RECOMMENDATIONS

- **Caregiver education**– The common cold is usually a mild and self-limiting viral illness. Supportive care and caregiver education are the mainstays of management. Caregiver education should include information about the expected course of illness, the importance of supportive care, the limited role of over-the-counter (OTC) medications, the lack of benefit from antibiotics, education about therapies with no proven benefit, and indications for reevaluation. (See'Discussion points'above.)

- In infants and young children, the symptoms usually peak on day 2 to 3 of illness and then gradually improve over 10 to 14 days  (figure 1). In older children and adolescents, symptoms usually resolve in five to seven days. A follow-up visit may be warranted if the symptoms worsen or exceed the expected duration. (See'Severe, worsening, or persistent symptoms'above.)

- We counsel caregivers that OTC cough and cold medications should be**avoided**in children <6 years. We generally do not use OTC cough and cold medications in children between 6 and 12 years of age. These medications have unproven benefit in children and may have serious adverse effects  (table 3). (See'Limited role of over-the-counter medications'above.)

- **Supportive care**– General supportive care measures include (see'General measures'above):

Maintain adequate hydration

Provide warm fluids for ingestion

Use a cool mist humidifier or vaporizer

Apply nasalsalineusing nose drops and a bulb syringe for infants and nasal spray or nasal irrigation for older children

- **Symptomatic therapy**– Symptomatic therapy may be warranted if the symptoms bother the child (eg, interrupting sleep, interfering with drinking, causing discomfort) or other family members and do not respond to supportive measures. It is best to use single-ingredient medications that target the most bothersome symptom(s). (See'Symptomatic therapy'above.)

**Discomfort due to fever, headache, or myalgia**– Analgesics may relieve fever, headache, or myalgia.Acetaminophencan be used in children >3 months, andibuprofencan be used in children >6 months.Aspirinshould not be administered to children. (See'Discomfort due to fever, headache, or myalgia'above.)

**Nasal symptoms**– In children ≥12 years, an OTC decongestant can be used for relief of bothersome nasal symptoms. In children <12 years of age, we generally avoid decongestants since they appear to have limited efficacy, and the risk of adverse effects may be increased in this age group. Nasal suctioning, sprays, or irrigation are the mainstay of treatment for bothersome nasal symptoms in children <12 years of age. (See'Nasal symptoms'above.)

**Cough**– Options for treating cough in children include honey (appropriate for children ≥1 year of age) and lozenges/hard candy (appropriate for children >6 years of age who have no aspiration risk). These are generally safe and may provide some relief. (See'Cough'above.)

**Sore throat**– Symptomatic relief of sore throat is discussed separately. (See"Acute pharyngitis in children and adolescents: Symptomatic treatment".)
