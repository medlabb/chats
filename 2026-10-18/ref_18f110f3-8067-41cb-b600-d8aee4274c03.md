## Symptomatic treatment
The treatment of an infant or child with a cold is different than treatment recommended for adults. Antihistamines, decongestants, cough medicines, and expectorants, alone and in combinations, are all marketed for the symptoms of a cold. However, there have been few clinical trials of these products in infants and children.
The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) advisory panel has recommended against the use of these medications in children younger than six years [2]. We agree with this recommendation because these medications are not proven to be effective and have the potential to cause dangerous side effects. For children older than six years, cold medications may have fewer risks; however, there is still no proven benefit.

### Vitamins and herbal remedies

- **Vitamin C**–Vitamin Cis often touted as a natural remedy for the common cold, and while the available data are numerous, they are of varied quality and frequently in opposition to each other. A 2013 meta-analysis of 29 trials (n = 11,306) showed a small reduction in the duration of cold symptoms in adults regularly taking vitamin C supplements (at least 200 mg/day), as well as a reduction in severity of symptoms when colds did occur [37]. A subsequent meta-analysis in 2023 showed that Vitamin C usage may reduce the severity of cold symptoms by 15 percent (95% CI 9-21 percent) [38].

- **Echinacea**– Echinacea has not been found to have efficacy for treatment of upper respiratory tract infections (including the common cold). This is discussed separately. (See"Clinical use of echinacea", section on 'Treatment'.)

### Other

- **Codeine**– Althoughcodeineis effective in suppressing chronic cough, trials in patients with acute cough due to the common cold have found no consistent benefit of codeine compared with placebo [21].

- **Glucocorticoids**– We do not use either intranasal or oral glucocorticoids for the treatment of the common cold.

**Intranasal glucocorticoids**- Multiple small trials indicate that intranasal glucocorticoids are not effective in reducing the duration or severity of symptoms associated with the common cold [39,40]

**Oral glucocorticoids**- Oral glucocorticoids have not been well-studied for the common cold [40]; we do not use them due to the lack of proven benefit and potential for adverse effects. In one small study of 47 males with rhinovirus, those who received oralprednisonehad similar symptom scores compared with those who received placebo, but viral titers were higher in the group receiving prednisone [41].

- **Humidified air**– We do not routinely recommend humidified air in the treatment of the common cold. For patients who would like to use a humidifier, we generally prefer cool mist rather than heated humidification to avoid the risk of thermal injury. However, only heated humidification has been examined in clinical studies and does not have a clear benefit in the treatment of the common cold.

- A 2017 systematic review (six trials, 387 participants) with two trials providing data for pooling did not find unequivocal evidence that warm vapor inhalation reduced symptoms [42]. Results were inconsistent regarding its effect on nasal airway resistance, and one study showed no difference in viral shedding between treatment and placebo groups. Minor side effects (eg, nasal discomfort or irritation) were infrequently reported. Another randomized trial (performed in a "real-world" clinical setting) that was not included in the systematic review also found no symptomatic benefit for the use of heated, humidified air and an infrequent (2 percent) incidence of mild thermal injury [3].

## Therapies that may be effective
The following therapies may be effective for some individuals and are options for patients with moderate to severe symptoms. Choice of therapy will depend on what symptoms predominate. Importantly, there are no data supporting any single universal, highly effective treatment option for the common cold and we do not favor any one of the following treatments over others.
### Analgesics
Available data suggest thatacetaminophenand nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) are roughly equivalent at relieving some symptoms (eg, headache, ear pain, muscle and joint pains, malaise, and sneezing) associated with common cold, and that short courses of standard doses in this setting are generally safe and well-tolerated [3-5]. In one randomized trial involving nearly 400 patients with upper respiratory tract infections, acetaminophen and NSAIDs were more effective than placebo in reducing headache, achiness, and feverish discomfort [4]. NSAIDs may be helpful in decreasing some cold symptoms (headache, ear pain, muscle and joint pain), but a meta-analysis found that NSAIDS did not improve cough or nasal discharge and did not significantly reduce the total symptom score or duration of colds [5].
### Antihistamine/decongestant combinations
The combination of antihistamines and decongestants may be more beneficial than either component alone [6-8]. Antihistamine use alone in patients with the common cold is of minimal benefit and frequently results in troublesome side effects. (See'Antihistamines'below.)
In a systematic review of combination products, antihistamine-decongestant products were shown to be effective in reducing symptoms compared with control interventions (odds ratio of therapy failure 0.31; 95% CI 0.20 to 0.48; number needed to treat for benefit = 4) [6]. More patients experienced adverse effects (eg, drowsiness, dry mouth, insomnia, and dizziness) with antihistamine-decongestant products, although the difference was not statistically significant (31 versus 13 percent; OR 1.58, 95% CI 0.78 to 3.21).
### Intranasal/inhaled cromolyn sodium
Various formulations of intranasal or inhalationalcromolynsodium are available over the counter. Cromolyn sodium administered intranasally and/or by inhalation may improve cold symptoms. A randomized study of 118 adult patients with symptoms of rhinorrhea, throat pain, or cough for less than 24 hours compared the use of sodium cromoglycate dry powder (20 mg per inhalation in spincaps), sodium cromoglycate aqueous nasal spray (5.2 mg per dose), or matching placebos every two hours during waking hours on days 1 and 2, and four times daily on days 3 to 7 [9]. Symptoms resolved faster and were less severe in patients treated with sodium cromoglycate than with placebo. Side effects were mild and did not differ among the three treatment groups.
### Intranasal ipratropium bromide
Symptoms of rhinorrhea and sneezing may be improved by the use of intranasalipratropiumbromide, though nasal congestion is not affected. A systematic review of seven trials comparing ipratropium and placebo (2144 participants) found improvement in rhinorrhea with ipratropium, although the possibility of inadequate blinding raises the potential for bias [10]. The systematic review found a twofold increase in side effects (nasal dryness, blood-tinged mucus, and epistaxis) in patients assigned to ipratropium.

### Cough

- **Honey**– In children ≥1 year of age, we use honey to treat cough that has not responded to supportive measures.

- Honey (2.5 to 5 mL [0.5 to 1 teaspoon]) can be administered at full strength or diluted in liquid (eg, tea, juice [35]). Honey should be avoided in children younger than one year of age because of the risk of botulism. (See"Botulism", section on 'Infant botulism'.)

- In a meta-analysis comparing honey with usual care to treat the symptoms of upper respiratory tract infections in children and adults, honey reduced cough frequency (standard mean difference [SMD] -0.36, 95% CI -0.50 to -0.21; eight trials, 832 participants) and cough severity (SMD -0.44, 95% CI -0.64 to -0.25; five trials, 598 participants) [64]. Adverse effects were uncommon and mild (eg, nausea).

- Given the relative safety and low cost of honey, the World Health Organization, AAP, and American College of Chest Physicians all suggest honey to treat cough from the common cold in children who are older than one year [12,35,65].

- **Lozenges**– Hard candy or lozenges are a reasonable alternative to honey for treating cough in children older than six years who have no aspiration risk.

- Although there is no evidence from controlled trials that cough lozenges and hard candy are effective in decreasing cough, they are unlikely to be harmful [12]. The AAP suggests that cough lozenges or hard candy may be used to coat the irritated throat caused by cough in children older than six years [35].

### Sore throat
Symptomatic relief of sore throat in children and adolescents is discussed separately. (See"Acute pharyngitis in children and adolescents: Symptomatic treatment".)
### Middle ear symptoms
Analgesics can be used to relieve pain caused by increased middle ear pressure. Decongestants and decongestant-antihistamine combinations have no proven benefit in the treatment of middle ear abnormalities, such as conductive hearing loss, in children with the common cold. Although abnormalities of middle ear pressure associated with the common cold may predispose to the development of acute otitis media, in a prospective crossover study, treatment with a decongestant-antihistamine did not prevent the development of acute otitis media [66].

## Symptomatic therapy

### Limited role of over-the-counter medications
Symptoms of the common cold do not need to be treated unless they bother the child or other family members (eg, interrupting sleep, interfering with staying hydrated, causing discomfort) and have not responded to supportive care [35]. (See'General measures'above.)
Our general advice to caregivers about the use of OTC cough and cold medications depends on the age of the child as follows:
- **<6 years**– We counsel that OTC cough and cold medications should be avoided.

- **6 to 12 years**– We generally advise caregivers that OTC cough and cold medications be avoided. However, there are times when shared decision-making may lead to the use of OTC cough and cold medications, which have some evidence of benefit and minimal risk.

- **≥12 years**– OTC decongestants may provide symptomatic relief of nasal symptoms in adolescents ≥12 years. (See'Nasal symptoms'below.)

In randomized trials, systematic reviews, and meta-analyses, OTC cough and cold medications have not been proven to work any better than placebo in children and may have serious side effects [36-45]. OTC cough and cold medications have been associated with fatal overdose in children younger than two years [46-50]. OTC medications have the potential for enhanced toxicity in young children because metabolism, clearance, and drug effects may vary according to age. Safe dosing recommendations have not been established for children [15]. (See"Over-the-counter cough and cold preparations: Approach to pediatric poisoning".)
Symptomatic therapies have associated risks, particularly in young children  (table 3). If caregivers choose to administer OTC medications to treat the common cold in children >6 years old, they should be advised to use single-ingredient medications for the most bothersome symptom, and the clinician should provide them with proper dosing, storage, and administration instructions to avoid potential toxicity [15]. As an example, inverting the container rather than holding it upright when administering intranasal medication may provide a dose that is 20 to 30 times greater than recommended [44].
The following OTC medications have no proven benefit for the treatment of the common cold in children:
- **Antihistamines**– Antihistamines are not effective for the treatment of the common cold in children. In randomized trials, neither antihistamines nor combination antihistamine-decongestants have been effective in relieving nasal symptoms or cough in children with the common cold [37,39,40,51]. Furthermore, these medications may have adverse effects, including sedation, paradoxical excitability, respiratory depression, hallucinations, and increased seizure risk  (table 3) [52].Diphenhydramineis the most common OTC medication associated with pediatric fatality [50].

- **Antitussives**– OTC antitussive medications (eg,dextromethorphan) may provide some relief from cough, but they may also cause harm [37,41,46]. (See"Dextromethorphan misuse and poisoning: Clinical features and diagnosis", section on 'Epidemiology'.)

- In one trial (128 children, 6 to 12 years of age) comparing the antitussive efficacy ofdextromethorphanwith placebo, there was a greater reduction in cough frequency and self-reported cough severity in the dextromethorphan group [53]; however, this benefit only occurred during daytime hours. Additional trials that assess the safety and efficacy of dextromethorphan in children are needed before this medication can be recommended.

## The common cold

### Clinical findings and diagnosis
Nasal congestion is the most common symptom; other common symptoms include sore throat, cough, and malaise. Fever is uncommon; conjunctivitis occurs variably. The diagnosis of the common cold is clinical, based upon these reported symptoms and/or observed signs. (See"The common cold in adults: Diagnosis and clinical features"and"The common cold in adults: Treatment and prevention".)
### Treatment
As in nonpregnant patients, symptoms are usually mild and self-limited and generally neither require nor respond well to intervention. However, many pregnant people with the common cold will seek advice and symptomatic therapy from their clinician.
Patients with the common cold can be reassured that their symptoms will generally resolve within 10 days, although the cough may linger longer. Drug therapy may relieve some symptoms; however, treatment does not shorten the duration of symptoms, and the risks of drug therapy for the common cold in pregnancy have not been studied in randomized trials. If the patient still seeks symptomatic relief after being informed of the self-limited nature of the illness, the limited efficacy of all available treatments, and the absence of high-quality evidence of safety, they should be steered toward those agents with the most evidence of lack of fetal harm.
