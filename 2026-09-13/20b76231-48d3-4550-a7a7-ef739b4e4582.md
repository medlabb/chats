## History and physical examination
For patients with suspected MD, we perform a comprehensive clinical evaluation, including a detailed history, neurologic examination, and otologic evaluation. Specific components and techniques for the neurologic and otologic examinations are described in detail elsewhere. (See"Evaluation of the patient with vertigo", section on 'Examination'and"Evaluation of hearing loss in adults", section on 'Examination'.)
### Findings that warrant urgent evaluation
The first step of the clinical evaluation is to identify patients who require urgent testing and management.
- **Neurologic deficits or central vertigo features**– Patients with associated neurologic symptoms (eg, dysarthria), neurologic deficits on examination, or central features of vertigo  (table 2) require urgent neuroimaging and neurology evaluation. These findings are not consistent with MD and suggest a serious central neurologic abnormality (eg, vertebrobasilar ischemia). The causes of central vertigo and detailed evaluation of patients with central vertigo findings are discussed in detail elsewhere. (See"Evaluation of the patient with vertigo", section on 'Diagnostic approach'and"Causes of vertigo", section on 'Central etiologies'.)

- **Sudden sensorineural hearing loss**– Patients who present with sudden sensorineural hearing loss (SSNHL) require urgent audiometry and otolaryngology evaluation. We distinguish sensorineural from conductive hearing loss using the Weber and Rinne tests  (figure 2andtable 3); if testing suggests sensorineural hearing loss, urgent audiometry is necessary to establish the diagnosis of SSNHL  (table 4). (See"Sudden sensorineural hearing loss in adults", section on 'Distinguish sensorineural from conductive hearing loss'.)

- Early treatment with glucocorticoid therapy (oral or intratympanic) may help improve hearing recovery for patients with SSNHL, even if MD is the known or suspected underlying diagnosis. (See"Sudden sensorineural hearing loss in adults", section on 'Glucocorticoid therapy for all patients'.)

### Confirming and categorizing vertigo
After ruling out situations that warrant urgent testing or referral, we confirm and categorize the vertigo to ensure symptoms are consistent with MD and not suggestive of an alternative diagnosis.
- **Description of attacks –**We ask patients to describe their attacks and confirm that they are experiencing true vertigo (ie, an illusion of motion), which is usually described as a "spinning" sensation, but may also be described as "rocking," "whirling," or "tilting." We distinguish this from other forms of dizziness, including presyncope (ie, the prodromal symptom of fainting or a near faint) and disequilibrium (ie, a sense of imbalance, primarily with walking). A detailed approach for distinguishing these symptoms, as well as subsequent management of other forms of dizziness, is discussed in detail elsewhere. (See"Approach to the patient with dizziness".)

- **Duration of attacks**– The duration of vertigo attacks is a key feature to help distinguish between causes of episodic vertigo. In MD, symptoms usually last between 20 minutes and 12 hours, and occasionally as long as 24 hours. Shorter or longer symptom duration may suggest vestibular migraines, which can last anywhere from five minutes to 72 hours. Very short attacks (eg, less than five minutes) may suggest benign paroxysmal positional vertigo, whereas attack duration longer than 72 hours or chronic disequilibrium may suggest vestibular schwannoma.

## Neuroimaging to exclude alternative diagnoses
Imaging is not required to establish a diagnosis of MD but is appropriate in most patients to exclude important conditions with similar symptoms (eg, vestibular schwannoma). We obtain imaging in patients with any of the following clinical features:
- **Central features of vertigo or associated neurologic signs or symptoms**– These features are not consistent with a diagnosis of MD and require prompt imaging and neurology evaluation. Choice of imaging depends in part on the most likely suspected diagnosis. As examples, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain and magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) of the head and neck are appropriate in patients with suspected vertebrobasilar ischemia, and MRI of the brain and spinal cord is appropriate for patients with suspected MS. (See"Evaluation of the patient with vertigo", section on 'Brain imaging'and"Posterior circulation cerebrovascular syndromes"and"Evaluation and diagnosis of multiple sclerosis in adults", section on 'Evaluation'.)

- **Asymmetric or progressive hearing loss**–****Patients with asymmetric or progressive hearing loss (which describes the majority of patients with suspected MD) should have an MRI with gadolinium of the internal auditory canal and posterior fossa to rule out a vestibular schwannoma or other retrocochlear lesion.

- **Recurrent attacks after diet and lifestyle modification**–****We obtain MRI with gadolinium of the internal auditory canal and posterior fossa in patients with recurrent attacks after diet and lifestyle modification. Although guidelines are not definitive about the need for neuroimaging in this setting [27], we prefer to rule out structural auditory canal or posterior fossa abnormalities prior to initiating pharmacotherapy.

In contrast, patients who have infrequent episodes of self-limited vertigo without associated asymmetric or progressive hearing loss and without associated neurologic symptoms may not need imaging, particularly if their symptoms resolve with simple diet and lifestyle measures. (See'Initial diet and lifestyle measures for all patients'below.)
MRI of the internal auditory canal and posterior fossa requires specialized images and interpretation that may not be available in all facilities. Some specialists, including UpToDate authors, prefer to order and interpret these studies themselves because of the specific instructions and expertise required to detect subtle findings (eg, slight inflammation of the vestibulocochlear nerve that may indicate an inflammatory process). Of note, MRI imaging may identify features that support a diagnosis of MD, such as a less prominent endolymphatic duct, but these findings are not diagnostic [32].
Although MRI is the preferred testing modality, computed tomography (CT) scan with contrast can be performed when MRI is contraindicated. (See"Principles of magnetic resonance imaging", section on 'Precautions'.)

## Patients with acute-onset, sustained vertigo
Common diagnoses in this setting include vestibular neuritis, demyelinating disease, and a stroke in the brainstem or cerebellum. Clinicians use the presence of cerebrovascular risk factors and associated neurologic deficits to determine the relative probability of a cerebrovascular etiology [71]. The HINTS examination, in particular the head impulse test  (figure 4), can also be particularly useful in this setting to distinguish a peripheral cause of vertigo (eg, vestibular neuritis) from a central, cerebrovascular cause  (figure 1). In a young patient without cerebrovascular risk factors in whom a normal head impulse test suggests a localization within the central nervous system, multiple sclerosis might be more likely. However, the utility of the head impulse test or the HINTS examination has not been specifically studied in this setting. (See'Head impulse test'above and'Other vestibular signs'above.)
