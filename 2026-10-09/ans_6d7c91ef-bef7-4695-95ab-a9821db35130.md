 ## Workup of bloody urine (hematuria)

1. **Confirm that blood is truly present**
   - Perform urinalysis with microscopy. Dipstick positivity without red blood cells may indicate **myoglobinuria**, often associated with rhabdomyolysis. (1)
   - Consider transient causes such as menstruation, viral illness, exercise, allergy, or mild trauma; exercise-related hematuria may be reassessed after 1–2 weeks of rest. (2)

2. **Characterize the urine sediment**
   - **Isolated hematuria** without proteinuria, other cells, or casts suggests bleeding from the urinary tract. (1)
   - **Dysmorphic RBCs, RBC casts, and protein excretion >500 mg/day** strongly suggest glomerulonephritis and warrant serologic evaluation and renal biopsy as outlined in **Figure 55-2, “Approach to the patient with hematuria.”** (1)
   - Proteinuria, hypertension, or evidence of renal dysfunction supports a glomerular process. (3)

3. **Assess for infection**
   - Hematuria with pyuria and bacteriuria is typical of infection; obtain appropriate urine cultures and treat when indicated. (1)
   - Lower urinary tract symptoms include dysuria, frequency, urgency, incomplete emptying, and poor urinary stream. (2)

4. **Identify the likely anatomic source**
   - Flank pain or ureteral colic suggests a stone or obstructing lesion. Heavy bleeding with clots is more characteristic of a **lower urinary tract/postrenal source**, although trauma can also cause clots. (2)
   - Review prior urinalyses to establish whether the hematuria is persistent or chronic. (2)
   - Perform abdominal, genital, and rectal examination when obstruction, prostate disease, or a pelvic mass is possible. (4)

5. **Evaluate persistent, significant, or gross hematuria**
   - Significant hematuria includes **more than 3 RBCs/HPF on three urinalyses, more than 100 RBCs on a single urinalysis, or gross hematuria**. (1)
   - Recent-onset hematuria from approximately the fourth decade onward should undergo urologic evaluation, usually including cystoscopy. (2)
   - For painless gross or microscopic hematuria, evaluation should include:
     - **Urine cytology**
     - **Cystoscopy**
     - Imaging of the kidneys and upper urinary tract with **CT urography**; an MR urogram may be substituted in patients with poor renal function. (5)

6. **Image when obstruction or stones are suspected**
   - If urinary tract obstruction is suspected, insert a bladder catheter when indicated and perform abdominal ultrasonography to assess the kidneys, bladder, and hydronephrosis. (4)
   - For acute flank pain, **noncontrast CT** is described as the test of choice for identifying stones and obstruction. (4)

7. **Further evaluation of suspected malignancy**
   - If bladder or upper-tract abnormalities are found, endoscopic resection for histologic diagnosis and staging should be performed when possible. If the bladder is normal but an upper-tract tumor is suspected, visualization may be performed with ureteroscopy or retrograde pyelography. (5)
