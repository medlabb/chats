## Answer

 ## Management of hyperkalemia in rhabdomyolysis

Rhabdomyolysis causes potassium release from damaged muscle and may produce rapid, severe hyperkalemia, particularly with impaired kidney function. (1) Patients with rhabdomyolysis should be evaluated for electrolyte abnormalities, with particular attention to hyperkalemia. (2)

### If severe or symptomatic

Treat as a **hyperkalemic emergency** when there are symptoms or ECG changes, when potassium is generally >6.5 mEq/L, or when there is ongoing tissue breakdown such as rhabdomyolysis—even with moderate hyperkalemia in patients with impaired kidney function. (3)

- **IV calcium:** Give when ECG changes consistent with hyperkalemia are present; some experts also give it when potassium is >6.5 mEq/L despite absent ECG changes. Calcium stabilizes the cardiac membrane but does **not** remove potassium. (4)
- **IV insulin with IV glucose:** Temporarily shifts potassium from the extracellular space into cells. (4)
- If severe acute metabolic acidosis is present (**pH <7.1**), bicarbonate therapy with hyperventilation may help shift potassium intracellularly. (5)
- **Remove potassium from the body concurrently:**
  - Hemodialysis is the most efficient method and is indicated/preferred when kidney function is severely impaired or potassium cannot be controlled promptly. (6)
  - Use a gastrointestinal potassium binder, such as sodium zirconium cyclosilicate or patiromer, when appropriate; sodium zirconium cyclosilicate is generally preferred for more rapid onset. (6)
  - Loop diuretics may be used when kidney function and urine output are preserved, but they should not be the sole potassium-removal treatment in an emergency. (6)
- Obtain **urgent nephrology consultation** when hyperkalemia occurs with kidney-function impairment. (4)

### Rhabdomyolysis-specific supportive care

- Provide saline fluid repletion and closely monitor urine output, targeting a minimum urine flow of **4 mL/kg/hour or 200 mL/hour in adults**, according to the cited reference. (2)
- Address ongoing causes of muscle injury and discontinue causative drugs or toxins when relevant. (7)
- Rhabdomyolysis-associated hypocalcemia is usually transient; **avoid calcium administration unless severe symptoms, such as tetany, are present**, while recognizing that IV calcium may still be required for hyperkalemia-related ECG changes. (2)
