## Answer

 - **If this was a confirmed primary or nonprimary first-episode genital HSV infection within approximately 6 weeks of delivery**, the reference suggests cesarean delivery even when no lesions are present at labor, although optimal management is uncertain. (1)

- For an **asymptomatic infant whose mother has a history of genital HSV but no lesions at delivery**, the infant **does not routinely require blood PCR, surface swabs, CSF studies, or empiric acyclovir**. The infant should be clinically monitored for the first **six weeks**, and caregivers should be educated about concerning signs. (2)

- **CSF HSV PCR, CSF cell count/chemistries, blood PCR, surface PCR/cultures, liver enzymes, and CBC** are recommended in the reference for an asymptomatic infant born to a mother with **no prior HSV history but active genital lesions at delivery**, with empiric IV acyclovir while results are pending. (rf_35) This does not directly describe an infant born after maternal treatment with **no active lesions**.

- If the infant develops concerning clinical findings—such as vesicular rash, conjunctival lesions, irritability, or sepsis—the infant should undergo a **full diagnostic evaluation, including CSF assessment**, and receive empiric acyclovir pending results. (2) (3)

- Cesarean delivery **reduces but does not eliminate** neonatal HSV risk. The reference identifies cesarean delivery, rupture-of-membrane duration, maternal antibody status, and delivery route as factors affecting transmission risk. (1) (2) It does not provide a separate testing protocol specifically for elective cesarean delivery before rupture of membranes.

The maternal statement that “blood PCR is negative for IgG and IgM” is not interpretable as written, because IgG and IgM are serologic tests rather than blood PCR results. The selected references do not provide further guidance for resolving that result.
