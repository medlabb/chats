## Answer

 **Cholestasis** is impaired bile formation or flow and may be **intrahepatic** or **extrahepatic**.

### Causes

**Intrahepatic causes** include:

- Viral or alcoholic hepatitis, including fibrosing cholestatic hepatitis from hepatitis B or C
- Drug-induced cholestasis
- Primary biliary cholangitis and primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC)
- Vanishing bile duct syndrome
- Inherited disorders such as progressive familial intrahepatic cholestasis and benign recurrent intrahepatic cholestasis
- Cholestasis of pregnancy
- Total parenteral nutrition, sepsis, postoperative cholestasis, congestive hepatopathy, ischemic hepatitis, infiltrative disease, malaria, and leptospirosis (1)

**Extrahepatic causes** include:

- **Choledocholithiasis**, the most common cause
- Malignancies such as cholangiocarcinoma, pancreatic, gallbladder, or ampullary cancer
- Postoperative biliary strictures, PSC, chronic pancreatitis, AIDS cholangiopathy, Mirizzi syndrome, and parasitic disease (2)

### Clinical and laboratory features

Biliary obstruction may cause **jaundice, dark urine, and acholic stools**. Serum alkaline phosphatase is usually elevated and may rise before jaundice; aminotransferases can increase two- to tenfold, particularly with acute obstruction. After relief of obstruction, aminotransferases generally normalize rapidly, bilirubin may take 1–2 weeks, and alkaline phosphatase typically declines more slowly. (3)

### Selected diagnostic clues

- **Primary biliary cholangitis:** antimitochondrial antibody is detected in approximately 95% of patients. (1)
- **PSC:** MRCP demonstrates multifocal or segmental strictures, often producing a beaded appearance; approximately 75% of patients also have inflammatory bowel disease. (4)
- **IgG4-associated cholangitis:** may resemble PSC but is associated with elevated serum IgG4 and may respond to glucocorticoids. (4)
