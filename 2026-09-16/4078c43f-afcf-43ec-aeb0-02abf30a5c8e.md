## Answer

 In a **well-appearing 1-month-old with total bilirubin 14 mg/dL, predominantly indirect**, normal CBC and peripheral smear, the approach should be:

1. **Confirm and characterize the jaundice**
   - Repeat **total and direct (conjugated) bilirubin**, because jaundice persisting at or beyond 3 weeks requires fractionation. If the direct bilirubin is elevated, evaluate for cholestasis. (1)
   - Review the bilirubin trend and prior NICU records, including the peak bilirubin, gestational age, previous treatment, blood group/DAT results, and response to phototherapy.

2. **Detailed history and examination**
   - Assess feeding adequacy, frequency of breastfeeding, weight gain, dehydration or excessive weight loss, urine and stool color, fever or illness, family history of jaundice, and medication exposure.
   - Examine for hepatosplenomegaly, abnormal stool or urine pigmentation, and any abnormal neurobehavioral findings. (2)

3. **Laboratory evaluation**
   - If not already done, consider maternal and infant **ABO/Rh typing, DAT**, reticulocyte count, and **G6PD testing** when jaundice is unexplained, has an exponential rise, or responds poorly to phototherapy. (3)
   - Check newborn-screen results, particularly **thyroid and galactosemia screening**. If clinically indicated, evaluate for urinary infection; the reference specifically recommends urinalysis and urine culture when direct bilirubin is elevated. (1)
   - Liver enzymes and other hepatocellular investigations are mainly indicated when there is significant conjugated hyperbilirubinemia or evidence of liver disease. (3)

4. **Consider breast-milk jaundice only after exclusion of important causes**
   - A thriving, otherwise healthy breastfed infant with predominantly unconjugated bilirubin and no evidence of hemolysis may have breast-milk-associated jaundice. In such infants, observation may be reasonable when the bilirubin is not at a level requiring phototherapy, provided thyroid function is normal and urinary infection is not suspected. (4)
   - Brief interruption of breastfeeding or supplementation may lower bilirubin and can help support this diagnosis, but it should not be undertaken routinely unless clinically indicated. (4)

5. **Treatment and follow-up**
   - Management should be based on the **bilirubin level, gestational age, risk factors, hemolysis, clinical condition, and bilirubin trajectory**; intensive phototherapy is the main treatment when treatment thresholds are reached. (5)
   - Arrange close clinical and bilirubin follow-up, particularly because of the previous NICU admission. Seek urgent assessment if the infant becomes lethargic, has poor feeding, abnormal tone, abnormal movements, fever, appears ill, develops pale stools/dark urine, or has hepatosplenomegaly.

The selected references do not provide a specific phototherapy threshold for a **1-month-old** infant with bilirubin 14 mg/dL; therefore, treatment cannot be determined from the provided information alone.
