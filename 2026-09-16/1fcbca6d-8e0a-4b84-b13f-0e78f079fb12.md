## Answer

 A **1-month-old with persistent unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia** requires further evaluation, even if clinically well and the CBC/peripheral smear are normal, because jaundice persisting beyond 7 days warrants assessment for an underlying cause. (1)

**Suggested approach:**

1. **Repeat fractionated bilirubin**
   - Measure total and direct/conjugated bilirubin.
   - With a TSB ≥5 mg/dL, a direct bilirubin **>20% of the total** is concerning for cholestasis. (1)
   - Conjugated bilirubin >1 mg/dL is also considered abnormal in the cited reference. (2)

2. **Clinical assessment**
   - Assess feeding adequacy, urine and stool output, weight trajectory, and hydration.
   - Examine for hepatomegaly, splenomegaly, bruising, or cephalohematoma.
   - Check urine and stool pigmentation; pale/acholic stools or dark urine are concerning for cholestasis. (3) (1)

3. **Consider persistent unconjugated causes**
   - **Breast-milk jaundice**, particularly in an otherwise well, exclusively breastfed infant, is common and usually mild. (1) (2)
   - Despite a normal CBC and smear, consider **G6PD deficiency** and other inherited hemolytic disorders; G6PD enzyme activity should be obtained if not already performed. (1) (4)
   - Consider inherited disorders of bilirubin conjugation, including **Gilbert syndrome** and, if bilirubin is marked and persistent, **Crigler–Najjar syndrome**. (1)
   - Review the **newborn-screening results**, including screening for congenital hypothyroidism and inborn errors of metabolism. (1) (4)

4. **Assess for illness**
   - Fever, toxic appearance, or an abnormal white-cell count warrants prompt evaluation for sepsis and empiric antibiotics if clinically indicated. (5)

5. **Specialist involvement**
   - Clinically significant hyperbilirubinemia persisting beyond 14 days should undergo additional evaluation with expert consultation involving pediatric gastroenterology, hematology, and metabolic specialists. (4)
