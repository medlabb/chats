#### 2. Type 2 diabetes

Therapeutic recommendations are based on the relative contributions of β cell insufficiency and insulin insensitivity in individual patients. The possibility that the individual patient has a specific etiologic cause for their diabetes should always be considered, especially when the patient does not have a family history of type 2 diabetes or does not have any evidence of central obesity or insulin resistance. Such patients should be evaluated for other types of diabetes such as **autoimmune (type 1)**, prior pancreatic injury, or MODY ([Table 32–1](#papacmdt26_ch32tb1)). Patients with **autoimmune diabetes** should be prescribed insulin when the disease is diagnosed. Importantly, many patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus have a progressive loss of β cell function and will require additional therapeutic interventions with time.

###### (2) When initial pharmacologic therapy does not control glucose adequately

A second agent, usually a GLP-1 or GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonist or an SGLT-2 inhibitor, or [metformin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660576) is added. Combined GLP-1 or GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonist and SGLT-2 inhibitor therapies should be considered for patients with cardiovascular disease and/or kidney disease. Sulfonylureas have been available for many years, and their use in combination with [metformin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660576) is well established. They do, however, have the propensity to cause hypoglycemia and weight gain. In patients who experience hyperglycemia after a carbohydrate-rich meal (such as dinner), a short-acting secretagogue ([repaglinide](/drug.aspx?gbosId=658740) or [nateglinide](/drug.aspx?gbosId=658775)) before meals may suffice to get glucose levels into the target range. Patients with severe insulin resistance may be candidates for [pioglitazone](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660671). [Pioglitazone](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660671) may also reduce the risk for recurrent stroke in patients who have a history of stroke or transient ischemic attack. If two agents are inadequate, then a third agent is added. The combination of [metformin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660576), a GLP-1 receptor agonist, and an SGLT-2 inhibitor should be considered in patients with obesity, heart disease, and kidney disease, although data regarding efficacy of such combined therapy are limited.

When the combination of non-insulin agents fails to achieve euglycemia in patients with type 2 diabetes, insulin treatment should be instituted. Various insulin regimens may be effective. One proposed regimen is to continue the oral agents (and injectable GLP-1 or dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonists) and then simply add a bedtime dose of long-acting insulin analog (insulin glargine, or degludec) to reduce excessive nocturnal hepatic glucose output and improve fasting glucose levels. If the patient does not achieve target glucose levels during the day, then daytime insulin treatment can be initiated. If this regimen fails to achieve satisfactory glycemic goals or is associated with unacceptable frequency of hypoglycemic episodes, then a more intensive regimen of multiple insulin injections can be instituted as in patients with type 1 diabetes. [Metformin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660576) principally reduces hepatic glucose output, and it is reasonable to continue with this medication when insulin therapy is instituted. [Pioglitazone](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660671), which improves peripheral insulin sensitivity, can be used together with insulin but this combination is associated with more weight gain and peripheral edema. Sulfonylureas, GLP-1 receptor agonists, DPP-4 inhibitors, and SGLT-2 inhibitors also have been shown to be of continued benefit and may be continued with close monitoring for hypoglycemia. Weight-reducing interventions should continue even after initiation of insulin therapy and may allow for simplification of the therapeutic regimen in the future.

### C. Weight Management

One of the primary modes of therapy for patients with obesity and type 2 diabetes is weight reduction. Normalization of glycemia can be achieved by weight loss resulting in improved tissue sensitivity to insulin. A combination of caloric restriction, increased exercise, and behavioral modification is required if a weight reduction program is to be successful. Understanding the risks associated with the diagnosis of diabetes may motivate the patient to lose weight.

[Liraglutide](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659080) 3 mg subcutaneously daily (Saxenda), [semaglutide](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659353) 2.4 mg subcutaneously weekly (Wegovy), and [tirzepatide](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659575) 2.5 to 15 mg subcutaneously weekly (Zepbound) are approved for weight loss in persons with or without diabetes along with diet and exercise. In a 68-week study in adults without diabetes, patients taking [semaglutide](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659353) lost 14.9% of their initial body weight compared with 2.4% body weight loss in patients taking placebo. In a 72-week study, patients taking [tirzepatide](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659575) 15 mg lost about 21% of their initial body weight compared to 3.1 % with placebo. Common adverse reactions include nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea. Serious side effects include pancreatitis. The medication should not be used in patients with MEN 2 or personal or family history of medullary [thyroid](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660112) cancer.

Other medical options for weight loss include [orlistat](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660668), [phentermine](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659950)/[topiramate](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659464), and [naltrexone](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660421)/extended-release [bupropion](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660571) (see [Part 34](/content.aspx?legacysectionid=papacmdt26_ch34)). [Orlistat](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660668) (Xenical) is a reversible inhibitor of gastric and pancreatic lipases and prevents the hydrolysis and absorption of dietary triglycerides. It is available over the counter and in prescription strength. In 1-year studies in patients with obesity and type 2 diabetes, those taking [orlistat](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660668) had lost more weight, had lower HbA<sub>1c</sub> values, and had improved lipid profiles. The main adverse reactions were GI, with oily spotting, oily stool, flatus, and fecal urgency and frequency. Malabsorption of fat-soluble vitamins also occurs, and patients should take a multivitamin tablet containing fat-soluble vitamins at least 2 hours before or 2 hours after the administration of [orlistat](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660668). Cases of severe liver injury have been reported with this medication, although a cause-and-effect relationship has not been established.

[Phentermine](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659950) is a sympathomimetic amine stimulating release of [norepinephrine](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660259) from the hypothalamus. [Topiramate](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659464) is primarily used as an anticonvulsant, but it also appears to reduce appetite. In a 56-week phase 3 study, an extended-release preparation of [phentermine](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659950)/[topiramate](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659464) (Qsymia) together with diet and lifestyle intervention resulted in 10 kg weight loss (9.8%) compared to 1.4 kg (1.2%) with placebo. As might be expected, the diabetes subgroup on active therapy had greater reductions in HbA<sub>1c</sub> levels, and fewer patients with prediabetes on active therapy progressed to diabetes. The adverse events are consistent with those of the constituent drugs. The most common adverse reactions were paresthesia, dizziness, dysgeusia, insomnia, constipation, and dry mouth. [Topiramate](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659464) can worsen depression and increase risk of suicidal thoughts. It is also teratogenic, and the FDA has required the manufacturer to conduct a risk evaluation and mitigation strategy. The medication is only available through specialty mail-order pharmacies.
