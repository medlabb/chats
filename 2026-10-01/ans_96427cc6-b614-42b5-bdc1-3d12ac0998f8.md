## Answer

 **Management of hypernatremia** depends on how quickly it developed, the patient’s volume status, ongoing losses, and the underlying cause. Many patients require hospitalization. Treatment generally replaces the water deficit and ongoing water losses, while addressing causes of further losses (1, 2).

- **Assess volume status and urgency.** If there is significant hypovolemia, restoring intravascular volume with isotonic fluid takes priority over correcting sodium; water and electrolyte deficits may both need replacement (3).
- **Chronic hypernatremia (>48 hours):** The described initial adult regimen is intravenous 5% dextrose in water at about **1.35 mL/hour × body weight in kg**, up to 150 mL/hour. The goal is to lower serum sodium by about **10 mEq/L over 24 hours**, and avoid lowering it by more than **12 mEq/L in 24 hours**. Account for ongoing water losses when adjusting fluid replacement. Stable patients who are alert and able to drink may receive oral water, or water through a nasogastric tube (4, 2).
- **Acute hypernatremia (≤48 hours):** Management differs by cause. Acute salt poisoning may require urgent, aggressive lowering with hypotonic IV fluids or emergency hemodialysis. For rapid water losses due to arginine vasopressin disorders, the reference describes IV 5% dextrose in water with frequent sodium and glucose monitoring, plus treatment directed at the underlying disorder when appropriate (2, 5, 6).
- **Monitor and adjust:** Reassess fluid status clinically and follow sodium levels frequently; revise the fluid prescription according to the response and ongoing losses (3).

The treatment approach is also summarized in **algorithm 1** (4, 2).
