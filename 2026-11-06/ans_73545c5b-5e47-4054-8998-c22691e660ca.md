## Answer

 **Myopathy** refers to disorders involving structural changes or functional impairment of muscle, distinguished from lower motor neuron, peripheral nerve, and neuromuscular-junction disorders by clinical and laboratory findings. (1)

### Typical features
- Most commonly: **proximal, symmetric limb weakness** with preserved reflexes and sensation.
- Asymmetric or predominantly distal weakness may occur.
- Sensory loss suggests neuropathy or a central nervous system disorder rather than primary myopathy.
- Some myopathies cause **muscle pain, cramps, stiffness, contractures, or myotonia**. (2) (3)

**Myotonia** is prolonged muscle contraction followed by slow relaxation, often causing difficulty releasing an object after a firm grasp. It occurs in conditions such as myotonic dystrophy, myotonia congenita, hyperkalemic periodic paralysis, potassium-sensitive myotonia, and paramyotonia congenita. (3)

### Evaluation
Assessment may include:
- **Serum CK**
- Endocrine studies, including thyroid function, parathyroid hormone, and vitamin D
- Autoantibodies
- Forearm exercise testing
- Electromyography and nerve-conduction studies
- Muscle biopsy
- Genetic testing
- Muscle MRI or ultrasound (rf_99)

CK is the most sensitive measure of muscle damage, although it may also rise after strenuous activity, trauma, prolonged cramps, or generalized seizures. (rf_99) EMG and nerve-conduction studies help distinguish myopathy from motor-neuron disease, neuropathy, and neuromuscular-junction disorders. (rf_99)

The pattern of weakness is diagnostically useful; **Table 460-1** classifies myopathies by patterns such as proximal, distal, axial, ocular, episodic weakness or myoglobinuria, and muscle stiffness/decreased ability to relax. (2)
