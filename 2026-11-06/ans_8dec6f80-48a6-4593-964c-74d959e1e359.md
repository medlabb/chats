## Answer

 **Cramps** are typically painful, involuntary muscle contractions relieved by passive stretching. They are often benign and occur at night or during/after exercise. Treatment should first address reversible causes such as **dehydration, electrolyte disturbances, pregnancy, uremia, hypothyroidism, adrenal insufficiency, peripheral vascular disease, motor neuron disease, polyneuropathy, or medication effects**. (1)

- **During a cramp:** Passive stretching typically relieves the contraction. For nocturnal cramps, locally applied **heat or cold** may help. (1)  
- **If no reversible cause is found:** Vitamin B supplementation, diphenhydramine, calcium-channel blockers, or antiseizure medicines such as phenytoin, carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, or baclofen have been used, but the reference states that convincing evidence of efficacy is lacking. Levetiracetam and gabapentin may occasionally help. (1)  
- **Nocturnal cramps:** Some patients may respond to bedtime phenytoin, carbamazepine, diazepam, or quinine. **Quinine should not be prescribed routinely** because it can rarely cause serious hematologic complications; it should generally be reserved for disabling cramps that fail other approaches. (1)  
- Vitamin B complex and calcium-channel blockers such as diltiazem or verapamil may occasionally help, but evidence is limited or anecdotal. (2)

These treatment options should be discussed with a clinician, particularly because several require monitoring and may have significant adverse effects. Table **9-19, “Motor-Unit Hyperactivity States,”** lists phenytoin, carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, baclofen, and quinine as treatments used for cramps. (3)
