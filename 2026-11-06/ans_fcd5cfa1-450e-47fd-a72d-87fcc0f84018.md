## Answer

 ## **Hypokalemia** (low potassium)

- **Definition:** Serum potassium **<3.5 mEq/L (3.5 mmol/L)**. (1)
- It is often asymptomatic, but severe hypokalemia can cause **muscle weakness, rhabdomyolysis, ECG changes, and potentially life-threatening arrhythmias**. (2; 3)
- ECG changes may include **T-wave flattening, ST depression, T-wave inversion, and U waves**. (3)

### Common causes
- **Potassium shifting into cells:** alkalosis, insulin, β-adrenergic agonists, or hypokalemic periodic paralysis. (2)
- **Gastrointestinal loss:** diarrhea, vomiting, or laxative abuse. (4)
- **Renal loss:** loop or thiazide diuretics, magnesium deficiency, renal tubular acidosis, Bartter or Gitelman syndrome, and excess aldosterone activity. (2; 4)
- **Magnesium deficiency:** can cause persistent renal potassium loss and make hypokalemia resistant to treatment until magnesium is corrected. (2; 5)

### Evaluation
Serum potassium should be assessed along with **magnesium**, and the **urine potassium-to-creatinine ratio** can help distinguish renal from nonrenal potassium loss. (1; 2)

### Treatment
- Treat the underlying cause and discontinue causative drugs when appropriate. Correct magnesium deficiency, particularly in refractory cases. (6)
- **Oral potassium** is generally safest for mild-to-moderate deficiency. (6)
- **Intravenous potassium** is generally reserved for severe hypokalemia below **3.0 mEq/L** and requires careful monitoring. (6)
- Symptomatic or severe hypokalemia below **2.5 mEq/L**, especially with cardiac manifestations, requires hospital admission and cardiac monitoring. (7)
