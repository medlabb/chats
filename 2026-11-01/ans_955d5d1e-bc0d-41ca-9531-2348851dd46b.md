## Answer

 ## MDR gram-negative bacteria: summary

**MDR gram-negative bacilli** include resistant **Enterobacterales**, *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*, *Acinetobacter baumannii*, and *Stenotrophomonas maltophilia*. Enterobacterales may acquire resistance genes or develop resistance through mutation and selection, while *Pseudomonas*, *Acinetobacter*, and *Stenotrophomonas* have intrinsic resistance and may acquire additional mechanisms. Important resistance mechanisms include **ESBLs and carbapenemases**. (1)

Resistance is particularly important in bloodstream infections and may be associated with healthcare exposure, prior antibiotic exposure, and spread between patients or healthcare facilities. (1) For *Acinetobacter*, colonization is common; positive cultures from sterile sites should be regarded as infection, whereas cultures from nonsterile sites should be treated only when clinical evidence supports active infection. (2)

## When to choose combination antibiotics

### 1. Empiric therapy for severe infection with high risk of resistance

Combination therapy may be considered when the consequences of initially inactive therapy are especially serious:

- **Sepsis or septic shock**, particularly gram-negative bacteremia
- **Severe neutropenia or immunosuppression**
- Known or suspected MDR *P. aeruginosa*
- Burn patients with serious infection
- High local resistance to the chosen empiric agent—approximately **>10–20%**, depending on the clinical context
- Moderate-to-severe infection when MDR *Acinetobacter* is suspected (3) (4) (5)

For suspected serious *P. aeruginosa* infection, empiric combination therapy generally means **two agents from different classes with in-vitro activity**, commonly a beta-lactam plus an aminoglycoside; a fluoroquinolone may be used instead of an aminoglycoside when renal insufficiency or high local aminoglycoside resistance is present. (4)

The main rationale is to increase the likelihood that **at least one empiric agent is active** while susceptibility results are pending. (3) (4)

### 2. Directed therapy for highly resistant isolates

For MDR *Acinetobacter*, the regimen should be guided by susceptibility testing:

- If multiple first-line agents are active: combine two active agents from different classes.
- If only one first-line agent is active: combine it with a susceptible second-line agent.
- If all first-line agents are resistant: combination of two active second-line agents may be used if possible.
- If only one or very few agents remain active, more complex regimens—including triple therapy—may be considered, but outcomes are uncertain and expert consultation is advised. (6)

For extensively resistant organisms, combination therapy may be used with the agent having the lowest MIC plus a susceptible aminoglycoside or polymyxin, if possible. For isolates resistant to all available antibiotics, evidence is minimal and specialist consultation is recommended. (7)

### 3. Carbapenem-resistant pathogens

Polymyxins such as colistin may be combined with another agent because resistance to colistin can emerge during treatment. Novel beta-lactamase-inhibitor combinations may be alternatives when active, but susceptibility data and clinical evidence may be limited. (8)

## When combination therapy is usually unnecessary

Routine combination therapy is **not supported for most gram-negative bacteremia** once an active definitive agent is known. Studies summarized in the references found no consistent mortality benefit, while combination therapy—particularly beta-lactam plus aminoglycoside—was associated with greater toxicity, including nephrotoxicity. (3) (9)

For *Acinetobacter*, mild infections are generally treated with **monotherapy**, whereas the cited recommendations favor initial combination therapy for moderate-to-severe infection, followed by step-down to one active agent after clinical improvement. (2)

## Practical approach

1. **Start empiric combination therapy** only when the patient is critically ill or has a high risk of MDR infection and inappropriate initial therapy would be particularly dangerous.  
2. Obtain cultures and susceptibility testing promptly.  
3. Use prior patient isolates and local resistance data to select agents. (4)  
4. Once susceptibility results are available and the patient improves, **de-escalate to a single active agent whenever appropriate**. (2)  
5. Avoid combinations with predictable additive toxicity—for example, polymyxin plus aminoglycoside when alternatives exist—and seek expert advice for pan-resistant or highly unusual resistance patterns. (6)
