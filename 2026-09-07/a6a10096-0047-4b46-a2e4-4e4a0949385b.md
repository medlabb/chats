## Major causes of unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia in term newborns

- **Physiologic bilirubinemia:** increased bilirubin production/load after birth, immature hepatic **UGT1A1-mediated conjugation**, and reduced hepatic uptake and excretion. Enteric reabsorption also contributes. (1) (2)
- **Hemolysis or increased bilirubin production:**
  - **Isoimmune hemolytic disease:** Rh, ABO, or other blood-group incompatibility
  - **G6PD deficiency** and other erythrocyte enzyme defects, such as pyruvate kinase deficiency
  - **RBC membrane disorders:** hereditary spherocytosis, elliptocytosis, and infantile pyknocytosis
  - **Infection**, including bacterial or viral infection
  - **Sequestered blood** from cephalohematoma, subdural or subgaleal hematoma, bruising, or large hemangiomas
  - **Polycythemia** (listed among causes associated with increased erythrocyte destruction). (3) (4)
- **Sepsis-associated hyperbilirubinemia:** may result from hemolysis and/or impaired bilirubin conjugation and excretion. (4)

## Distinguishing physiologic from pathologic jaundice

**Physiologic jaundice** in a term infant generally follows this pattern:

- Bilirubin rises progressively from approximately **2 mg/dL in cord blood**.
- It peaks at about **48–120 hours**—most often at 72–96 hours in Caucasian and African American infants—and then declines.
- In Caucasian and African American infants, it usually falls to approximately **3 mg/dL by day 5** and approaches the adult range by the end of the second week. Asian-American infants may have a later and higher peak. (1)
- The natural course is illustrated in **Fig. 95.11**; the global transcutaneous bilirubin trajectory is shown in **Fig. 95.10**. (1) (5)

Features suggesting **pathologic jaundice** include:

- Jaundice appearing during the **first 24 hours**
- Bilirubin that is excessive for the infant’s age
- A rapid rise of **≥0.5 mg/dL/hour over 4–8 hours** or **≥5 mg/dL/day**
- Total bilirubin of **13–15 mg/dL or higher at any time in a term infant**
- Jaundice persisting beyond **10–14 days**
- Clinical concern for hemolysis, infection, anemia, or a hematoma. (6)

When these features are present, evaluation may include total and direct bilirubin, blood type and **DAT/Coombs test**, CBC and smear, and—when indicated—reticulocyte count, G6PD testing, and assessment for sepsis. These recommendations are summarized in **Table 95.3**. (6)

Conjugated and unconjugated fractions should be measured separately; a significant direct-reacting fraction indicates a different, often mixed or predominantly conjugated, process rather than isolated physiologic unconjugated jaundice. (5)
