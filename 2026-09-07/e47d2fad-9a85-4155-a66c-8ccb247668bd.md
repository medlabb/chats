## Major causes of unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia in term newborns

- **Physiologic bilirubinemia:** Increased bilirubin production combined with immature hepatic uptake and conjugation—particularly low UGT1A1 activity—during the first days of life. (rf_82)
- **Hemolysis and increased erythrocyte destruction:**
  - Isoimmune hemolytic disease: **Rh, ABO, or other blood-group incompatibility**
  - **G6PD deficiency**
  - Red-cell enzyme defects, such as pyruvate kinase or hexokinase deficiency
  - Membrane disorders, especially **hereditary spherocytosis**, as well as elliptocytosis and infantile pyknocytosis (1) (2)
- **Infection, especially sepsis:** May cause hemolysis and/or impair bilirubin conjugation and excretion. (2)
- **Sequestered blood from birth trauma:** Cephalohematoma, subdural or subgaleal hematoma, bruising, and occasionally large hemangiomas. (1) (2)
- **Other mechanisms:** Disorders affecting bilirubin uptake, conjugation, excretion, or reabsorption can exaggerate or prolong the normal neonatal pattern. (3)

## Distinguishing physiologic from pathologic jaundice

**Physiologic jaundice** in a term newborn typically:

- Develops during the expected early neonatal period, with bilirubin rising from cord levels and peaking around **48–120 hours**—most often at **72–96 hours** in Caucasian and African American infants. (rf_82)
- Peaks at a mean of approximately **5–6 mg/dL** in Caucasian and African American infants and **10–14 mg/dL** in Asian-American infants. (rf_82)
- Then declines, reaching about **3 mg/dL by day 5** in Caucasian and African American infants; minimal residual jaundice should gradually resolve by approximately **10–14 days**. (rf_82)

Features suggesting **pathologic jaundice** include:

- Jaundice present in the **first 24 hours**
- Jaundice that appears excessive for the infant’s age
- A rapid bilirubin increase of **≥0.5 mg/dL/hour over 4–8 hours** or **≥5 mg/dL/day**
- Total bilirubin **≥13–15 mg/dL** at any time in a full-term infant
- Persistence beyond **10–14 days**
- A rapid rise, need for phototherapy, or bilirubin approaching exchange-transfusion levels without an adequate explanation (4)

When these features are present, evaluation may include bilirubin fractionation, blood type and DAT/Coombs testing, CBC and smear, and—when indicated—reticulocyte count, G6PD testing, and sepsis assessment, as summarized in **Table 95.3**.
