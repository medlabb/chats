## Major causes of unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia in term newborns

- **Physiologic bilirubinemia:** Increased bilirubin production, enterohepatic reabsorption, and immature hepatic uptake/conjugation—particularly low UGT1A1 activity during the first days after birth—contribute to the normal neonatal rise. (1) (2)
- **Hemolysis and increased erythrocyte destruction:**
  - **Isoimmune hemolytic disease:** Rh, ABO, or other blood-group incompatibility
  - **G6PD deficiency**
  - Other erythrocyte defects, including pyruvate kinase or hexokinase deficiency, hereditary spherocytosis, hereditary elliptocytosis, and infantile pyknocytosis (3) (4)
- **Sequestration of blood:** Cephalohematoma, subdural or subgaleal hematoma, bruising/ecchymoses, and occasionally large hemangiomas. (3) (4)
- **Infection, especially sepsis:** May cause hemolysis or impair bilirubin conjugation and excretion. (4)
- **Population/genetic or environmental susceptibility:** Some infants may have delayed maturation of bilirubin metabolism; in G6PD deficiency, exposure to certain chemicals or drugs may precipitate severe hemolysis. (5)

## Distinguishing physiologic from pathologic jaundice

**Typical physiologic jaundice in a term infant:**

- Begins after the first day of life.
- Bilirubin rises progressively and generally peaks at approximately **48–120 hours**; most Caucasian and African American infants peak at 72–96 hours.
- In Caucasian and African American infants, the mean peak is about **5–6 mg/dL**; in Asian-American infants, it may be **10–14 mg/dL**.
- It then declines, reaching approximately **3 mg/dL by day 5** in Caucasian and African American infants, with gradual return toward the adult value by the end of the second week. (1)

**Features suggesting pathologic jaundice and requiring further evaluation:**

- Jaundice present during the **first 24 hours**.
- Jaundice that appears excessive for the infant’s age.
- A rapid bilirubin rise of **≥0.5 mg/dL/hour over 4–8 hours** or **≥5 mg/dL/day**.
- Total bilirubin **≥13–15 mg/dL at any time in a term infant**.
- Clinical jaundice persisting beyond **10–14 days**.
- Need for phototherapy, bilirubin rising across risk percentiles, or bilirubin approaching exchange-transfusion levels. (6) (7)

Evaluation may include total and direct bilirubin, blood type and DAT/Coombs testing, complete blood count and smear, and—when indicated—reticulocyte count and G6PD testing. (7)
