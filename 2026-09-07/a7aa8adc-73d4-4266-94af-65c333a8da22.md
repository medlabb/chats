## Major causes of unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia in term newborns

- **Physiologic immaturity:** Increased bilirubin production, low hepatic UGT1A1 activity, and reduced hepatic uptake/excretion contribute to physiologic jaundice. (1)
- **Hemolysis and increased erythrocyte destruction:**
  - Immune-mediated disease: **Rh, ABO, and other blood-group incompatibilities**
  - Enzymatic defects: **G6PD deficiency**, pyruvate kinase deficiency, and hexokinase deficiency
  - RBC membrane disorders: **hereditary spherocytosis**, elliptocytosis, and infantile pyknocytosis
  - **Infection**, particularly bacterial sepsis
  - **Sequestered blood** from cephalohematoma, subdural or subgaleal hematoma, bruising, ecchymoses, or large hemangiomas (2) (3)
- **Breastfeeding-associated jaundice:** Poor intake, relative dehydration/starvation, delayed meconium passage, and increased enterohepatic reuptake cause breastfeeding-failure jaundice. Breast milk jaundice is a later-onset form related to milk composition and may persist for weeks. (4)
- **Inherited impairment of bilirubin metabolism:** Genetic variation affecting **UGT1A1** may contribute to significant or prolonged jaundice; Crigler–Najjar syndrome can cause severe unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia. (4) (5)

## Distinguishing physiologic from pathologic jaundice

**Physiologic jaundice** in a term infant generally:

- Begins after the first day of life.
- Peaks at approximately **48–120 hours**; in many Caucasian and African American infants, the mean peak is **5–6 mg/dL**, while peaks of **10–14 mg/dL** are described in Asian-American infants.
- Declines thereafter, reaching about **3 mg/dL by day 5** in Caucasian and African American infants and by days **7–10** in Asian-American infants, with near-adult values by the end of the second week. (1)
- Is predominantly **unconjugated**, without evidence of hemolysis or systemic illness.

**Features suggesting pathologic jaundice** include:

- Appearance during the **first 24 hours** of life.
- A rapid bilirubin rise of **≥0.5 mg/dL/hour** over 4–8 hours or **≥5 mg/dL/day**.
- Total bilirubin **≥13–15 mg/dL at any time in a term infant**, or a level approaching exchange-transfusion thresholds.
- Persistence of jaundice beyond **10–14 days**; jaundice at or beyond 3 weeks requires total and direct bilirubin assessment.
- Evidence of hemolysis, such as a positive **DAT/Coombs test**, anemia or reticulocytosis, spherocytes, or G6PD deficiency.
- An elevated direct/conjugated bilirubin fraction, which indicates a different pathologic process rather than typical unconjugated physiologic jaundice. (6) (7)
