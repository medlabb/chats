## Major causes of unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia

- **Physiologic jaundice:** Results primarily from the newborn’s increased bilirubin load and immature hepatic conjugation, with reduced bilirubin uptake also contributing. UGT1A1 activity is very low in fetal life and rises progressively after birth. (1) (2)
- **Hemolysis and increased bilirubin production:**
  - Isoimmune hemolytic disease—**Rh, ABO, or other blood-group incompatibility**
  - Enzymatic defects—especially **G6PD deficiency**, as well as pyruvate kinase and hexokinase deficiency
  - RBC membrane disorders—particularly **hereditary spherocytosis**, and less commonly elliptocytosis or infantile pyknocytosis
  - Infection-associated hemolysis, including bacterial infection or sepsis (3) (4)
- **Sequestered blood from birth trauma:** Cephalohematoma, subdural or subgaleal hematoma, bruising/ecchymoses, and occasionally large hemangiomas can increase bilirubin production as trapped RBCs are broken down. (3) (4)
- **Other contributors:** Genetic or geographic predisposition to slower maturation of bilirubin metabolism, and—in G6PD-deficient infants—exposure to oxidant substances or medications may precipitate severe hemolysis. (5)

## Distinguishing physiologic from pathologic jaundice

**Typical physiologic jaundice in a term infant:**

- Does **not appear during the first 24 hours**.
- Rises gradually, generally peaking at **48–120 hours**; in the cited data, the mean peak is approximately **5–6 mg/dL** in Caucasian and African American infants and **10–14 mg/dL** in Asian-American infants.
- Subsequently declines, reaching about **3 mg/dL by day 5** in Caucasian and African American infants, and by approximately **days 7–10** in Asian-American infants; it then slowly approaches the adult level. (1)

**Features suggesting pathologic jaundice or requiring evaluation:**

- Jaundice in the **first 24 hours**
- Jaundice that appears excessive for the infant’s age
- A rapid bilirubin rise of **≥0.5 mg/dL/hour over 4–8 hours** or **≥5 mg/dL/day**
- Total bilirubin **≥13–15 mg/dL at any time in a term infant**
- Persistence of clinical jaundice beyond **10–14 days**
- A bilirubin level of **≥4 mg/dL in cord blood** (6)

When these features are present, evaluation may include total and direct bilirubin, blood type and DAT/Coombs testing, CBC and smear, and—when indicated—reticulocyte count, G6PD testing, and assessment for infection.
