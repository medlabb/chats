 # Pediatric autoimmune hepatitis (AIH)

## What it is

**Autoimmune hepatitis** is an immune-mediated liver disease characterized by some combination of:

- Elevated serum aminotransferases  
- Liver-associated autoantibodies  
- Hypergammaglobulinemia, including elevated IgG  
- Liver inflammation, often affecting hepatocytes and sometimes bile ducts (1; 2)

The disease can range from isolated biochemical abnormalities to chronic liver disease, cirrhosis, or hepatic failure. Chronicity may be suggested by disease lasting more than 3–6 months, low albumin, thrombocytopenia, clubbing, spider telangiectasias, splenomegaly, or ascites. (1)

## Main types

- **Type 1 AIH:** antinuclear antibody (**ANA**) and/or anti–smooth muscle antibody (**SMA**) positive.
- **Type 2 AIH:** anti–liver-kidney microsomal type 1 antibody (**anti-LKM-1**) positive. Cytochrome P450 2D6 is the main autoantigen in type 2 disease. (1; 3)

AIH can occasionally be **autoantibody-negative**, so negative antibodies do not exclude the diagnosis. (2)

## Why it occurs

AIH is thought to develop in a genetically susceptible child after an unknown trigger initiates a T-cell–mediated immune response against liver autoantigens. Possible triggers include infections, molecular mimicry, medications, and environmental toxins. Susceptibility has been associated particularly with HLA-DR3, DR4, and DR7 variants. (3)

The inflammatory infiltrate includes T and B lymphocytes, macrophages, and plasma cells. Some children have pathogenic variants in **AIRE**, which can also be associated with autoimmune polyendocrinopathy-candidiasis-ectodermal dystrophy, in which AIH occurs in approximately 20% of patients. (3)

## How children may present

Children may be:

- Asymptomatic, with abnormal liver tests found incidentally
- Presenting with elevated aminotransferases
- Showing signs of chronic liver disease
- Presenting with hepatic failure or acute liver failure (2; 4)

Acute hepatic failure is attributed to AIH in approximately 5–28% of cases. Autoimmune markers may be positive, IgG may be elevated, and biopsy may show interface hepatitis with a plasma-cell infiltrate. (4)

## Diagnostic approach

Diagnosis combines:

1. **Clinical assessment**
2. **Biochemical testing**
3. **Autoantibody testing**
4. **Immunoglobulin testing**
5. **Liver biopsy**
6. **Exclusion of other liver diseases** (2)

Typical biochemical and immunologic findings include:

- Predominant elevation of aminotransferases rather than alkaline phosphatase or GGT
- Elevated γ-globulins or IgG
- ANA, SMA, or anti-LKM antibodies
- Histologic features compatible with AIH (2)

Children with elevated liver tests or signs of chronic liver disease should be evaluated for AIH, including those who are asymptomatic, in liver failure, or antibody-negative. (2)

### Conditions that must be excluded

Important alternatives include:

- Hepatitis A, B, C, and D
- Epstein–Barr virus infection
- Drug- or toxin-induced hepatitis
- Wilson disease
- Alpha-1-antitrypsin deficiency
- Nonalcoholic steatohepatitis
- Other metabolic, infectious, and chronic liver diseases (2)

Suggested tests described in the references include:

- Hepatitis A IgM
- Hepatitis B surface antigen
- Hepatitis C antibody
- Alpha-1-antitrypsin level and phenotype
- Serum ceruloplasmin (2)

## Liver biopsy

Liver biopsy is ultimately necessary to confirm compatible histology and to evaluate for small-duct primary sclerosing cholangitis. Characteristic findings include:

- Portal-tract fibrous expansion
- Portal lymphocytic inflammation rich in plasma cells
- Extensive **interface hepatitis** (2)

## AIH and bile-duct disease

Bile-duct involvement may occur in children, and overlap between hepatocyte-directed and bile-duct-directed disease may be more common in children than adults. Important related disorders include:

- Autoimmune cholangiopathy
- Primary sclerosing cholangitis
- AIH–PSC overlap syndrome (1)

**Magnetic resonance cholangiography** can screen for sclerosing cholangitis. AIH–PSC overlap is increasingly recognized and may have features of both AIH and PSC, including cholestasis, pruritus, bile-duct abnormalities, or abnormal cholangiography. (2; 5)

In children with inflammatory bowel disease, PSC–AIH overlap is estimated to occur in approximately 2% and generally has less favorable long-term outcomes than AIH alone. (5)

## Treatment

The goals are to suppress hepatic inflammation, achieve biochemical remission, and minimize treatment-related adverse effects. (6)

### Initial treatment

- **Prednisone:** 1–2 mg/kg every 24 hours, continued until aminotransferases normalize.
- **Azathioprine:** may be started with glucocorticoids or approximately 2 weeks later, depending on clinical practice.
- **Budesonide:** 9 mg daily may be used instead of prednisone in children without cirrhosis or severe acute AIH. (6)

Before or early during azathioprine therapy, **thiopurine methyltransferase activity** should be measured because low or absent activity increases the risk of severe myelotoxicity. (6)

### Tapering and monitoring

Once liver tests normalize and biochemical remission is achieved:

- Prednisone or budesonide is gradually reduced over several months.
- Liver tests should be monitored every 1–2 weeks during steroid tapering.
- After steroids are stopped, liver tests should be monitored every 3–4 months. (6)

### Treatment-resistant disease

Second-line options may include:

- Cyclosporine
- Tacrolimus
- Mycophenolate mofetil

These should be used by clinicians experienced with them because of their narrower therapeutic-to-toxic range. (6)

### Duration and withdrawal

If the child has had normal aminotransferases, negative autoantibodies, and normal IgG for at least 2 years after steroids have been withdrawn, a follow-up biopsy may be considered. If there is no ongoing inflammation, gradual withdrawal of azathioprine may be attempted. However, relapse is common, and many children require lifelong azathioprine. (6)

### AIH–PSC overlap

Children with AIH–PSC overlap may be treated with:

- Prednisone
- Azathioprine
- Ursodeoxycholic acid, 10 mg/kg per dose twice daily (6)

## Monitoring for treatment complications

Long-term follow-up should include assessment for:

- Linear growth
- Eye complications
- Bone density
- Blood pressure
- Biochemical disease activity (7)

Nonadherence is one of the most common explanations for apparent treatment resistance. Persistent or steroid-resistant disease should prompt reassessment for sclerosing cholangitis or Wilson disease. (7)

## Prognosis

- More than 75% of children initially achieve remission with treatment.
- Aminotransferases and bilirubin often improve within 1–3 months.
- Albumin and prothrombin-time abnormalities may take 3–9 months to improve.
- Only about 20% of children with type 1 AIH achieve long-term biochemical remission after treatment withdrawal.
- Long-term remission is rare in type 2 AIH.
- Relapses usually respond to retreatment. (7)

Cirrhosis can still develop despite an initially good response. Some children require continued low-dose prednisone or azathioprine to minimize ongoing biochemical activity. (7)

## Severe or fulminant disease

Corticosteroids may be useful in fulminant autoimmune disease but must be used cautiously because of increased susceptibility to systemic bacterial and fungal infections. Liver transplantation has been successful for end-stage or fulminant AIH. Disease recurs after transplantation in approximately 30% of patients, and recurrence generally responds to increased immunosuppression, particularly addition of azathioprine. (7)

## Associated conditions

AIH may occur with other autoimmune diseases, including:

- Celiac disease
- Systemic lupus erythematosus
- Inflammatory bowel disease
- Primary sclerosing cholangitis (2; 8; 5)

Children with persistent unexplained aminotransferase elevation should be evaluated for celiac disease. When autoimmune liver disease coexists with celiac disease, treatment includes both a gluten-free diet and immunosuppressive therapy. (8)

## Key practical summary

AIH should be considered in a child with unexplained aminotransferase elevation, chronic liver disease, or liver failure—even when autoantibodies are absent. Diagnosis requires exclusion of infections, drug injury, Wilson disease, alpha-1-antitrypsin deficiency, and other causes, with liver biopsy generally needed for confirmation. Standard therapy is prednisone with or without azathioprine, followed by prolonged biochemical monitoring because relapse is frequent and lifelong treatment may be necessary. (2; 6; 7)
