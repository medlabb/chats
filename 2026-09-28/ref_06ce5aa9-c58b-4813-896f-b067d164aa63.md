### TOLERABILITY

Both the therapeutic and adverse event profiles of SSRIs are the result of their blockade of the 5-HT transporter function. They eventually raise 5-HT levels in the CNS, as described above, but not necessarily to the same extent throughout the body, with a rapid action being particularly evident in the gastrointestinal (GI) tract. This is because 90% of the 5-HT in the body is in the periphery, where the degree of regulation is not as tightly controlled by autoreceptors. #### Gastrointestinal Effects

Nausea occurs in at least 25% of patients and can range from mild discomfort to, in some patients, vomiting. It is the most common early side effect of SSRIs and most often diminishes during the first to 2 weeks of treatment. Nausea at the initiation of treatment is generally dose-related. Accordingly, typical starting doses that are used to reduce this side effect are half of the usual starting dose. After 1 to 2 weeks at the lower doses, or when GI side effects resolve, the dose can be optimized. The disappearance of the GI side effects may be due to a desensitization of 5-HT<sub>3</sub> receptors. This is supported by the observations that GI side effects can be blocked by 5-HT<sub>3</sub> antagonists such as ondansetron (Zofran) and mirtazapine (Remeron).

#### Sexual Dysfunction

Sexual dysfunction, such as delayed ejaculation, delayed orgasm or anorgasmia, and diminished libido, is a common side effect of 5-HT reuptake inhibition. SSRI-associated sexual dysfunction is the most frequent side-effect–associated cause of premature medication discontinuation. Package insert data, almost always derived from phase III clinical trials, have underestimated the true incidence of sexual dysfunction, which occurs in clinical practice in about half of patients. Reliable estimates of the incidence and severity of untoward experiences involving sexual desire, performance, and satisfaction are difficult to obtain in part because patients and physicians may be reluctant to discuss them. Accordingly, estimates of the incidence of untoward sexual experience and performance cited in product labeling are underestimated in their actual incidence, as revealed by questionnaires on sexual function used in subsequent studies.

### SELECTIVE SEROTONIN REUPTAKE INHIBITORS AND SUICIDALITY

In the early 1990s, a debate arose as to whether fluoxetine treatment was associated with an increase in suicidal ideation and suicide attempts in some patients. A comparison of the emergence of suicidal ideation in patients receiving fluoxetine, tricyclic derivatives, or placebo suggested that fluoxetine-treated patients had less emergent suicidal ideation than those treated with the older drugs. Both groups on medication showed less emergent suicidal ideation than the group treated with a placebo. At times, fluoxetine produces symptoms, often described as restlessness, that are similar to akathisia in some patients. In the spring of 2004, the FDA asked manufacturers of the SSRIs, along with those of the other newer-generation medications for depression, to include a black box warning in their labeling recommending close observation of both adult and pediatric patients for signs or symptoms of worsening depression, including suicidal ideation, early on in therapy with these medications or on titration of dosing. This followed earlier recommendations concerning the potential of SSRIs to be associated with a higher rate of agitation, suicidal thoughts, or both in clinical trials when these rates were compared to treatment with placebo in studies of children and adolescents with MDD. Many clinicians believe that the recommendations to observe patients for worsening symptoms during the initiation of therapy represent a recommendation for sound clinical care. The issue regarding the treatment of children and adolescents remains somewhat unclear. One distinct possibility is that the 2% rate of agitation that was generally observed in studies that have been made public may represent pediatric patients who would ultimately go on to develop bipolar disorder.

A problem with the FDA advisory is that it was based on short-term trials and did not include suicidal patients. A study—the Adolescent Depression and Psychotherapy Trial (ADAPT)—actively included suicidal patients and found that the propensity for depressive relapse and self-reported suicidality in adolescents was reduced with SSRI administration. Furthermore, this study found that the reduction in clinical symptoms with SSRIs appeared to be a long-term effect. The authors of that study concluded that SSRIs are not detrimental to adolescent brain development and that therapeutic benefits outweigh the potential developmental risks.

A meta-analysis assessed the efficacy and suicidality risk of SSRIs, nefazodone, venlafaxine, and mirtazapine in a pediatric population. The authors found that these medications were effective for MDD, OCD, and anxiety disorders, as showed by an NNT of 10 (95% CI = 7–15), 6 (95% CI = 4–8), and 3 (95% CI = 2–5), respectively. Regarding the risk of reported suicidal ideation or attempt, the overall NNH was 143 (95% CI = 77–1,000). No completed suicide was identified.

#### Diabetes

Population-based studies have shown that SSRIs may increase the risk of diabetes. An analysis was done based on three large well-known US datasets: the Nurses’ Health Studies I and II and the Health Professionals Follow-up Study, a total of 139,000 women and 30,000 men. During over 1.6 million person-years of follow-up, the analysis documented a total of 6,641 new cases of type 2 diabetes and found that self-reported use of medications for depression was associated with an approximately 10% to 30% excess risk of developing diabetes. This association was robust despite adjustment for age, ethnicity, marital and living status, smoking habits, alcohol intake, physical activity, family history of diabetes, diet, body mass index, and major comorbidities (e.g., hypertension and dyslipidemia) and their treatment. There needs to be additional evidence that this increased risk is due to associated factors or SSRI treatment, but these findings suggest that it is prudent to monitor patients periodically for increased glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1C).

#### Sleep Disturbances and Daytime Somnolence

Up to one-fourth of persons taking SSRIs note trouble sleeping, excessive somnolence, or overwhelming fatigue. Bruxism is another possible side effect. Nocturnal motor activity, including nocturnal myoclonus, may be increased during SSRI treatment. Some persons taking SSRIs report recalling extremely vivid dreams or nightmares. They describe sleep as “busy.” Some persons benefit from taking their SSRI dose before going to bed, but others prefer to take it in the morning. SSRI-induced insomnia can be treated with Z drugs, trazodone, or other sedating medicines. Significant SSRI-induced somnolence often requires switching to another SSRI or bupropion.

#### Emotional Blunting

Emotional blunting or indifference are largely overlooked side effects associated with long-term SSRI use. Patients report an inability to cry in response to emotional situations, a feeling of apathy or indifference, or a restriction in the intensity of emotional experiences. This side effect is related to the inhibitory effect of 5-HT on the firing of dopamine and noradrenaline neurons. This side effect often leads to treatment discontinuation even when the drugs provide relief from depression or anxiety. The frequency of this side effect is unknown. It has a delayed onset, appears to be related to dosing, and may resolve with a dose reduction.

#### Extrapyramidal Symptoms

SSRIs rarely cause akathisia, dystonia, tremor, cogwheel rigidity, torticollis, opisthotonos, gait disorders, and bradykinesia. Rare cases of tardive dyskinesia have also been reported. Some people with well-controlled Parkinson disease may experience acute worsening of their motor symptoms when they take SSRIs, as 5-HT exerts an inhibitory effect on the firing of dopamine neurons.

#### Fractures

A recent meta-analysis found an increased risk of fracture among older adults treated with SSRIs (relative risk = 1.67, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.56–1.79). The authors reported an NNH of 19 for one additional fracture to occur over 5 years in SSRI-treated versus non-SSRI–treated individuals.

#### Laboratory Interferences

SSRIs do not interfere with any laboratory measures.

#### Bleeding

Both case reports and epidemiologic studies confirm that SSRIs alone may increase bleeding risk in some patients. There have been numerous case reports and case series describing abnormal bruising, bleeding, and heavy menses in patients taking SSRIs. Serotonin is released from platelets following vascular injury. It promotes vasoconstriction and a change in the shape of the platelets that lead to aggregation. Platelets cannot themselves synthesize 5-HT, so SSRIs deplete 5-HT in the platelets, which may decrease coagulation, theoretically leading to excessive bleeding diathesis in some patients. Other physiologic mechanisms may also be involved. There is an additive increase in bleeding risk when SSRIs are used concurrently with other drugs that can cause bleeding. SSRIs, for example, increase the incidence of serious bleeding in patients taking warfarin. Risk is also increased with concurrent use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, aspirin, or clopidogrel. A large general practice database search of data involving nearly 400,000 patients found that the number needed to harm (NNH) for upper GI bleeding with SSRI treatment in a low-risk population was 3,177, and in a high-risk population, it was 881. Thus, this risk associated with SSRI use is modest, requires monitoring and should not preclude their use in the presence of another predisposing factor.

#### Hyponatremia

There is an increased risk of hyponatremia in patients taking SSRIs. Risk factors include older age, female gender, volume depletion, and use of other medications or medical conditions causing hyponatremia like diuretics. SSRI-induced hyponatremia typically begins during the first weeks of treatment. This may be secondary to the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone.

#### Cardiovascular Effects

All SSRIs can potentially lengthen the QT interval in otherwise healthy people and cause drug-induced long QT syndrome, possibly leading to torsade de pointes when taken in overdoses. In 2011, the FDA issued a safety warning about high-dose citalopram treatment. It made the following recommendations regarding citalopram use:

- 20 mg/day is the maximum recommended dose for patients with hepatic impairment, who are older than 60 years of age, who are CYP2C19 poor metabolizers, or who are taking concomitant cimetidine.

- No longer prescribe at doses greater than 40 mg/day.

- Do not use it in patients with congenital long QT syndrome.

- Correct hypokalemia and hypomagnesemia before administering citalopram.

- Monitor electrolytes as clinically indicated.

- Consider more frequent EKGs in patients with chronic heart failure, bradyarrhythmias, or patients on concomitant medications that prolong the QT interval.

#### Pharmacodynamic Interactions

The most dreaded outcome of pharmacokinetic interactions involving SSRIs is the 5-HT syndrome or 5-HT toxicity. Serotonin syndrome is a life-threatening syndrome that can lead to autonomic instability, abdominal pain, diarrhea, confusion or delirium, increased motor activity, myoclonus, hyperpyrexia, cardiovascular shock, or death.

Serotonin-related problems occur on a continuum. For instance, mild elevation of the synaptic 5-HT at a therapeutic dose of an SSRI, is associated with common serotoninergic side effects such as GI discomfort or nausea. A real 5-HT syndrome is rare and occurs when dramatic levels of 5-HT are in the synapse. This syndrome is mostly mediated by the postsynaptic 5-HT<sub>2A</sub> and 5-HT<sub>1A</sub> receptors. 5-HT levels in the synapse increase significantly via three major mechanisms most often involved in 5-HT toxicity: 5-HT reuptake blockade, MAO inhibition, and increased 5-HT release from presynaptic neurons. A 5-HT syndrome is expected when a combination of these mechanisms occurs. For instance, 5-HT toxicity is expected when SSRIs are combined with MAOIs. Hence, this combination should never be prescribed. In the vast majority of cases, it is not expected that SSRIs alone in overdose would trigger a real 5-HT syndrome, as the MAO would prevent dramatic 5-HT increases. A review found that SSRI overdoses up to 30 times the typical daily dose resulted in minor or no symptoms. Combining two SSRIs is not an evidence-based strategy for treatment-resistant depression. However, such an approach would not lead to serotonin toxicity. Tryptophan (5-HT precursor) in combination with SSRIs also has the potential to produce serotonergic side effects. Again, real 5-HT toxicity is very rare when the MAO is not inhibited.

SSRIs must not be administered within 14 days of discontinuing treatment with an irreversible MAOI. When switching from an SSRI to an irreversible MAOI, clinicians must wait for five to seven half-lives after the SSRI has been stopped before introducing the MAOI.

Treatment of the 5-HT syndrome consists of removing the offending agents and promptly instituting comprehensive support care. Some benefits of pharmacologic interventions such as cyproheptadine and chlorpromazine have been suggested, but further research on this topic is needed.

A frequent drug combination in clinical practice is the use of a triptan to treat migraine along with an SSRI. Concurrent use of a triptan and an SSRI is normally uneventful, but significant reactions may occur. In response to multiple reports of serious reactions, in 2006, the FDA issued a Public Health Advisory warning of the possibility of 5-HT syndrome when triptans and SSRIs are taken together. According to the FDA advisory, physicians prescribing a triptan and SSRI should weigh the potential risk of 5-HT syndrome against the expected benefit of using a triptan with an SSRI. They should follow patients closely if a triptan and an SSRI are used together, particularly during treatment initiation, with dose increases, or with the addition of another serotonergic medication. It is important to remember that migraines on their own can produce severe physical and neurologic symptoms. Furthermore, most triptans do not cross the blood–brain barrier, and even if they did gain access to the CNS during a migraine attack (presumably due to increased permeability), they would decrease 5-HT release because of their agonistic effect at inhibitory 5-HT<sub>1D</sub> autoreceptors on 5-HT terminals. Therefore, patients who take a triptan and an SSRI together should be advised to seek medical attention only if they experience symptoms that they do not usually experience.
