### TOLERABILITY

Both the therapeutic and adverse event profiles of SSRIs are the result of their blockade of the 5-HT transporter function. They eventually raise 5-HT levels in the CNS, as described above, but not necessarily to the same extent throughout the body, with a rapid action being particularly evident in the gastrointestinal (GI) tract. This is because 90% of the 5-HT in the body is in the periphery, where the degree of regulation is not as tightly controlled by autoreceptors. As a group, the SSRIs possess the adverse effects listed in [Table 33.5a.1–2](#tt33-5a-1-2).

#### Gastrointestinal Effects

Nausea occurs in at least 25% of patients and can range from mild discomfort to, in some patients, vomiting. It is the most common early side effect of SSRIs and most often diminishes during the first to 2 weeks of treatment. Nausea at the initiation of treatment is generally dose-related. Accordingly, typical starting doses that are used to reduce this side effect are half of the usual starting dose. After 1 to 2 weeks at the lower doses, or when GI side effects resolve, the dose can be optimized. The disappearance of the GI side effects may be due to a desensitization of 5-HT<sub>3</sub> receptors. This is supported by the observations that GI side effects can be blocked by 5-HT<sub>3</sub> antagonists such as ondansetron (Zofran) and mirtazapine (Remeron).

#### Sexual Dysfunction

Sexual dysfunction, such as delayed ejaculation, delayed orgasm or anorgasmia, and diminished libido, is a common side effect of 5-HT reuptake inhibition. SSRI-associated sexual dysfunction is the most frequent side-effect–associated cause of premature medication discontinuation. Package insert data, almost always derived from phase III clinical trials, have underestimated the true incidence of sexual dysfunction, which occurs in clinical practice in about half of patients. Reliable estimates of the incidence and severity of untoward experiences involving sexual desire, performance, and satisfaction are difficult to obtain in part because patients and physicians may be reluctant to discuss them. Accordingly, estimates of the incidence of untoward sexual experience and performance cited in product labeling are underestimated in their actual incidence, as revealed by questionnaires on sexual function used in subsequent studies.

With a similar adverse effect profile to SSRIs, SNRIs are generally well tolerated; among them, venlafaxine extended-release is the most prescribed globally. Its common adverse effects include dry mouth, increased sweating, and constipation. Other notable side effects include headache, insomnia, diarrhea, and sexual dysfunction; the severity of most side effects increases with higher doses. At the upper end of the therapeutic range (e.g., venlafaxine 225 mg or higher), routine blood pressure monitoring is also warranted because some patients (fewer than 5%) experience dose-related hypertension. Venlafaxine at lower doses (i.e., <150 mg) is said to have mainly serotonergic effects. Thus, it should be prescribed at higher doses to obtain the dual reuptake inhibition. A newer SNRI, duloxetine, has a similar efficacy and tolerability profile to venlafaxine, including discontinuation syndrome, and is not recommended for individuals with liver dysfunction. More recently, desvenlafaxine, the major metabolite of venlafaxine, was released as a standalone compound. It has primarily been investigated as a treatment for major depression, although it is often used off-label for other conditions, including anxiety disorders (GAD, SAD, and PD in particular). It is thought to offer the advantage of dual uptake inhibition even at lower doses, unlike venlafaxine, where it occurs only in the upper dose range. To date, reports of tolerability relative to its parent compound are quite favorable.

Levomilnacipran is an antidepressant recently introduced into the North American market. Similar to other SNRIs, levomilnacipran is safe to use and generally well tolerated among patients who may report mild side effects such as nausea, sweating, and agitation following use. There is also a need to monitor an increase in blood pressure following initiation of use. The advantage of levomilnacipran is that the degree of norepinephrine reuptake inhibition is said to be proportionally the highest (compared to the serotonin effect) among all SNRIs. Published evidence of levomilnacipran’s use in pure anxiety conditions is lacking, but the literature suggests individuals with anxiety conditions comorbid with depression are known to benefit.

Both with SSRIs and SNRIs, abrupt stopping can lead to discontinuation syndrome. Commonly reported symptoms of this condition, which tend to occur 2 to 4 days after medication cessation, include increased anxiety, irritability, tearfulness, dizziness, lightheadedness, malaise, sleep disturbance, and concentration difficulties. Although discontinuation symptoms can occur with all SSRIs and SNRIs, they are more common with agents with shorter half-lives (e.g., paroxetine and venlafaxine). Patients should discontinue SSRIs or SNRIs only under the supervision of their physician, with gradual taper; if discontinuation symptoms emerge while tapering, the dose should be increased to stop symptoms, followed by a restart of the discontinuation at a slower rate. An alternate option is the use of fluoxetine as a transition agent because of its long plasma half-life.

#### Diabetes

Population-based studies have shown that SSRIs may increase the risk of diabetes. An analysis was done based on three large well-known US datasets: the Nurses’ Health Studies I and II and the Health Professionals Follow-up Study, a total of 139,000 women and 30,000 men. During over 1.6 million person-years of follow-up, the analysis documented a total of 6,641 new cases of type 2 diabetes and found that self-reported use of medications for depression was associated with an approximately 10% to 30% excess risk of developing diabetes. This association was robust despite adjustment for age, ethnicity, marital and living status, smoking habits, alcohol intake, physical activity, family history of diabetes, diet, body mass index, and major comorbidities (e.g., hypertension and dyslipidemia) and their treatment. There needs to be additional evidence that this increased risk is due to associated factors or SSRI treatment, but these findings suggest that it is prudent to monitor patients periodically for increased glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1C).

#### Sleep Disturbances and Daytime Somnolence

Up to one-fourth of persons taking SSRIs note trouble sleeping, excessive somnolence, or overwhelming fatigue. Bruxism is another possible side effect. Nocturnal motor activity, including nocturnal myoclonus, may be increased during SSRI treatment. Some persons taking SSRIs report recalling extremely vivid dreams or nightmares. They describe sleep as “busy.” Some persons benefit from taking their SSRI dose before going to bed, but others prefer to take it in the morning. SSRI-induced insomnia can be treated with Z drugs, trazodone, or other sedating medicines. Significant SSRI-induced somnolence often requires switching to another SSRI or bupropion.

#### Emotional Blunting

Emotional blunting or indifference are largely overlooked side effects associated with long-term SSRI use. Patients report an inability to cry in response to emotional situations, a feeling of apathy or indifference, or a restriction in the intensity of emotional experiences. This side effect is related to the inhibitory effect of 5-HT on the firing of dopamine and noradrenaline neurons. This side effect often leads to treatment discontinuation even when the drugs provide relief from depression or anxiety. The frequency of this side effect is unknown. It has a delayed onset, appears to be related to dosing, and may resolve with a dose reduction.

#### Extrapyramidal Symptoms

SSRIs rarely cause akathisia, dystonia, tremor, cogwheel rigidity, torticollis, opisthotonos, gait disorders, and bradykinesia. Rare cases of tardive dyskinesia have also been reported. Some people with well-controlled Parkinson disease may experience acute worsening of their motor symptoms when they take SSRIs, as 5-HT exerts an inhibitory effect on the firing of dopamine neurons.

In some but not most instances, sexual dysfunction appears to be a dose-related effect, and occasionally decreasing the dose might be helpful. There have been a number of other strategies suggested to deal with this important adverse effect, including drug holidays for shorter half-life SSRIs (potentially associated with discontinuation symptoms or even a relapse) or the addition of buspirone, amantadine, yohimbine, cyproheptadine, stimulants, or bupropion. Anecdotally, selective inhibitors of cGMP-specific phosphodiesterase type 5, such as sildenafil (Viagra), have been reported by some patients to be helpful. A randomized controlled trial that used sildenafil as a treatment of sexual dysfunction in women taking SSRIs found that its use was associated with a reduction in sexual side effects. These observations lack solid replication, and, in fact, none of these approaches have been successful in completely reversing sexual side effects. Therefore, gradual switching to a medication not associated with sexual dysfunctions is often the most successful strategy. These include bupropion, mirtazapine, low-dose vortioxetine, vilazodone, or moclobemide (after an SSRI washout).

#### Sweating

Excessive sweating is not uncommon while being treated with SSRIs. The sweating is unrelated to ambient temperature. Nocturnal sweating may rarely drench bed sheets and require a change of night clothes. Terazosin, 1 or 2 mg per day, is often effective in counteracting sweating.

#### Discontinuation Syndrome

5-HT reuptake inhibitors have the potential to produce a discontinuation syndrome if stopped abruptly. However, it is important to emphasize that individuals treated with SSRIs do not develop addictions to these drugs. These drugs do not cause cravings, and individuals do not feel the need to increase the dose to get the same effect. Longer durations of treatment appear to be associated with a higher probability of discontinuation syndrome than shorter treatment periods (i.e., intermittent treatment in PMDD). In general, good clinical practice dictates a gradual tapering down of a therapeutic regimen after successful treatment.

Typical symptoms include asthenia, anxiety, agitation, GI distress, myalgias, sometimes a flu-like syndrome but with the absence of a fever, disequilibrium, and, in extreme cases, an experience described as electrical shocks coursing through the arms and legs. When discontinuing the medication, it should be done over the course of weeks rather than days. Drug taper can be problematic when the last dose is stopped. This problem can be managed by prescribing fluoxetine 20 mg per day for 1 to 2 weeks and then stopping that medication. The longer half-lives of fluoxetine and norfluoxetine help counter the discontinuation effects to a significant degree.
