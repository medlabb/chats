## Answer

 ## Gastroenteritis management

Management depends on severity and suspected cause. **Acute viral gastroenteritis is usually self-limited** and is treated mainly with supportive care; specific antiviral therapy is unavailable. Antibiotics are generally not indicated for viral gastroenteritis. (1)

### 1. Rehydration
- **Oral rehydration therapy (ORT)** is first-line for mild-to-moderate dehydration, particularly in children. Replace ongoing fluid losses as well. (2)
- **Intravenous isotonic fluids** are required for severe dehydration, inability to tolerate oral fluids, or severe hypovolemia. (2) (3)
- Adults without volume depletion may maintain hydration with suitable fluids such as oral rehydration solutions, broths, or sports drinks; high-sugar soft drinks and fruit juices should be avoided. (4)
- ORT should not be used in patients with impaired mental status or ileus. (4)

### 2. Nutrition
- Resume an **age-appropriate, unrestricted diet as tolerated** once rehydration is complete; breastfeeding should continue. (2)
- Complex carbohydrates, lean meats, yogurt, fruits, and vegetables may be better tolerated than foods high in fat or simple sugars. Restrictive clear-liquid and BRAT diets are not recommended. (2)
- Adults should eat as tolerated; smaller meals and bland, low-residue foods may reduce vomiting. (4)

### 3. Symptom-directed treatment
- Routine antidiarrheal therapy is generally discouraged in acute viral gastroenteritis because benefit is limited and adverse effects are possible. (5)
- Avoid antimotility agents when fever, bloody stools, or mucoid stools suggest dysentery. (6)
- In children, routine antiemetics are not recommended when there is no dehydration. For children aged ≥6 months with mild-to-moderate dehydration and vomiting that prevents ORT, a single oral dose of ondansetron may facilitate rehydration; the cited regimen is **0.15 mg/kg, maximum 8 mg**. (5)
- Adults unable to tolerate oral rehydration because of excessive vomiting may receive an antiemetic such as ondansetron or prochlorperazine to facilitate oral hydration. (3)
- Probiotics and prebiotics have inconsistent evidence of benefit, and zinc is not recommended for children in resource-abundant countries; zinc is recommended for children older than six months with diarrhea in resource-limited settings where zinc deficiency is prevalent. (5) (7)

### 4. When to seek hospital care
Hospitalization may be needed for **dehydration or volume depletion, intractable vomiting, electrolyte or kidney abnormalities, excessive bloody stool or rectal bleeding, severe abdominal pain, symptoms lasting more than one week, significant comorbidities, pregnancy, or older age with hypovolemia**. (8)

For children discharged after medical care, minimum criteria include restored fluid status, ability to maintain hydration orally, arranged follow-up, and caregiver understanding of when to return for care. (5)
