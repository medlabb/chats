#### DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS

Putaminal, thalamic, and lobar hypertensive hemorrhages may be difficult to distinguish from infarction in the same locations. However, severe headache, nausea and vomiting, and impaired consciousness suggest hemorrhage. CT or MRI (**[Figure 13-20](#greenberg11_ch13fg20)**) provides a definitive diagnosis.

#### TREATMENT


##### Medical

1. **Initial management** of intracerebral hemorrhage includes airway support with ventilatory assistance if required.
2. **Hypertension** should be treated by reducing systolic blood pressure to 140-179 mm Hg with intravenous [nicardipine](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660570) and, if needed, intravenous [labetalol](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660418). This is as effective as more intensive reduction to 110-139 mm Hg in improving clinical outcome and is associated with fewer adverse hypotensive effects.
3. **Coagulopathy** should be reversed by clotting factor replacement with prothrombin complex concentrate or fresh frozen plasma and severe **thrombocytopenia** corrected by platelet transfusion. If coagulopathy is due to anticoagulation or thrombolytic or antiplatelet therapy, the agent responsible should be discontinued and, if necessary, its effect reversed (see [Table 13-4](#greenberg11_ch13tb4)). Platelet transfusion for patients on antiplatelet agents is indicated only if they are to undergo surgical evacuation of a hematoma.


4. **Hyperglycemia** and **hypoglycemia** should both be avoided and insulin or glucose administered as needed.

#### INTRACEREBRAL HEMORRHAGE


##### Etiology

The most common cause of nontraumatic intracerebral hemorrhage is chronic hypertension. This and other causes are discussed in more detail in [Chapter 13](/content.aspx?legacysectionid=greenberg11_ch13), Stroke.


##### Clinical Findings

Intracerebral hemorrhage usually occurs while the patient is awake. Hemorrhage is not preceded by transient prodromal symptoms, such as the transient ischemic attacks (TIAs) often associated with cerebral infarction.

Headache occurs in many cases and can be moderate to severe. If present, headache may be localized to the site of hemorrhage or generalized. Nausea and vomiting are common. Altered consciousness may progress steadily to stupor or coma over minutes to hours.

On examination, patients are nearly always hypertensive (blood pressure ≥170/90 mm Hg), even in the late stages of transtentorial herniation. The funduscopic examination usually shows vascular changes associated with chronic hypertension. Nuchal rigidity is common. Gaze deviation—toward the side of a putaminal or lobar hemorrhage or downward and medially in thalamic hemorrhage—may occur. Hemiparesis is frequent because of the proximity of common hemorrhage sites, such as the basal ganglia and thalamus, to the internal capsule, which conveys descending motor fibers from the cerebral cortex.

Seizures occur in ~10% of cases and are often focal. Neurologic deficits do not fluctuate spontaneously.


##### Investigative Studies

CT brain scan without contrast or MRI shows intraparenchymal and sometimes intraventricular blood and confirms the diagnosis (see [Chapter 13](/content.aspx?legacysectionid=greenberg11_ch13), Stroke).


##### Treatment

1. **Blood pressure**—Systolic blood pressure should be reduced to 140-179 mm Hg to limit hematoma expansion, but excessive blood pressure reduction should be avoided, as it may compromise blood flow in brain tissue adjacent to the hemorrhage.
2. **Cerebral edema**—The mass effect of intracerebral hemorrhage is typically compounded by progressive cerebral edema, which becomes evident by ~24 hours and maximal within 5-6 days following hemorrhage. transiently relieve headache. Cells, glucose, and protein content of the CSF are normal. However, obese individuals without idiopathic intracranial hypertension may have opening pressures as high as 250 mm H<sub>2</sub>O.



If a cause is identified, specific treatment should be given. Cerebral venous thrombosis must be excluded by imaging. The mainstay of treatment in idiopathic cases is [acetazolamide](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660265) (1,000-1,250 mg/d); [topiramate](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659464) and tricyclic antidepressants may be useful for headache. Glucocorticoids are effective, but have untoward side effects, and papilledema tends to rebound when they are discontinued. Obese patients should be encouraged to lose 5-10% of body weight. There is no increased risk of idiopathic intracranial hypertension with pregnancy.

In cases refractory to medical treatment, optic nerve sheath fenestration or lumboperitoneal or ventriculoperitoneal shunting may be needed to preserve vision and decrease headache. Venous stenting has been employed in some cases.


### TRIGEMINAL NEURALGIA

Trigeminal neuralgia is a pain syndrome occurring in the distribution of the trigeminal nerve, consisting of brief, recurrent jolts of unilateral electric shock-like episodes that begin and end suddenly. The pain may induce facial muscle spasm, thus the descriptive term “**tic douloureux**.” This syndrome develops after age 50 and is more common in women than men.

Pain is unilateral and typically confined to the area supplied by the second (V2) and third (V3) divisions of the trigeminal (V) nerve and can be intraoral, extraoral, or both (**[Figure 6-7](#greenberg11_ch6fg7)**). Involvement of the first trigeminal division or bilateral symptoms occur in <5% of cases. Pain occurs as recurrent, brief, excruciating, lightning-like jabs, which abate spontaneously; attacks are stereotypic in a given patient. There may be a continuous dull pain between paroxysms, and dull facial pain can be the presenting symptom. Occurrence during sleep is uncommon. Pain-free intervals may last for minutes to weeks, but long-term spontaneous remission is rare. Stimulation of trigger zones about the midline (cheek, nose, or mouth) by touch, cold, wind, talking, or chewing can precipitate the pain. In classical trigeminal neuralgia, physical examination discloses no abnormalities, and trigeminal sensory deficits or abnormal trigeminal (eg, corneal or jaw jerk) reflexes exclude an idiopathic diagnosis. Rarely, similar pain may occur in multiple sclerosis or with brainstem tumors, which should be considered in young patients and those with ophthalmic division pain, neurologic abnormalities on examination, bilateral symptoms, or pain in the tongue.
