 ## Iron deficiency anaemia: practical summary

**Diagnosis and cause**
- Iron deficiency is supported by serum ferritin **<12 ng/mL**, or **<30 ng/mL when anaemia is also present**. (1; 2)
- In adults, iron deficiency anaemia is commonly due to chronic blood loss, particularly menstrual or gastrointestinal bleeding. Other causes include blood donation, reduced absorption (for example, coeliac disease or *H. pylori* gastritis), and increased iron requirements such as pregnancy. Identifying the cause—especially possible occult blood loss—is an important part of management. (2; 3)

**Oral treatment**
- A standard approach is ferrous sulfate **325 mg once daily or every other day**, taken on an empty stomach. Food may reduce nausea or constipation but also reduces absorption. (3)
- A suitable response is a return of the haematocrit halfway toward normal within about **3 weeks**, with full return to baseline after about **2 months**. Continue iron for **3–6 months after blood values normalize** to replenish stores. (3)
- If response is inadequate, possible reasons in the reference include nonadherence, poor absorption, an incorrect diagnosis, coeliac disease, or ongoing blood loss. (3)

**Intravenous iron and referral**
- Intravenous iron may be considered when oral iron is not tolerated or does not work, when gastrointestinal disease prevents oral treatment, or when ongoing blood loss cannot be corrected. Most iron-deficient patients need **1–1.5 g** of parenteral iron. (4)
- Refer to a haematologist if the suspected diagnosis is not confirmed or the patient does not respond to oral iron. (5)
