## Answer

 ## Diagnosis

MS is diagnosed using the **2017 McDonald criteria**, which require evidence that lesions affect at least two different central nervous system regions (**dissemination in space**) and occurred at different times (**dissemination in time**), with no better explanation for the symptoms. MRI findings, clinical attacks, and—where applicable—unique oligoclonal bands in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) can help establish these criteria. Primary progressive MS requires at least one year of progression plus two of: a typical brain lesion, at least two spinal lesions, or CSF oligoclonal bands. (1)

Laboratory results alone cannot establish MS. CSF findings and evoked-potential tests may support the assessment, but other conditions—including infections, connective-tissue diseases, sarcoidosis, metabolic disorders, and lymphoma—may need to be excluded. (rf_6a)

A single event that does not meet MS criteria may be classified as **clinically isolated syndrome**; follow-up MRI in 6–12 months may be considered. MRI findings suggestive of MS without a typical clinical attack may be termed **radiologically isolated syndrome**. (1)

## Treatment

- **Acute relapses:** Corticosteroids, commonly high-dose methylprednisolone, may speed recovery but do not change the eventual extent of recovery. Plasmapheresis may be used for severe relapses that do not respond to corticosteroids. (2, 3)
- **Relapse prevention:** Disease-modifying medicines are used to reduce attacks. The choice depends on disease severity, risks, tolerability, and patient preference; some treatments require special monitoring, and specialist management is recommended. Table 27–10 lists treatment options. (2, 3)
- **Progressive disease:** Ocrelizumab is identified as the only medication effective in slowing disability progression in primary progressive MS. (3)
- **Symptoms:** Treatment may also be needed for problems such as spasticity, bladder symptoms, fatigue, or depression. (3)
