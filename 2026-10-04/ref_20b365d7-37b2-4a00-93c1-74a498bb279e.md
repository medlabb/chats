## TREATMENT

### A. Slowing Progression

Slowing CKD progression is a multipronged task that includes (1) treatment of the underlying cause, (2) blood pressure control, (3) therapy with antiproteinuric agents (ACE inhibitor or ARB, MRA, SGLT-2-inhibitor, or a combination of these) if appropriate, (4) weight loss if a patient has obesity, (5) avoidance of possible nephrotoxins (iodinated contrast agents, NSAIDs, PPIs), and (6) management of cardiovascular risk factors. Treatment of metabolic acidosis is recommended. GLP-1 agonists have emerged as a promising class of medications to help slow disease progression in patients with diabetes. Laboratory results should be monitored after starting or titrating antiproteinuric agents. An initial fall in eGFR within the first several weeks may be seen, but as long as further testing demonstrates stabilization of kidney function and there is no concerning hyperkalemia, the agent should be continued.

### B. Dietary Management

Patients with CKD should be evaluated by a renal dietitian. Patient-specific recommendations should be made concerning protein, salt and water, potassium, and phosphorus intake to help mitigate CKD progression and complications.

#### 1. Protein restriction

Limiting protein intake to 0.6–0.8 g/kg/day may slow CKD progression; plant-based sources of protein are thought to be better than animal sources for long-term kidney health. Restricting beyond these amounts is not advisable and should be avoided in those with cachexia or low serum albumin.

#### 2. Salt and water restriction

Efforts should prioritize [sodium](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659912) restriction over water restriction; patients should be encouraged to drink water to thirst so long as they are following guidance on [sodium](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659912) intake appropriately. In advanced CKD, the kidney is unable to adapt to large changes in [sodium](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659912) intake. Intake of > 3–4 g/day can lead to hypertension and hypervolemia, whereas intake of < 1 g day can lead to volume depletion and hypotension. A goal of 2 g/day of [sodium](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659912) is reasonable for most patients.

#### 3. Potassium restriction

Restriction is needed once the GFR falls below 10–20 mL/min/1.73 m<sup>2</sup>, or earlier if the patient is hyperkalemic. Patients should receive detailed lists describing potassium content of foods and should limit their intake to < 50–60 mEq/day (2 g/day). Constipation can contribute to hyperkalemia in CKD, since a higher percentage of potassium is excreted through the GI tract as GFR declines.

#### 4. Phosphorus restriction

Guidelines suggest lowering elevated serum phosphorus levels toward normal in advanced CKD and to a level of < 5.5 mg/dL in ESKD. Initial dietary [phosphate](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659912) restriction should include avoidance of highly bioavailable phosphorus (fast foods and highly processed foods, and cola beverages), and subsequently moderation of intake of other phosphorus-rich foods such as eggs, dairy products, nuts, beans, and meat. Care must be taken to avoid protein malnutrition. When ESKD is reached, dietary restriction alone rarely is sufficient to reach target levels, and phosphorus binders are often required (see section on [Mineral Bone Disorders](#papacmdt26_ch25lev3sec20)).

### B. Mineral Bone Disorders

Mineral bone disorders of CKD refer to the complex disturbances of [calcium](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659912) and phosphorus metabolism, parathyroid hormone (PTH), active vitamin D ([calcitriol](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660040)), and fibroblast growth factor-23 (FGF-23) homeostasis (see [Part 30](/content.aspx?legacysectionid=papacmdt26_ch30) and [Figure 25–3](#papacmdt26_ch25fg3)). A typical pattern seen in advanced CKD is relative hyperphosphatemia and hypocalcemia with low active vitamin D, resulting in secondary hyperparathyroidism. These abnormalities contribute to bone disease and excess fracture risk, as well to vascular calcification that is partly responsible for the accelerated CVD and excess mortality seen in CKD. Epidemiologic studies associate elevated phosphorus levels with increased risk of cardiovascular mortality in early CKD through ESKD. Data regarding how best to manage mineral bone disorders in these patients are limited; control of mineral and PTH levels per guidelines is discussed below.

Bone disease, or renal osteodystrophy, in advanced CKD is common and manifests in a variety of lesions ([eFigure 25–3](/content.aspx?legacysectionid=papacmdt26_ch25efg3)). The gold standard for diagnosing renal osteodystrophy is bone biopsy, but this is not widely available. The most common bone disease, osteitis fibrosa cystica, is a result of secondary hyperparathyroidism and the osteoclast-stimulating effects of PTH. This is a high-turnover disease with bone resorption and subperiosteal lesions, which can cause bone pain and proximal muscle weakness. Adynamic bone disease, or low bone turnover, is becoming more common and may occur iatrogenically from suppression of PTH or via spontaneously low PTH production. Osteomalacia is characterized by lack of bone mineralization. Although historically associated with aluminum toxicity, osteomalacia is now most commonly due to vitamin D deficiency. Bisphosphonate administration in advanced CKD also may result in osteomalacia.

Management involves correction of [calcium](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659912), phosphorus, and 25-OH vitamin D levels toward normal values, and mitigation of hyperparathyroidism. Understanding the interplay between these abnormalities can help target therapy ([Figure 25–3](#papacmdt26_ch25fg3)). Declining GFR leads to phosphorus retention. Loss of renal mass and low 25-OH vitamin D levels often seen in CKD patients result in low 1,25(OH) vitamin D production by the kidney. Hypocalcemia can result from poor gut [calcium](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659912) absorption due to hypovitaminosis D as well as excess [phosphate](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659912), which complexes with [calcium](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659912) and leads to vascular, valvular and soft tissue calcification. Hyperphosphatemia, hypocalcemia, and hypovitaminosis D are all stimuli for PTH production, and, over time, parathyroid gland hyperplasia.

### C. Hematologic Complications

#### 1. Anemia

Anemia of CKD is primarily due to decreased erythropoietin production. It often becomes clinically significant at stage 3b–4 disease. The approach to a patient with CKD and anemia begins with ensuring that the bone marrow can respond to erythropoietin. Thus, [thyroid](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660112) function tests, serum vitamin B<sub>12</sub> levels, reticulocyte count, and iron stores (ferritin and iron saturation) should be checked in the initial evaluation. CKD is also associated with high levels of hepcidin, which blocks GI iron absorption and mobilization of iron from body stores; this results in a functional iron deficiency or “anemia of chronic disease.” In patients with CKD and anemia, KDIGO guidelines recommend offering iron supplementation if serum ferritin is below 500 ng/mL and iron saturation is < 30%. Iron therapy usually is withheld if the serum ferritin is > 700 ng/mL, even if iron saturation is < 20%. Oral therapy with ferrous [sulfate](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659912), [gluconate](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659912), or fumarate, 325 mg once every other day, is typically prescribed in pre-dialysis CKD; higher doses may result in increasing hepcidin levels. For those who do not respond due to poor GI absorption or intolerance, intravenous iron (eg, iron sucrose or iron [gluconate](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659912)) can be considered.

Erythropoiesis-stimulating agents (ESAs, eg, recombinant erythropoietin [epoetin alfa or beta] and darbepoetin) are used to treat anemia of CKD if other treatable causes are excluded. Initiation of an ESA is usually considered when hemoglobin (Hgb) values are 9–10 g/dL. Typical ESA starting doses are epoetin alfa 50–100 U/kg once or twice weekly; darbepoetin 0.45 mcg/kg every 2–4 weeks; or epoetin beta 60–100 mcg every 2–4 weeks. These agents can be given intravenously (eg, during hemodialysis) or subcutaneously; subcutaneous dosing of epoetin alfa is roughly 30% more effective than intravenous dosing. ESAs should be titrated to an Hgb of 10–11 g/dL for optimal safety; studies show that targeting a higher Hgb increases the risk of stroke and possibly other cardiovascular events. When titrating doses, Hgb levels should rise no more than 1 g/dL every 3–4 weeks. Other concerns regarding ESA use include possible exacerbation of HTN and acceleration of underlying malignancy growth. For this reason, [daprodustat](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659637), a hypoxia-inducible factor prolyl hydroxylase inhibitor, has emerged as an alternative to ESAs in treating anemia; however, it is not widely available to date.

#### 2. Coagulopathy

Bleeding diathesis may occur in stage 4–5 CKD due to platelet dysfunction or severe anemia.

Treatment of bleeding diathesis is required only in patients who are symptomatic. Raising the Hgb to 9–10 g/dL can reduce risk of bleeding via improved clot formation. [Desmopressin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660320) (25 mcg intravenously every 8–12 hours for two doses) is a short-lived but effective treatment for platelet dysfunction and can be used in preparation for surgery or kidney biopsy; hyponatremia is a potential adverse effect of this treatment. Dialysis improves platelet function in uremic patients.
