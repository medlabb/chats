## Answer

 **Treatment depends on the type of UTI:**

### Uncomplicated cystitis in women
Short-course antibiotics are recommended. First-line options include (Table 26–1):

- **Trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole 160/800 mg every 12 hours for 3 days**
- **Nitrofurantoin 100 mg every 12 hours for 5 days**
- **Fosfomycin 3 g orally as a single dose**

Alternative agents include cephalexin, amoxicillin/clavulanate, or cefpodoxime. Local resistance patterns should guide antibiotic selection. Fluoroquinolones are second-line and should be restricted for uncomplicated infections. (1)

### Pyelonephritis
Hospitalized patients may receive intravenous ampicillin plus gentamicin, ceftriaxone, ciprofloxacin, or cefepime/enmetazobactam, with treatment lasting **7–14 days** depending on the regimen. Non-hospitalized patients may receive an initial intravenous dose—ceftriaxone, ciprofloxacin, or gentamicin—followed by an oral regimen. If local resistance to the oral antibiotic exceeds 10%, an initial intravenous antibiotic dose is recommended. (1)

### Symptom relief
**Phenazopyridine** may relieve urinary discomfort when used with antibiotic therapy, but it should be used for **no more than 2 days** and can turn urine orange-red. (2)

### Recurrent UTIs
Preventive measures include adequate fluid intake, frequent complete bladder emptying, urinating before and after intercourse, and—in appropriate postmenopausal women—vaginal estrogen. A single postcoital antibiotic dose may help some women. Prophylactic antibiotics are generally discouraged unless a urologic evaluation has excluded anatomic abnormalities. (3)

### Important distinctions
- UTIs in men are uncommon and may require evaluation for prostatitis, stones, or urinary retention. (4)
- **Asymptomatic bacteriuria** generally should not be treated with antibiotics, except in pregnancy or before certain urologic procedures. (5)
- Failure to respond promptly may indicate antibiotic resistance or an anatomic abnormality requiring further investigation. (6)
