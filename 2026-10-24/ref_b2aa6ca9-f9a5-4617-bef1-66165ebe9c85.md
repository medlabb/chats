### Distribution



Stepping back and observing the anatomic distribution of skin lesions can also prove useful. For example, plaques of psoriasis often favor *extensor* surfaces (e.g. elbows and knees) while lichenified plaques of atopic dermatitis favor *flexural* surfaces in older children and adults(e.g. the antecubital and popliteal fossae; Table 0.8 ). However, to complicate matters, there is an “inverse” pattern of psoriasis in which lesions are present in major body folds, i.e. in flexural areas (see Ch. 8 ). Langer cleavage lines refer to natural skin tension lines used to guide the orientation of surgical excisions. The long axis of oval lesions of pityriasis rosea 19 and erythema dyschromicum perstans may follow these cleavage lines, and this pattern is often most apparent on the posterior trunk.

Occasionally, the pattern represents a *locus minoris resistentiae* (see text).





A *seborrheic* distribution pattern includes the head and neck as well as the upper trunk; it reflects areas rich in sebaceous glands. Seborrheic dermatitis, acne vulgaris, and pityriasis versicolor often have a seborrheic distribution pattern. The term “ *photodistribution* ”describes an accentuation of lesions in areas exposed to ultraviolet irradiation. Photodermatoses include polymorphic light eruption, phototoxic drug reactions (e.g. to doxycycline), and subacute cutaneous lupus erythematosus. Of note, sometimes a disorder will display a combination of distribution patterns; for example, in dermatomyositis, lesions can be photodistributed and involve extensor surfaces (e.g. elbows, knees).

In addition to differences in the color of inflammatory lesions, individuals with darkly pigmented skin also have an increased frequency of several cutaneous disorders (see section on Color) and certain types of reaction and distribution patterns 20 . Examples of these reaction patterns include papular eczema and a follicular accentuation of atopic dermatitis and pityriasis versicolor, as well as an annular configuration of seborrheic dermatitis and facial secondary syphilis. With regard to distribution, pityriasis rosea often assumes an “inverse” pattern, in which lesions occur primarily in the axillae and groin rather than on the trunk. Although underlying pathomechanisms are not known, it is important to be aware of their occurrence 20 .

Sometimes a distribution pattern is better explained by the phenomenon of *locus minoris resistentiae* , in which certain anatomic sites are more vulnerable than others to a particular disease process 21 . Examples would be cutaneous infections in a lymphedematous limb or asteatotic eczema within a skin graft site. There is also the poorly understood phenomenon of *Wolf isotopic response* , in which a second condition, such as granulomatous dermatitis or leukemia cutis, occurs preferentially in the site of a prior healed process (often herpes zoster; see Table 80.7 ) 22 .

In individuals with darkly pigmented skin, visible lines of demarcation between dorsal and ventral surfaces are more conspicuous, especially on the anterolateral portion of the upper arm ( Fig. 67.11A ). These demarcation lines are bilateral, symmetric, and present from infancy through adulthood. Additional lines occur on the posteromedial aspect of the thighs ( Fig. 67.11B ), upper chest ( Fig. 67.11C ), paraspinal region of the back, and face ( Table 67.6 ; Fig. 67.12 ).
