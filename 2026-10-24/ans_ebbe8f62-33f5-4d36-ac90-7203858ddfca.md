## Answer

 ## Diagnosis of asthma

### Clinical assessment
A presumptive diagnosis is based on **recurrent episodes of wheezing, shortness of breath, chest tightness, or cough** associated with bronchoconstrictor triggers, after considering and excluding alternative diagnoses. A therapeutic trial of low-dose inhaled corticosteroid (ICS) may sometimes be considered, but—except in the mildest cases—diagnosis should generally be confirmed objectively. (1)

History should assess:

- Timing and pattern of symptoms, including nocturnal or morning symptoms
- Exercise, temperature, humidity, allergens, pets, mold, dust, smoke, and occupational exposures
- Rhinitis, sinusitis, gastroesophageal reflux, and medication triggers such as NSAIDs, beta blockers, and ACE inhibitors
- Occupational causes, particularly in adult-onset asthma (2)

Physical examination may be normal between attacks. Findings can include expiratory wheezing, allergic rhinitis, nasal polyps, tachypnea, tachycardia, accessory-muscle use, and a prolonged expiratory phase. A “silent chest” with absent breath sounds may indicate severe obstruction during an attack. (2)

### Pulmonary function testing
Asthma may show:

- Reduced **PEFR**
- Reduced **FEV₁**
- Reduced **FEV₁/FVC ratio**
- A scalloped flow-volume loop

Reversibility is defined in the text as a **≥10% increase in FEV₁ percent predicted** at least 15 minutes after a β₂-agonist or after several weeks of corticosteroid therapy. The older criterion remains commonly used: a **≥12% and at least 200-mL increase in baseline FEV₁**. Diurnal peak-flow variability above 20% has been proposed but is less reliable. (2)

If pulmonary function tests are inconclusive, assessment of **airway hyperresponsiveness** may be needed. Therapy can reduce obstruction and hyperresponsiveness, sometimes requiring medication tapering for diagnostic reassessment. (1)

Additional testing may include eosinophil count, total and specific IgE or skin testing, and exhaled nitric oxide. In untreated patients, FeNO levels above approximately **35–40 ppb** indicate type 2 airway inflammation. (3)

## Treatment of asthma

Treatment aims to reduce symptoms and exacerbations, optimize lung function, maintain normal activities, and minimize medication adverse effects. The goals listed in **Table 298-4** include symptoms and reliever use no more than twice weekly, nighttime awakenings no more than twice monthly, no more than one exacerbation yearly, and optimized lung function. (4)

### General measures
- Identify and reduce relevant triggers and exposures, including occupational exposures, secondhand smoke, combustion products, and clearly associated pets.
- Address comorbidities.
- Confirm adherence and inhaler technique.
- Provide education regarding trigger avoidance.
- Consider vaccination against pneumococcal disease, influenza, COVID-19, and respiratory syncytial virus as described in the reference. (5)

### Stepwise pharmacologic therapy
For patients aged 12 years and older, **Table 298-5** outlines treatment:

| Step | Preferred regular therapy | Reliever options |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | None | Low-dose ICS/formoterol as needed, or alternatives listed in the table |
| 2 | None or low-dose ICS | Low-dose ICS/formoterol as needed, or other table-listed alternatives |
| 3 | Low-dose ICS/formoterol | ICS/formoterol as needed |
| 4 | Medium-dose ICS/formoterol | ICS/formoterol as needed |
| 5 | High-dose ICS/LABA plus add-on LAMA | ICS/formoterol as needed |

Alternative or add-on therapies include leukotriene receptor antagonists, high-dose ICS, leukotriene modifiers, azithromycin, oral corticosteroids, and biologics such as anti-IgE, anti–IL-5, anti–IL-4 receptor-α, or anti-TSLP therapy. Oral corticosteroids should be avoided as much as possible, and biologics are generally reserved for severe asthma with appropriate endotypes or biomarkers. (6)

The preferred approach intensifies or reduces therapy according to asthma control. The cornerstone is appropriate ICS therapy, often combined with a LABA to achieve better control at lower ICS doses. (4)

### Acute worsening or asthma attack
For mild-to-moderate deterioration:

- A β₂-agonist may be administered as often as hourly initially.
- Increasing ICS dose four- to fivefold may help.
- Persistent need for hourly β₂-agonist treatment for several hours warrants urgent care. (7)

In urgent care, treatment may include:

- PEFR or FEV₁ assessment
- Nebulized β₂-agonists, sometimes every 20 minutes
- Corticosteroids if response is inadequate after 1–2 hours
- Supplemental oxygen for hypoxemia
- Nebulized anticholinergics, and sometimes an LTRA or magnesium

Failure to achieve PEFR above 60% of predicted or persistent severe tachypnea over 4–6 hours should prompt consideration of hospital admission. Antibiotics are recommended only when there are signs of infection. Normal or near-normal PCO₂ in a patient with respiratory distress may indicate impending respiratory failure. (7)
