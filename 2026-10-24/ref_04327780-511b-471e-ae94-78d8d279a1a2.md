### C. Laboratory Findings and Diagnostic Tests

Diagnostic criteria are the same for all types of diabetes. Abnormal tests of glycemia must be present on two different occasions in the absence of symptoms. HbA<sub>1c</sub> should be obtained initially and then every 3–6 months. Laboratory evaluation at initial and subsequent annual evaluations includes measurement of lipid panel, liver function tests, urine albumin/creatinine ratio, serum potassium, serum creatinine and eGFR, CBC, and vitamin B12 (when on [metformin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660576)). TSH should be measured annually in individuals with type 1 diabetes.

#### 1. Plasma or serum glucose

Plasma or serum from venous blood samples has the advantage over whole blood of providing values for glucose that are independent of hematocrit and that reflect the glucose concentration to which body tissues are exposed. Venous samples should be collected in tubes containing [sodium](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659912) fluoride to prevent glycolysis, placed on ice and the plasma separated from cells within 60 minutes. In the absence of fluoride, the rate of disappearance of glucose in presence of blood cells has been reported to be approximately 10 mg/dL/h (0.56 mmol/L). The glucose concentration is 10–15% higher in plasma or serum than in whole blood because structural components of blood cells are absent. A plasma glucose level ≥ 126 mg/dL (7 mmol/L) on more than one occasion after at least 8 hours of fasting is diagnostic of diabetes mellitus ([Table 32–4](#papacmdt26_ch32tb4)).

#### 2. Glycated hemoglobin (hemoglobin A<sub>1C</sub>)

Hemoglobin becomes spontaneously glycated when glucose enters the erythrocyte through the glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1). Glucose reacts non-enzymatically to amine groups of proteins such as hemoglobin to form a stable ketoamine called the Amadori compound. HbA<sub>1c</sub> is formed specifically when glucose attaches to the N-terminal valine of the β subunit of hemoglobin. HbA<sub>1c</sub> was chosen as the diagnostic marker for diabetes management due to its stability. HbA<sub>1c</sub> is elevated in persons with hyperglycemia due to greater glycosylation of hemoglobin. Methods for measuring HbA<sub>1c</sub> include electrophoresis, cation-exchange chromatography, boronate affinity chromatography, and immunoassays. Office-based immunoassays for HbA<sub>1c</sub> using capillary blood give a result in about 9 minutes, and this allows for nearly immediate feedback to the patients regarding their glycemic control. HbA<sub>1c</sub> reflects glucose bound to hemoglobin within RBCs. Since the average RBC life span is about 120 days, the HbA<sub>1c</sub> is a weighted average of recent blood glucose exposure during the preceding 8–12 weeks, providing an indirect method of assessing mean glucose. HbA<sub>1c</sub> is a primary technique to diagnose diabetes and assess management. However, there is substantial inter- and intra-individual variability in HbA<sub>1c</sub>, and caution should be exercised in estimating average glucose levels from measured HbA<sub>1c</sub>. For example, for HbA<sub>1c</sub> values between 6.9% and 7.1%, the glucose levels can range from 125 mg/dL to 205 mg/dL (6.9–11.4 mmol/L).

#### 3. Oral glucose tolerance test

In an asymptomatic nonpregnant adult in whom diabetes is suspected, a standardized oral glucose tolerance test can diagnose diabetes. This test can identify individuals with diabetes whose diagnoses might otherwise be missed, such as those with posttransplant diabetes or cystic fibrosis–related diabetes. In order to optimize insulin secretion and effectiveness, a minimum of 150–200 g of carbohydrate daily should be included in the diet for 3 days before the test. The patient should eat nothing after midnight before the test day. The test is performed in the morning because of diurnal variation in oral glucose tolerance; patients should not smoke or be active during the test. On the morning of the test, patients consume 75 g of glucose in 300 mL of water within 5 minutes. Blood samples for plasma glucose are obtained at 0 and 120 minutes after ingestion of glucose. An oral glucose tolerance test is normal if the fasting venous plasma glucose value is < 100 mg/dL (5.6 mmol/L) and the 2-hour value falls below 140 mg/dL (7.8 mmol/L). A fasting value ≥ 126 mg/dL (7 mmol/L) or a 2-hour value > 200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L) is diagnostic of diabetes mellitus ([Table 32–4](#papacmdt26_ch32tb4)). Patients with a 2-hour value of 140–199 mg/dL (7.8–11.1 mmol/L) have impaired glucose tolerance. False-positive results may occur in patients who are malnourished, are bedridden, or have an infection or severe emotional stress.

#### 4. Urine glucose

A convenient method to detect glucosuria is the paper strip impregnated with glucose oxidase and a chromogen system (Clinistix, Diastix), which is sensitive to as little as 100 mg/dL (5.5 mmol) glucose in urine. A normal renal threshold for glucose as well as reliable bladder emptying are essential for interpretation.

Nondiabetic glycosuria (renal glycosuria) is a benign asymptomatic condition wherein glucose appears in the urine despite a normal amount of glucose in the blood, either basally or during a glucose tolerance test. Its cause may vary from mutations in the *SGLT2* gene coding for sodium-glucose transporter 2 (familial renal glycosuria) to one associated with dysfunction of the proximal renal tubule (Fanconi syndrome, CKD), or it may merely be a consequence of the increased load of glucose presented to the tubules by the elevated GFR during pregnancy. As many as 50% of pregnant patients typically have demonstrable sugar in the urine, especially during the third and fourth months. This sugar is practically always glucose except during the late weeks of pregnancy, when lactose may be present.

## ESSENTIALS OF DIAGNOSIS

ESSENTIALS OF DIAGNOSIS

**Type 1 diabetes**

- Polyuria, polydipsia, and weight loss with random plasma glucose ≥ 200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L).

- Plasma glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL (7.0 mmol/L) after an overnight fast, documented on more than one occasion.

- Ketonemia, ketonuria, or both.

- Islet autoantibodies frequently present.

- Generally progresses from stage 1 (autoantibodies but normal glucose tolerance) to stage 2 (autoantibodies with glucose intolerance) to stage 3 (symptomatic disease).

**Prediabetes**

- An intermediate stage of glucose dysregulation between normal blood glucose and development of type 2 diabetes

- Fasting plasma glucose 100–125 mg/dL (6.1–6.9 mmol/L), plasma glucose 2 hours after 75 g oral glucose 140–199 mg/dL (7.8–11 mmol/L), and/or HbA<sub>1c</sub> (5.7–6.4%)

- More than 1 in 3 US adults have prediabetes.

**Type 2 diabetes**

- Most patients are > 40 years of age and have obesity, but onset at any age is possible

- Polyuria and polydipsia, ketonuria, and weight loss are less common at time of diagnosis

- Candidal vaginitis may be an initial manifestation, or asymptomatic

- Plasma glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL after an overnight fast on more than one occasion, and/or 2 hours after 75 g oral glucose, plasma glucose ≥ 200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol)

- HbA<sub>1c</sub> ≥ 6.5%

## TREATMENT

Foundational aspects of the management of individuals with diabetes include building positive health behaviors and maintaining psychosocial wellbeing. To achieve this, diabetes self-management education and support (DSMES), medical nutrition therapy (MNT), routine physical activity, adequate-quality sleep, support for cessation of tobacco products and vaping, counseling on health behaviors, and psychosocial care are vital. Individuals living with diabetes should receive person-centered collaborative care that involves shared decision-making in the treatment plan and facilitated access to medical, technological, psychosocial, and behavioral health resources and support.

### B. Medications

Treatment must be individualized on the basis of the type of diabetes and specific needs of each patient. However, certain general principles of management can be outlined for hyperglycemic states of different types.

#### 1. Type 1 diabetes

Traditional once- or twice-daily insulin regimens are usually ineffective in patients with type 1 diabetes who do not have residual endogenous insulin. If near-normalization of blood glucose is attempted, at least four measurements of capillary blood glucose and three or four insulin injections are necessary.

A combination of rapidly acting insulin analogs and long-acting insulin analogs allows for more physiologic insulin replacement. In a study comparing regular insulin with insulin lispro, daily insulin doses and HbA<sub>1c</sub> levels were similar, but insulin lispro improved postprandial control, reduced hypoglycemic episodes, and improved patient convenience compared with regular insulin. However, because of their relatively short duration (no more than 3–4 hours), the rapidly acting insulin analogs need to be combined with longer-acting insulins to provide basal coverage and avoid hyperglycemia prior to the next meal. In addition to carbohydrate content of the meal, the effect of simultaneous fat ingestion must also be considered a factor in determining the rapidly acting insulin analog dosage required to control the glycemic increment during and just after the meal. With low-carbohydrate content and high-fat intake, there is an increased risk of hypoglycemia from insulin lispro within 2 hours after the meal. #### 2. Type 2 diabetes

Therapeutic recommendations are based on the relative contributions of β cell insufficiency and insulin insensitivity in individual patients. The possibility that the individual patient has a specific etiologic cause for their diabetes should always be considered, especially when the patient does not have a family history of type 2 diabetes or does not have any evidence of central obesity or insulin resistance. Such patients should be evaluated for other types of diabetes such as **autoimmune (type 1)**, prior pancreatic injury, or MODY ([Table 32–1](#papacmdt26_ch32tb1)). Patients with **autoimmune diabetes** should be prescribed insulin when the disease is diagnosed. Importantly, many patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus have a progressive loss of β cell function and will require additional therapeutic interventions with time.
