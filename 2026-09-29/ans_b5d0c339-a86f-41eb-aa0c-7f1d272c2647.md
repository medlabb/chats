 ## Approach to tachycardia

### 1. Initial assessment
- **Assess hemodynamic stability immediately.** Hypotension, impaired consciousness, angina, or heart failure warrant immediate therapy guided by the rhythm type. (1)
- Begin **continuous ECG monitoring** and obtain a **12-lead ECG whenever possible**. (2)
- Review the history and examination for **structural heart disease**, concurrent illness, sympathetic stimulation, drug toxicity, and other precipitating factors; abnormal findings may warrant further cardiac evaluation. (1) (3)

### 2. Determine whether it is sinus tachycardia
Sinus tachycardia is the most common SVT and may occur with exercise, stress, stimulants, or acute illness. If sinus tachycardia is present, the primary treatment is correction of the **underlying inciting cause**, rather than treatment directed at terminating the rhythm. (1) (4)

### 3. Analyze the ECG
Classify the rhythm by:

- **QRS width:** narrow QRS is <120 ms; a wide QRS may reflect bundle-branch block, an accessory pathway, or ventricular tachycardia. (4)
- **Regularity:** regular versus irregular.
- **P-wave relationship and morphology:** the 12-lead ECG can help distinguish atrial tachycardia, AV nodal reentry, AV reentrant tachycardia, atrial flutter, and atrial fibrillation. **Figure 253-1** summarizes diagnostic possibilities based on the ECG appearance. (5)
- Mechanistically, tachycardias may be physiologic sinus tachycardia, atrial tachycardia/flutter/fibrillation, AV nodal reentry, or tachycardia associated with an accessory pathway; **Table 253-1** summarizes these categories. (4)

### 4. Management according to stability and QRS

#### Unstable tachycardia
If tachycardia causes hypotension with unconsciousness or respiratory distress, **QRS-synchronous direct-current cardioversion** is warranted. Emergency cardioversion and defibrillation should be available. (2)

#### Stable, regular narrow-complex tachycardia
For likely paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia:

1. Attempt **vagal maneuvers**, including Valsalva in cooperative patients. Carotid sinus massage is reasonable only when carotid vascular disease risk is low, with no carotid bruit or prior stroke. (2)
2. If unsuccessful or not feasible, give intravenous **adenosine**, which terminates most AV-node–dependent PSVTs. ECG recording should continue because the response may help establish the mechanism. (2)
3. Intravenous **beta blockers**, verapamil, or diltiazem are alternatives but may cause hypotension and have longer-lasting effects. (2)

The treatment algorithm for stable PSVT is shown in **Figure 256-6**. (2)

#### Wide-complex tachycardia
The differential includes ventricular tachycardia, PSVT with aberrancy, and preexcited tachycardia. It should generally be managed as **ventricular tachycardia until proven otherwise**. If regular and stable, a trial of intravenous adenosine may be reasonable. Very irregular wide-complex tachycardia is most likely preexcited atrial fibrillation or flutter and should be managed with cardioversion, intravenous procainamide, or ibutilide. (2)

#### Suspected focal atrial tachycardia
Vagal maneuvers or adenosine may increase AV block and expose P waves without terminating the atrial tachycardia; some cases terminate with adenosine. Beta blockers or calcium-channel blockers may slow the ventricular rate. Precipitating factors, intercurrent illness, drug toxicity, and underlying heart disease should be sought and corrected or excluded. (6)

### 5. Further evaluation and long-term management
Patients with recurrent symptomatic episodes may require electrophysiologic evaluation and catheter ablation. Echocardiography is generally the next test when structural heart disease is suspected, to assess cardiac structure and ventricular function. (7)
