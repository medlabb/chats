TD does not appear to be a progressive disorder for most patients. The disorder seems to develop rather rapidly and then stabilize and often improves with time. The consensus of a number of long-term studies is that the severity of TD in most patients improves, even if their dopamine blockers are continued. Moreover, this improvement can be clinically meaningful in some patients. Individuals who are most likely to remit are those with recent onset and those with an onset prior to age 40. When dopamine blockers are discontinued, a substantial proportion of patients with TD will demonstrate remission. Reinstatement of a dopamine blocker does not mean an automatic reoccurrence. This is particularly likely for those with a rather recent onset of TD. Sometimes, this remission will take several months to occur.

#### Perioral Tremor

Perioral tremor is an uncommon side effect of dopamine receptor antagonists that appears after months or years of drug treatment. The peculiar tremors of the mouth have led to this disorder being called rabbit syndrome.

Management of TD. Patients receiving dopamine blockers for more than 6 months should have regular and systematic evaluations for TD every 6 months. Although instruments such as the Abnormal Involuntary Movement Scale (AIMS) were originally developed as research tools, they also provide a structured system for evaluating and recording abnormal movements.

Once TD is diagnosed, the clinician should evaluate the need for continued antipsychotic treatment. This decision requires consideration of the dangers of both the psychotic illness and TD. It is a decision that, whenever possible, should be made in collaboration with the patient and their family. The results of this discussion should be documented in the patient’s medical record. For most patients with chronic schizophrenia, the best decision will probably involve continuing the antipsychotic. If this is the case, there is some evidence that treating patients with the lowest effective dose may minimize the risk of TD. Moreover, prescribing the lowest effective dosage of antipsychotic medication may also decrease the patient’s risk of developing the disorder. Patients with severe TD may also be candidates for a trial of clozapine or other dopamine–serotonin antagonists. Most clinical reports indicate that clozapine can suppress TD.

The American Psychiatric Association task force on TD recommended a number of steps for the prevention and management of the disorder, including (1) establishing objective evidence that antipsychotic medications are effective; (2) using the minimum effective dosage for long-term treatment; (3) exercising caution with children, the older adults, and patients with mood disorders; (4) examining patients on a regular basis for evidence of dyskinesia and noting the results of the examination in the medical record; (5) if TD is diagnosed, considering alternatives to antipsychotics, obtaining informed consent, and considering dosage reduction; and (6) if the dyskinesia worsens, considering the discontinuation of the antipsychotic, switching to a different drug, or considering a trial of clozapine.

The vesicular monoamine transporter 2 (VMAT2) is a protein embedded in the synaptic vesicle’s membrane and regulates dopamine uptake from the cytoplasm for storage and subsequent release. *Tetrabenazine*, an earlier generation VMAT2 inhibitor, was approved for the treatment of Huntington chorea and also considered as a potential therapy for TD. Due to its short half-life (2–10 hours), it needs to be dosed twice daily. In addition, common AEs can include body aches or pain, difficulty in breathing; difficulty with swallowing; drowsiness; fear; or nervousness.

Two new-generation VMAT2 inhibitors, *valbenazine* (Ingrezza) and *deutetrabenazine* (Austedo) were subsequently FDA approved specifically for TD and can differ from tetrabenazine in terms of pharmacokinetics, dosing requirements, tolerability, and safety. To date, however, there are no trials directly comparing valbenazine, deutetrabenazine, and tetrabenazine for the treatment of TD.

Valbenazine is the purified prodrug of the (+) α-isomer of tetrabenazine. It is a highly selective VMAT2 inhibitor with a half-life of 20 hours, allowing for once-daily dosing. It is the first medication approved by the FDA for the treatment of moderate to severe TD in adults. In one 6-week RCT, patients with moderate to severe TD (*n* = 339) were assigned to valbenazine (40 mg), valbenazine (80 mg), or placebo. At the study’s end, those on valbenazine had a significantly greater (*p* < .001) reduction in their Abnormal Involuntary Movement Scale (AIMS) scores versus placebo, with a larger difference found for the 80-mg dose (i.e., –3.2 vs. –0.1) versus the 40 mg dose (i.e., –1.9 vs. –0.1). Common adverse effects of valbenazine can include drowsiness, dizziness, restlessness, and vomiting. It is also associated with the risk of QT prolongation and contraindicated in combination with CYP3A4/2D6 isoenzymes or monoamine oxidase inhibitors (MAOIs) due to increased monoamine levels in the synapse, potentially predisposing to the development of the serotonin syndrome.

Deutetrabenazine was subsequently FDA approved for moderate to severe TD. This agent is an isotopic isomer of tetrabenazine in which six hydrogen atoms are substituted with deuterium. ### TARDIVE DYSKINESIA

#### Clinical Presentation

Symptoms of TD should develop after at least 2 months of exposure to a dopamine-blocking agent (or 1 month if the patient is 60 years or older); while the patient is on medication or within 4 weeks of withdrawal from an oral agent (8 weeks from long-acting injectable medication); and be present for at least 4 weeks. The patient usually demonstrates involuntary movements of the tongue (e.g., twisting, protrusion); jaw (e.g., chewing); lips (e.g., smacking, puckering); trunk; or extremities. Patterns of movements may include rapid, jerky, and nonrepetitive movements (i.e., choreiform); slow, continuous, and sinuous movements (i.e., athetoid); or rhythmic movements.

The onset of the disorder may be gradual, occurring over a period of months to years. Symptoms are often seen in older patients who have poorly fitting dentures or other reasons for dyskinetic movements. In older patients (55 years or older), the onset of TD is about 25% after 1 year, 34% after 2 years, and 53% after 3 years. This rate of onset is three to five times higher than in younger patients. Patients with multi-infarct dementia and a psychiatric diagnosis are at greater risk than patients with Alzheimer disease or other organic mental disorders. As noted earlier, TD may also occur in never-medicated patients with schizophrenia.

Risk factors include increasing age, pre-existing parkinsonism, previous brain damage, antipsychotic treatment duration, and exposure to FGAs. These symptoms should not be due to a general medical or neurologic disorder or present as an adverse effect of another class of medication.

#### Pathophysiology

Sustained D<sub>2</sub> receptor blockade resulting in receptor hypersensitivity is the most common hypothesis explaining the development of TD. Clozapine’s decreased propensity to induce TD and its multiple neuroreceptor effects (e.g., binding to 5-HT<sub>1C</sub>, 5-HT<sub>2</sub>, and 5-HT<sub>3</sub> receptors) suggest a role for serotonin as well. Serotonin-containing neurons interact with dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra and ventral tegmental areas. Genetic studies indicate a possible relationship with polymorphisms in the DA2 receptor, DA3 receptor, dopamine transporter (DAT1), and the serotonin 2A receptor genes. Oxidative stress and cell death secondary to increased glutamatergic neurotransmission caused by blockade of presynaptic dopamine receptors are also postulated. Other potential proposed theories involve GABA depletion and cholinergic deficiency.

#### Treatment Options and Implications

Since the advent of SGAs, fewer episodes and less severe symptoms of TD are now seen. For this reason, patients on FGAs are generally *switched to a newer agent if possible*. Further, there is evidence that agents such as clozapine may improve symptoms of TD without causing progression of the disorder. For example, some studies of clozapine report reduced symptom severity in tardive syndromes by up to 50%. Patients on an FGA who develop TD and are switched to an SGA have demonstrated improvement in their symptoms but which specific agents are preferable remains unclear. Although a small percentage of cases progress to florid and potentially disabling or life-threatening intensity, others remain stable, and some show spontaneous improvement.

Current evidence suggests that dopamine–serotonin antagonists are associated with a lower risk of TD than dopamine D2 receptor agonists. This has been proposed since the decreased tendency of these agents to cause acute EPS would predict a lower risk of TD. An accumulation of controlled and uncontrolled studies led a consensus group of experts in the pharmacotherapy of schizophrenia to conclude that dopamine–serotonin antagonists were associated with a lower risk of TD. Because most of these studies were not designed to focus on TD, this remains a controversial issue. A Cochrane review did not find sufficient evidence to conclude that there was an advantage for dopamine–serotonin antagonists, while other meta-analyses concluded that the available evidence supported an advantage. Still, despite the high penetration of dopamine–serotonin antagonists into clinical practice, the incidence and prevalence of TD appear relatively unchanged since the 1980s. Since it would be difficult to perform an adequate study of sufficient duration to prove that there is a difference, this issue remains unresolved.

There are currently two medications approved for the treatment of TD: deutetrabenazine and valbenazine. Both are VMAT2 inhibitors and can be administered in conjunction with antipsychotic treatments. Using these medications is suggested if TD symptoms cannot be controlled with dose reductions or medication changes.

### CHRONIC EXTRAPYRAMIDAL SYNDROMES

#### Tardive Dyskinesia

TD is a movement disorder that can follow chronic treatment with antipsychotic medications. Patients with TD may have any or all of a number of abnormal movements, including mouth and tongue movements (lip smacking, sucking, and puckering), as well as facial grimacing. Other movements may include irregular movements of the limbs, particularly choreoathetoid-like movements of the fingers and toes, and slow, writhing movements of the trunk. Although seriously disabling TD is uncommon, a small proportion may affect walking, breathing, eating, and talking.

The differential diagnosis of TD includes a number of disorders of the basal ganglia (including Huntington disease, Wilson disease, and Sydenham chorea), striatal hypercalcifications, hyperthyroidism, hypoparathyroidism, tardive Tourette disorder, and dyskinesias related to other drugs, such as L-dopa and amphetamines. Abnormal movements were also described in patients with schizophrenia before the introduction of antipsychotic medications.

Although dopamine-blocking medications will frequently suppress or reduce TD, dose increases are only appropriate when the movements are severe and incapacitating. If patients have their dosages of antipsychotic medication either decreased or discontinued, abnormal movements may worsen temporarily or appear for the first time and then diminish, termed withdrawal emergent dyskinesias. Antiparkinsonian medications will often worsen TD. However, when patients suffer from combined TD and acute EPS, prescribing antiparkinsonian medication is often unavoidable.

Although the biologic mechanism underlying TD is still controversial, it is likely that the disorder is related to an increased sensitivity of dopamine receptors in the basal ganglia. This is supported by the observation that drugs that block dopamine, such as antipsychotic medications, suppress TD, whereas dopamine agonists worsen the disorder. These observations are consistent with findings from preclinical laboratories indicating that blocking dopamine receptors can result in their upward regulation.

Approximately 10% to 20% of patients treated with dopamine blockers for more than a year develop TD. In chronically institutionalized patients, the prevalence of TD is between 15% and 20% but increases with each year of treatment. Recent prospective studies indicate that approximately 4% to 5% of patients who are undergoing antipsychotic drug treatment, particularly with dopamine D2 receptor antagonists, will develop TD each year, with this risk markedly compounded in older adult individuals whose first treatment with antipsychotics is after age 65. Certain populations are at a greater risk than others for developing TD. Older adult women are particularly vulnerable. Moreover, TD in older adults is less likely to remit when dopamine blockers are discontinued. Patients with affective disorders may also be at a greater risk of TD when they are treated with dopamine receptor antagonists. Other possible risk factors for TD are the dosage of antipsychotic medications and the duration of treatment. There is also evidence indicating that the presence of acute EPS increases the risk of developing TD later.
