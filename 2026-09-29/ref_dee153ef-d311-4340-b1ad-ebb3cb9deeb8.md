# STANDARDIZED METHODS AND ESTABLISHED BREAKPOINTS

Antifungal susceptibility methods for yeasts are similar to those used for bacteria [9,13,38].
The Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) and the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) have developed, validated, and published methods for broth microdilution testing against yeasts and filamentous fungi. CLSI has also established methods for disk diffusion testing against yeasts and certain filamentous fungi, although these are less often used by clinical laboratories, especially for testing against filamentous fungi.

# INDICATIONS FOR TESTING

Antifungal susceptibility testing should be considered to help identify resistance and provide treatment guidance in the following situations  (table 3):
- AllCandidaisolates from blood or normally sterile body fluids and tissues

- Mucosal candidiasis that is unresponsive to usual antifungal therapy

- Invasive fungal disease that is unresponsive to initial therapy

- Fungal infections caused by species with significant rates of acquired resistance (eg,Candida glabrataandCandida auris) that are failing to respond to usual antifungal therapy (see'Cross-resistance'below)

- Invasive disease caused by unusual fungal species for which susceptibility patterns are unpredictable or not well established

Some treatment guidelines also recommend antifungal susceptibility testing against isolates ofAspergillus fumigatusand otherAspergillusspp cultured from patients with invasive disease, although this recommendation is not universal [34,35].
Susceptibility testing for species/antifungal agents should not be performed when intrinsic resistance is known (eg,Candida kruseiandfluconazole,Cryptococcusspecies and echinocandins)  (table 2) [4,36,37].

# SUMMARY AND RECOMMENDATIONS

- **Importance of susceptibility testing**– The need for reproducible, clinically relevant antifungal susceptibility testing has been prompted by increasing numbers of invasive fungal infections, expanded use of new and established antifungal agents, and recognition of antifungal resistance as an important clinical problem. (See'Introduction'above.)

- **Antifungal susceptibility standards**– The Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) Subcommittee on Antifungal Susceptibility Testing and the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) have developed, validated, and standardized methods for broth-based antifungal susceptibility testing. (See'Standardized methods and established breakpoints'above.)

- Several commercial methods for antifungal susceptibility testing are available. (See'Commercial methods for antifungal susceptibility testing'above.)

- **Indications for susceptibility testing**– There are several situations in which antifungal susceptibility testing should be considered or obtained  (table 3):

AllCandidaisolates from blood or normally sterile bodily fluids and tissues

Mucosal candidiasis that is unresponsive to usual antifungal therapy

Invasive fungal disease that is unresponsive to initial therapy

Fungal infections caused by species with significant rates of acquired resistance (eg,Candida glabrataandCandida auris) that are failing to respond to usual antifungal therapy (see'Cross-resistance'above)

Invasive disease caused by unusual fungal species for which susceptibility patterns are unpredictable or not well established  (table 5)

- **Susceptibility patterns**– Many fungal species have predictable antifungal susceptibility patterns  (table 5). However, rising rates of resistance to antifungal agents have occurred among some fungal species due to the increase in the immunocompromised population and the frequent use offluconazoleand echinocandins. (See'Susceptibility patterns and notable trends'above.)

- **Cross-resistance**– Cross-resistance is a concern, particularly among members of the same class of antifungal agents. (See'Cross-resistance'above.)

## *Candida*species

### Amphotericin B
Amphotericin B has broad-spectrum activity against mostCandidaspp. AgainstC. albicans, the 90 percent minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC90) has been reported to be 0.5 mcg/mL. Although the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) has not set breakpoints for amphotericin B against any fungal species, the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) has set breakpoints for this polyene against commonCandidaspp, with the susceptible threshold at ≤1 mcg/mL and resistance at >1 mcg/mL (eg, MICs ≥2 mcg/mL).
BothC. glabrataandC. kruseican exhibit decreased susceptibility to amphotericin B compared withC. albicans(table 6). Amphotericin B susceptibility testing may not be clinically useful againstCandida lusitaniaebecause resistance frequently develops during treatment despite low MIC results against the initially cultured isolates  (table 5) [64-67].
Amphotericin B resistance has also been reported inC. aurisisolates based on the recommended United States Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) breakpoint of ≥2 mcg/mL [68,69], although the range from different studies is quite variable (0 to 90 percent). Furthermore, amphotericin B MICs, and thus resistance rates, inC. aurisare influenced by the method used to perform susceptibility testing. High MICs and high rates of resistance have been reported in studies that have used the commercial systems including VITEK 2, YeastOne, and gradient diffusion strips, compared with the CLSI and EUCAST reference broth microdilution methods [21,70-77].
### Azoles
Fluconazolegenerally demonstrates good activity againstC. albicans,C. parapsilosis, andC. tropicalis, with MIC values generally ≤2 mcg/mL against most strains of these species  (table 5). However, resistance can develop in these species, and there is now concern for increased fluconazole resistance inC. parapsilosisas this has been reported in some institutions in the United States and in other countries [78-83]. Reduced fluconazole susceptibility is common in strains ofC. glabrataandCandida guilliermondii, andC. kruseiis intrinsically resistant to fluconazole.Voriconazole,posaconazole, andisavuconazolegenerally demonstrate potent in vitro activity againstC. kruseiand many other commonCandidaspp  (table 5).
C. aurisisolates are usually resistant tofluconazole(90 percent having fluconazole MICs ≥32 mcg/mL), and many also have elevatedvoriconazoleMICs [70]. Althoughposaconazoleandisavuconazoleoften have low MICs againstC. aurisisolates, the clinical significance of this is unknown, and resistance to fluconazole is often used to predict resistance to other members of this class of antifungals.
### Echinocandins and Ibrexafungerp
Each of the echinocandins demonstrates potent in vitro activity against mostCandidaspp  (table 5).
Echinocandin activity is often maintained against isolates that have developed resistance to the azoles. The echinocandins do have reduced in vitro activity against members of theC. parapsilosisspecies complex, includingC. parapsilosis sensu stricto, Candida orthopsilosis, andCandida metapsilosis, as well asC. guilliermondii[84-86].
Like the echinocandins,ibrexafungerpis active againstCandidaspp, includingC. albicans,C. glabrata,C. parapsilosis, andC. tropicalis, as well as the emerging pathogenC. auris[87-90]. This activity is also maintained against azole-resistant strains.
### Flucytosine
Flucytosinegenerally demonstrates good activity against mostCandidaspp, with 95 percent of clinical isolates having MICs of ≤4 mcg/mL [91]. Flucytosine MICs are generally very low forC. albicans,C. glabrata,C. parapsilosis,C. tropicalis,C. guilliermondii, andC. lusitaniae(table 6). In contrast, resistance to flucytosine is common among isolates ofC. krusei.
