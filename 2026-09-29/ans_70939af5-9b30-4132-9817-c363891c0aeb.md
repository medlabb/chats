 ## Principles of Neurolocalization

**Neurolocalization** is the process of identifying the anatomical site of a neurological lesion using the patient’s history and physical examination. This narrows the differential diagnosis and guides further investigations. (1)

### 1. Begin with a systematic approach
- Address immediate **airway, breathing, circulation, and other vital functions** first.
- Obtain a focused history followed by a brief neurological examination.
- After emergencies are addressed, assess the deficit systematically, proceeding from **cephalad to caudad and centrally to peripherally**. (2) (3)

### 2. Analyze the distribution of symptoms
- **Face, arm, and leg involvement on one side** usually indicates a central nervous system lesion. (4)
- **Weakness or sensory loss confined to one limb** suggests a peripheral nerve source. (4)
- A **myotomal pattern** suggests a nerve-root lesion; a deficit matching a particular peripheral nerve suggests a peripheral neuropathy. (3)
- Bilateral involvement should prompt assessment for spinal cord, brain, peripheral nerve, muscle, or neuromuscular-junction disease. (3)

### 3. Use the time course and associated symptoms
- Symptoms with **abrupt onset**, particularly over seconds, usually suggest a central lesion. (4)
- Symptoms occurring after prolonged positioning or activity are more suggestive of peripheral nerve compression. (4)
- Extremity pain accompanying neurological symptoms often favors a peripheral process, although some central disorders can also cause pain. (4)

### 4. Examine motor findings
Assess the distribution and character of weakness:
- **Upper motor neuron signs** include spasticity, increased reflexes, and a Babinski sign.
- **Lower motor neuron signs** include atrophy, flaccid weakness, and reduced or absent reflexes.
- **Proximal weakness** is more consistent with myopathy or neuromuscular-junction disease, whereas distal weakness suggests polyneuropathy. (5) (6)
- Fluctuating or fatigable weakness suggests a neuromuscular-junction disorder. (3)

### 5. Examine sensory findings
- Identify whether sensory loss follows a **dermatome**, a **peripheral nerve territory**, a **glove-and-stocking pattern**, or a **sensory level**.
- A sensory level, with relatively normal sensation above and reduced or absent sensation below, supports spinal cord localization. (5)
- Different sensory modalities cross at different levels, which helps localize lesions. (7)
- Relevant dermatome landmarks include C6 at the thumb and index finger, C7 at the middle finger, C8 at the fourth and fifth fingers, L5 at the great toe and lateral lower leg, and S1 at the fifth toe and sole. (7) **Figures 3, 5, and 6** illustrate relevant dermatomes.

### 6. Assess reflexes and associated signs
- Reduced or absent reflexes generally suggest a neuropathic lesion.
- Reflexes are usually preserved in muscle disease and are generally normal in myasthenia gravis. (6)
- Bladder dysfunction supports **myelopathy**, while ocular or bulbar weakness may suggest neuromuscular-junction disease or central involvement. (3)

### 7. Recognize important localization patterns
- **Cortical lesions:** weakness accompanied by cortical signs such as aphasia, neglect, agnosia, or apraxia. (3)
- **Brainstem lesions:** may produce cranial-nerve findings, vertigo, diplopia, or crossed face–body deficits. (8)
- **Spinal cord lesions:** bilateral motor or sensory findings that spare the face, long-tract signs, segmental lower motor neuron signs, and possibly a sensory level or sphincter dysfunction. (5)
- **Peripheral lesions:** weakness or sensory loss confined to a nerve, root, or plexus distribution. (6)
- **Neuromuscular-junction lesions:** fatigable weakness, ptosis, diplopia, or bulbar symptoms. (3)
