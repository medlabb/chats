## Answer

 A **frontal lobe lesion** may produce:

- **Contralateral motor weakness**, ranging from focal hand weakness to involvement of the face, arm, and leg. A parasagittal lesion characteristically affects both legs and may later involve the arms. Upper motor neuron signs include spasticity, increased reflexes, Babinski response, and relatively little muscle atrophy. (1)
- **Gaze deviation**: an acute hemispheric lesion may cause both eyes to deviate toward the lesion and away from the side of hemiparesis. A frontal seizure focus may drive the eyes away from the focus. (2) (rf_94)
- **Apraxia**, particularly unilateral apraxia from a contralateral premotor frontal cortex lesion; bifrontal lesions may cause bilateral apraxia, including gait apraxia. (3) (4)
- **Expressive (Broca) aphasia** when the dominant frontal language region is affected, with impaired language expression but relatively preserved comprehension. The aphasia syndromes are summarized in **Table 1-1**, and relevant language regions are illustrated in **Figure 1-7**. (5) (4)
- **Behavioral and language syndromes in frontotemporal dementia**: frontal involvement may cause apathy, disinhibition, blunted emotions, impaired judgment, inattention, and disorganization. Predominant dominant frontal involvement may cause nonfluent aphasia with halting, agrammatic speech and preserved comprehension. MRI may show frontal and temporal atrophy, as illustrated in **Figure 5-8**. (6)
