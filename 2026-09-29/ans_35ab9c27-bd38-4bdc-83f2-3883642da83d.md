 ## Approach to tachycardia

1. **Assess immediate stability**
   Look for hypotension, shock or poor perfusion, altered/decreased consciousness, ischemic chest pain, shortness of breath, or acute pulmonary edema/heart failure. Check oxygen saturation and signs of hypoxemia or labored breathing. (1) (2)

2. **If hemodynamically unstable**
   - If the tachycardia is **not sinus tachycardia**, perform **urgent synchronized cardioversion**; provide sedation and analgesia if time permits. (1) (2)
   - In pediatric patients, unstable tachycardia with a pulse is treated with synchronized cardioversion, initially **0.5–1 J/kg**. (3)
   - Some supraventricular tachycardias may respond to an immediate IV adenosine bolus while cardioversion is being prepared. (1)

3. **If stable**
   Obtain and systematically assess a 12-lead ECG—15 leads are generally preferred in children—and determine: (1) sinus or nonsinus rhythm, (2) narrow or wide QRS, and (3) regular or irregular rhythm. Continuous ECG monitoring is recommended during treatment. (1) (4)

4. **Regular, narrow-complex tachycardia**
   - Consider **sinus tachycardia**, which commonly results from fever, anemia, shock, sepsis, pain, heart failure, dehydration, or other physiologic stress. Treat the underlying cause; antiarrhythmic medication is not required for sinus tachycardia. (1)
   - For likely re-entrant SVT, attempt **vagal maneuvers** such as Valsalva; a modified Valsalva with supine repositioning and passive leg raise may be more effective. (1)
   - If vagal maneuvers fail, **adenosine** is recommended for acute SVT treatment. In children, the initial dose is **0.1 mg/kg IV/IO, maximum 6 mg**, administered rapidly and followed by a rapid saline flush. (3)
   - For orthodromic AVRT in adults, options after adenosine include IV verapamil or diltiazem when there is no hypotension or reduced left ventricular systolic function; refractory cases may be treated with IV procainamide or a beta blocker. (5)

5. **Wide-complex tachycardia**
   Wide QRS tachycardia may represent ventricular tachycardia or SVT with bundle-branch block, aberrancy, or accessory-pathway conduction. Management depends on the rhythm and hemodynamic status. (6)
   
   In a stable child with a **regular monomorphic** wide-complex rhythm, adenosine may be used diagnostically to assess for SVT with aberrant conduction. Antiarrhythmic treatment with agents such as amiodarone or procainamide has significant adverse effects, so pediatric cardiology consultation is strongly encouraged. (3)

6. **Address underlying causes**
   Management should include treatment of reversible or underlying causes, with additional therapy tailored to the arrhythmia type and the patient’s hemodynamic status. (7)
