### TARDIVE DYSTONIA/TARDIVE AKATHISIA

#### Clinical Presentation

Tardive dystonia and tardive akathisia are movement symptoms that arise later in treatment with an antipsychotic and may persist for months or years despite the reduction or discontinuation of the patient’s medication. Young male patients have a higher risk. This syndrome is differentiated from acute dystonia and acute akathisia based on the time to onset. Both tardive dystonia and akathisia are associated with a higher incidence of TD.

#### Pathophysiology

There is little known about the pathophysiology of tardive dystonia and tardive akathisia. Tardive dystonia appears to involve impairment of the basal ganglia, and tardive akathisia may involve dysfunction of the pontine region of the brain.

#### Treatment Options and Implications

Both of these movement disorders are preventable with the use of SGAs (e.g., clozapine; quetiapine). If the patient has symptoms while on or after discontinuing an antipsychotic, an increase in its dose or immediate reintroduction of the agent may be necessary to increase dopamine blockade. Such interventions can achieve short-term relief.

One potential treatment for refractory tardive dystonia is DBS. In one report, 60 patients with symptoms of various types of dystonia underwent DBS with leads placed in the globus pallidus pars interna. Using the Burke–Fahn–Marsden Dystonia Rating Scale (BFM), patients were assessed preoperatively, postoperatively, and for up to 5 years of follow-up. Improvement in the BFM severity and disability scores were 43% and 27%, respectively, by 6 months and were sustained thereafter. Thus, DBS may be a potential option for patients with severe, treatment-refractory, medication-induced tardive dystonia.

### CHRONIC EXTRAPYRAMIDAL SYNDROMES

#### Tardive Dyskinesia

TD is a movement disorder that can follow chronic treatment with antipsychotic medications. Patients with TD may have any or all of a number of abnormal movements, including mouth and tongue movements (lip smacking, sucking, and puckering), as well as facial grimacing. Other movements may include irregular movements of the limbs, particularly choreoathetoid-like movements of the fingers and toes, and slow, writhing movements of the trunk. Although seriously disabling TD is uncommon, a small proportion may affect walking, breathing, eating, and talking.

The differential diagnosis of TD includes a number of disorders of the basal ganglia (including Huntington disease, Wilson disease, and Sydenham chorea), striatal hypercalcifications, hyperthyroidism, hypoparathyroidism, tardive Tourette disorder, and dyskinesias related to other drugs, such as L-dopa and amphetamines. Abnormal movements were also described in patients with schizophrenia before the introduction of antipsychotic medications.

Although dopamine-blocking medications will frequently suppress or reduce TD, dose increases are only appropriate when the movements are severe and incapacitating. If patients have their dosages of antipsychotic medication either decreased or discontinued, abnormal movements may worsen temporarily or appear for the first time and then diminish, termed withdrawal emergent dyskinesias. Antiparkinsonian medications will often worsen TD. However, when patients suffer from combined TD and acute EPS, prescribing antiparkinsonian medication is often unavoidable.

Although the biologic mechanism underlying TD is still controversial, it is likely that the disorder is related to an increased sensitivity of dopamine receptors in the basal ganglia. This is supported by the observation that drugs that block dopamine, such as antipsychotic medications, suppress TD, whereas dopamine agonists worsen the disorder. These observations are consistent with findings from preclinical laboratories indicating that blocking dopamine receptors can result in their upward regulation.

Approximately 10% to 20% of patients treated with dopamine blockers for more than a year develop TD. In chronically institutionalized patients, the prevalence of TD is between 15% and 20% but increases with each year of treatment. Recent prospective studies indicate that approximately 4% to 5% of patients who are undergoing antipsychotic drug treatment, particularly with dopamine D2 receptor antagonists, will develop TD each year, with this risk markedly compounded in older adult individuals whose first treatment with antipsychotics is after age 65. Certain populations are at a greater risk than others for developing TD. Older adult women are particularly vulnerable. Moreover, TD in older adults is less likely to remit when dopamine blockers are discontinued. Patients with affective disorders may also be at a greater risk of TD when they are treated with dopamine receptor antagonists. Other possible risk factors for TD are the dosage of antipsychotic medications and the duration of treatment. There is also evidence indicating that the presence of acute EPS increases the risk of developing TD later.

### DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS

Differential diagnosis of tic disorders can be challenging ([Table 47–3](#tt47-3)). Medical and family histories, physical and neurologic examinations, and baseline laboratory testing enable clinicians to rule out potential causes of *de novo* tics such as infection, trauma, substance use, or toxin ingestion. Diagnostic hallmarks of tic disorders include the waxing-and-waning natural history, onset in childhood or adolescence, repetitive, rapid, nonrhythmic phenomenology, and involuntary features. In the absence of at least some history of simple tics, the diagnosis of a tic disorder may be questioned. Furthermore, the presence of vocal tics is helpful in ruling out other disorders because phonic manifestations are rare in other neurologic conditions. Tics must be distinguished from other types of abnormal movements: myoclonus, tremors, chorea, athetosis, dystonias, akathisia, and compulsive rituals.

Diagnostic considerations among the movement disorders include Sydenham chorea, poststreptococcal neurologic complications such as choreiform, writhing, and truncal movements; Huntington disease, an autosomal dominant disorder presenting with chorea and dementia and with an onset typically in the fourth or fifth decade; Parkinson disease, typically a late-life disorder, characterized by flattened facies, bradykinesia, rigidity, cogwheeling, and “pill-rolling” resting tremor; Wilson disease, an autosomal recessive disorder of copper metabolism that presents with liver abnormalities and neurologic symptoms: early dementia, mood disorders or psychosis, asterixis (a flapping tremor of the hands); Parkinsonism; structural diseases such as hemiballismus associated with subthalamic nucleus impairment; and neuroacanthocytosis, a rare movement disorder marked by progressive muscle weakness and atrophy, progressive cognitive loss, chorea, acanthocytosis (spiked red blood cells), and, sometimes, facial tics. In psychiatric settings, patients who have been exposed to neuroleptics are at risk for neuroleptic-related dyskinesias, including tardive and withdrawal dyskinesias. It is important to remember that dyskinesias may also be observed in tic disorder patients secondary to neuroleptic therapy but may be easily overlooked.

### TARDIVE DYSKINESIA

#### Clinical Presentation

Symptoms of TD should develop after at least 2 months of exposure to a dopamine-blocking agent (or 1 month if the patient is 60 years or older); while the patient is on medication or within 4 weeks of withdrawal from an oral agent (8 weeks from long-acting injectable medication); and be present for at least 4 weeks. The patient usually demonstrates involuntary movements of the tongue (e.g., twisting, protrusion); jaw (e.g., chewing); lips (e.g., smacking, puckering); trunk; or extremities. Patterns of movements may include rapid, jerky, and nonrepetitive movements (i.e., choreiform); slow, continuous, and sinuous movements (i.e., athetoid); or rhythmic movements.

The onset of the disorder may be gradual, occurring over a period of months to years. Symptoms are often seen in older patients who have poorly fitting dentures or other reasons for dyskinetic movements. In older patients (55 years or older), the onset of TD is about 25% after 1 year, 34% after 2 years, and 53% after 3 years. This rate of onset is three to five times higher than in younger patients. Patients with multi-infarct dementia and a psychiatric diagnosis are at greater risk than patients with Alzheimer disease or other organic mental disorders. As noted earlier, TD may also occur in never-medicated patients with schizophrenia.

Risk factors include increasing age, pre-existing parkinsonism, previous brain damage, antipsychotic treatment duration, and exposure to FGAs. These symptoms should not be due to a general medical or neurologic disorder or present as an adverse effect of another class of medication.

#### Pathophysiology

Sustained D<sub>2</sub> receptor blockade resulting in receptor hypersensitivity is the most common hypothesis explaining the development of TD. Clozapine’s decreased propensity to induce TD and its multiple neuroreceptor effects (e.g., binding to 5-HT<sub>1C</sub>, 5-HT<sub>2</sub>, and 5-HT<sub>3</sub> receptors) suggest a role for serotonin as well. Serotonin-containing neurons interact with dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra and ventral tegmental areas. Genetic studies indicate a possible relationship with polymorphisms in the DA2 receptor, DA3 receptor, dopamine transporter (DAT1), and the serotonin 2A receptor genes. Oxidative stress and cell death secondary to increased glutamatergic neurotransmission caused by blockade of presynaptic dopamine receptors are also postulated. Other potential proposed theories involve GABA depletion and cholinergic deficiency.

#### Treatment Options and Implications

Since the advent of SGAs, fewer episodes and less severe symptoms of TD are now seen. For this reason, patients on FGAs are generally *switched to a newer agent if possible*. Further, there is evidence that agents such as clozapine may improve symptoms of TD without causing progression of the disorder. For example, some studies of clozapine report reduced symptom severity in tardive syndromes by up to 50%. Patients on an FGA who develop TD and are switched to an SGA have demonstrated improvement in their symptoms but which specific agents are preferable remains unclear. Although a small percentage of cases progress to florid and potentially disabling or life-threatening intensity, others remain stable, and some show spontaneous improvement.

#### Tardive Dyskinesia and Other Tardive Syndromes

As with EPS, TD is less common with the SGAs than with conventional drugs, although there is probably a range of risks within both groups of drugs. Prospective studies lasting >6 months demonstrate a significantly lower risk of TD with SGAs (especially aripiprazole and olanzapine) than haloperidol. These results were somewhat expected in that early occurring EPS are significant risk factors for TD. However, the risk of TD is not absent with SGAs, and clinicians must recognize and manage TD.

TD commonly consists of abnormal, involuntary movements of the mouth, face and tongue, trunk, and extremities. The oral-facial movements occur in about three-fourths of TD patients and can include lip smacking, sucking, puckering, and facial grimacing or irregular movements of the limbs, particularly choreoathetoid-like movements of the fingers and toes, and slow, writhing movements of the trunk. Younger patients with TD tend to develop slower athetoid movements of the trunk, extremities, and neck, but coexistence with tardive dystonic movements is not uncommon.

The abnormal movements of TD are usually reduced by voluntary movements of the affected areas and are increased by voluntary movements of unaffected areas increased with emotional arousal and absent during sleep. According to the *Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders* (DSM-5), the abnormal movements should be present for at least 4 weeks, and patients should have been exposed to a postsynaptic dopamine receptor blocker for at least 3 months (at least one month in the older adults), and the onset of the abnormal movements should occur either while the patient is receiving the drug or within 4 weeks of discontinuing an oral or 8 weeks after the withdrawal of an LAI.

Prevalence surveys indicate that 25% to 30% of patients chronically treated with an FGA and 7% to 20% of patients chronically treated with an SGA will exhibit symptoms of TD. Three to five percent of young patients receiving an FGA develop TD each year. The risk in older adult patients is much higher. Although seriously disabling dyskinesia is uncommon, in a small proportion of cases walking, breathing, eating, and talking are affected.
