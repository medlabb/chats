### CHRONIC DISORDERS


#### ALCOHOLIC KORSAKOFF AMNESTIC SYNDROME

The Korsakoff amnestic syndrome, which occurs in chronic alcoholism and other malnutrition states, is thought to result from [thiamine](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660168) deficiency leading to bilateral degeneration of the dorsomedial thalamic nuclei. It is usually preceded by one or more episodes of Wernicke encephalopathy (see [Chapter 4](/content.aspx?legacysectionid=greenberg11_ch4), Confusional States), and is often associated with other manifestations of [thiamine](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660168) deficiency, such as nystagmus, gait ataxia, or polyneuropathy. The main defect is an inability to form new memories, resulting in significant impairment of short-term memory. Long-term memory is also frequently affected, although to a lesser extent, and registration is intact. Patients are typically apathetic and lack insight into their disorder. They may attempt to reassure the physician that no impairment exists and try to explain away their inability to remember. **Confabulation** is often, but not invariably, a feature.

Korsakoff syndrome can be prevented by prompt administration of [thiamine](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660168) to patients with Wernicke encephalopathy. Patients with established Korsakoff syndrome should also receive [thiamine](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660168) to prevent progression, although existing deficits are unlikely to be reversed.


#### POSTENCEPHALITIC AMNESIA

Patients who recover from acute viral encephalitis, particularly that caused by **herpes simplex virus** (see [Chapter 4](/content.aspx?legacysectionid=greenberg11_ch4), Confusional States), may be left with a permanent and static memory disorder. It resembles Korsakoff syndrome in chronic alcoholics in that inability to form new memories is its outstanding feature. Remote memories are affected to a lesser extent than recent ones, and registration is intact. Confabulation may occur. Often there is total amnesia for the period of the acute encephalitis.

Patients may also exhibit other symptoms of limbic system disease. These include docility, indifference, flatness of mood and affect, inappropriate jocularity and sexual allusions, hyperphagia, impotence, repetitive stereotyped motor activity, and the absence of goal-oriented activity. Complex partial seizures, with or without secondary generalization, may occur.
