# 30-04: Metabolic Bone Disease

The term “metabolic bone disease” refers to conditions producing diffuse reductions or increases in bone density that affect bone integrity. **Conditions causing reduced bone density** include osteopenia/osteoporosis and osteomalacia. Osteoporosis is characterized by reduction in both bone matrix and mineral, whereas in osteomalacia the bone matrix is intact but bone mineral is decreased. **Conditions causing increased bone density** are less common and include osteopetrosis and fluorosis. Disorders such as fibrous dysplasia and Paget disease of bone also involve disorganized bone and abnormal bone quality. Some metabolic bone disease includes disruption in [calcium](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659912) or [phosphate](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659912) homeostasis. Metabolic bone diseases may cause bone pain and fractures.

### A. Vitamin D Deficiency & Disorders

Vitamin D is predominantly synthesized in the skin during exposure to UV B light ([Table 30–3](#papacmdt26_ch30tb3)). Vitamin D is also consumed in the diet from plants ([ergocalciferol](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660039), D<sub>2</sub>) or animals/fish (cholecalciferol, D<sub>3</sub>). Both forms of vitamin D are converted in the liver to 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25-OHD); 25-OHD is subsequently converted in various tissues (mainly kidney) to 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D (1,25[OH]<sub>2</sub>D), the active hormone. 1,25(OH)<sub>2</sub>D binds to cytoplasmic vitamin D receptors, increasing the absorption of dietary [calcium](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659912) from the intestine and increasing the reabsorption of [calcium](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659912) in the renal tubule, thereby reducing [calcium](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659912) loss in the urine. 1,25(OH)<sub>2</sub>D also stimulates bone osteoblasts to release RANKL that stimulates osteoclasts, which release [calcium](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659912) from bone.

Vitamin D deficiency is the most common cause of osteomalacia. In some cases, its cause is low exposure to sunlight caused by urbanization, living at high latitudes, winter season, institutionalization, sunscreen use, or very modest dressing. Other risk factors for vitamin D deficiency include older age, obesity, dark skin, inadequate dietary intake (malnutrition, vegan diets), and intestinal malabsorption (due to pancreatic insufficiency, cholestatic liver disease, celiac disease, IBD, jejunoileal bypass, Billroth type II gastrectomy). [Orlistat](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660668) causes fat and vitamin D malabsorption. [Cholestyramine](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660219) binds bile acids necessary for vitamin D absorption. Patients with severe nephrotic syndrome lose large amounts of vitamin D–binding protein in the urine. Anticonvulsants (eg, [phenytoin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659866), [carbamazepine](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659978), valproate, [phenobarbital](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660306)) may inhibit the hepatic production of 25-OHD. [Phenytoin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659866) can also directly inhibit bone mineralization. About 36% of adults in the United States are deficient in vitamin D.

Vitamin D–dependent rickets type 1 is caused by a rare autosomal recessive disorder with a defect in the renal enzyme 1-α-hydroxylase leading to defective synthesis of 1,25(OH)<sub>2</sub>D. It presents in childhood with rickets and alopecia; osteomalacia develops in adults with this condition unless treated with oral [calcitriol](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660040) in doses of 0.5–1 mcg daily. Vitamin D–dependent rickets type 2 (hereditary 1,25[OH]<sub>2</sub>D-resistant rickets) is caused by a germline mutation in the 1,25(OH)<sub>2</sub>D receptor. Patients have hypocalcemia with childhood rickets and adult osteomalacia. Alopecia is common. These patients respond variably to oral [calcitriol](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660040) in very large doses (2–6 mcg daily).

### E. Disorders of Bone Matrix

Hypophosphatasia is a heritable rickets or osteomalacia characterized by low activity of the tissue-nonspecific isoenzyme of alkaline phosphatase. There is a range of severity, with perinatal, infantile, childhood, and adult presentations. Adult hypophosphatasia can present in adulthood with premature loss of teeth, metatarsal stress fractures, thigh pain due to femoral pseudofractures, or arthritis due to chondrocalcinosis. Bone density is low, and the osteomalacia is commonly misdiagnosed as osteoporosis. Serum alkaline phosphatase is low (below 40 U/L in adults and often < 20 U/L in severe cases). The differential diagnosis includes other causes of low serum alkaline phosphatase, such as early pregnancy, hypothyroidism, myeloma, severe anemia, or vitamin D intoxication; alkaline phosphatase may also be low in a patient who has been treated with an antiresorptive medication like a bisphosphonate.

Fibrogenesis imperfecta ossium is a rare condition that sporadically affects middle-aged patients, who present with progressive bone pain and pathologic fractures. Bones have a dense “fishnet” appearance on radiographs. Serum alkaline phosphatase levels are elevated. Some patients have a monoclonal gammopathy, indicating a possible plasma cell dyscrasia causing an impairment in osteoblast function and collagen disarray.

## GENERAL CONSIDERATIONS

Paget disease of bone is manifested by one or more localized bony lesions having high bone turnover and disorganized osteoid formation. The involved bone first has increased osteoclast activity, causing lytic lesions that may progress at about 1 cm/year. Increased osteoblastic activity follows, producing a high rate of disorganized bone formation. Involved bones become vascular, weak, and deformed. Eventually, there is a final burned-out phase with markedly reduced bone cell activity and abnormal bones that may be enlarged with skeletal deformity.

The prevalence of Paget disease has declined over the recent decades. It is most common in the United Kingdom and in areas of European migration, and it is rare in Africa, Asia, and Scandinavia. In the United States, Paget disease affects about 1% of White persons over age 55 years, with it increasing prevalence over age 40 years. Most cases are discovered incidentally during radiology imaging or because of incidentally discovered elevations in serum alkaline phosphatase.

Paget disease of bone may be due to a viral infection in genetically susceptible individuals, but the precise cause is unknown. About 20% of cases are familial and transmitted as an autosomal dominant trait with incomplete penetrance. Mutations in the *SQSTM1* gene have been discovered in about 35% of patients with familial Paget disease and in 7% of patients with apparently sporadic Paget disease.

## DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS

Familial expansile osteolysis, familial early-onset Paget disease, and familial skeletal hyperphosphatasia are autosomal dominant disorders caused by different tandem duplications of the gene encoding RANK, resulting in its constitutive activation. Juvenile Paget disease is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by an inactivating mutation in the gene encoding osteoprotegerin. The syndrome of Paget disease, inclusion body myopathy, and frontotemporal dementia is caused by a mutation in the gene that encodes valosin-containing protein. The differential diagnosis also includes myelofibrosis, intramedullary osteosclerosis, Erdheim-Chester disease, Langerhans cell histiocytosis, and sickle cell disease.

Paget disease must be differentiated from primary bone lesions (eg, osteogenic sarcoma, plasma cell myeloma, and fibrous dysplasia) and from secondary bone lesions (eg, osteitis fibrosa cystica and metastatic carcinoma to bone). Rare familial types of sclerosing bone dysplasias share phenotypic homologies with Paget disease of bone. Fibrogenesis imperfecta ossium is a rare symmetric disorder that can mimic the features of Paget disease; serum alkaline phosphatase is likewise elevated. This condition may be associated with paraproteinemias.
