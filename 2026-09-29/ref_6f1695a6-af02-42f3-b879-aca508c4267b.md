### TARDIVE DYSTONIA/TARDIVE AKATHISIA

#### Clinical Presentation

Tardive dystonia and tardive akathisia are movement symptoms that arise later in treatment with an antipsychotic and may persist for months or years despite the reduction or discontinuation of the patient’s medication. Young male patients have a higher risk. This syndrome is differentiated from acute dystonia and acute akathisia based on the time to onset. Both tardive dystonia and akathisia are associated with a higher incidence of TD.

#### Pathophysiology

There is little known about the pathophysiology of tardive dystonia and tardive akathisia. Tardive dystonia appears to involve impairment of the basal ganglia, and tardive akathisia may involve dysfunction of the pontine region of the brain.

#### Treatment Options and Implications

Both of these movement disorders are preventable with the use of SGAs (e.g., clozapine; quetiapine). If the patient has symptoms while on or after discontinuing an antipsychotic, an increase in its dose or immediate reintroduction of the agent may be necessary to increase dopamine blockade. Such interventions can achieve short-term relief.

One potential treatment for refractory tardive dystonia is DBS. In one report, 60 patients with symptoms of various types of dystonia underwent DBS with leads placed in the globus pallidus pars interna. Using the Burke–Fahn–Marsden Dystonia Rating Scale (BFM), patients were assessed preoperatively, postoperatively, and for up to 5 years of follow-up. Improvement in the BFM severity and disability scores were 43% and 27%, respectively, by 6 months and were sustained thereafter. Thus, DBS may be a potential option for patients with severe, treatment-refractory, medication-induced tardive dystonia.

#### Treatment Options and Implications

Patients should always be informed of the risk of muscle spasms associated with these drugs and encouraged to report such events immediately. With severe reactions or laryngeal dystonia, which can compromise breathing, immediate recognition and treatment are essential. Parenteral (intramuscular [IM] or IV) benztropine (1 to 2 mg) or diphenhydramine (50 mg) typically produce rapid relief. Prophylactic use of oral anticholinergic medication, as well as the ready availability of the parenteral form, is strongly encouraged when the use of a high-potency FGA is considered.

Anticholinergic medication has the greatest effect on antipsychotic-induced acute dystonia, less of an effect on tardive dystonia, and little effect on idiopathic dystonia. Because tardive dystonia can be a particularly disabling disorder, there is some support for the use of clozapine which may even improve this condition. Botulinum toxin (Botox) injections also offer symptomatic improvement for tardive dystonia. The prevalence of this adverse effect diminishes when acute agitation is treated with a combination of intramuscular haloperidol (e.g., 2 to 5 mg) plus lorazepam (Ativan) (e.g., 2 mg). It is likely that both the lower haloperidol dose and lorazepam’s sedative and peripheral muscle-relaxant effects contribute to the attenuation of this painful and frightening event. Ziprasidone (Geodon), olanzapine (Zyprexa), and aripiprazole all have approved acute IM formulations, which appear to carry a lower risk for acute dystonia and may be alternatives to haloperidol plus lorazepam.

<small>ACUTE DYSTONIA.</small>

Acute dystonia is characterized by a spastic contraction of muscle groups and often affects the neck, eyes, and trunk. These involuntary muscle contractions can be painful and emerge suddenly, thereby frightening the patient. In rare cases, the contractions can be life-threatening (e.g., laryngospasm). The various forms of dystonia include torticollis, retrocollis, trismus or lockjaw, grimacing, dysarthria, blepharospasm, oculogyric crisis, and opisthotonos. Acute dystonia frequently develops after rapid dose escalation of antipsychotic medication or at the beginning of regular treatment, oftentimes within just a few days.

Dystonic reactions are typically treated with benztropine (1 to 2 mg) or diphenhydramine (25 to 50 mg), either IM or IV. The onset of action of these medications, IM or IV, is rapid, with a resolution of the symptoms often within 10 to 15 minutes. As dystonic reactions may recur, prophylaxis with oral medications such as benztropine (1 to 2 mg twice daily) or trihexyphenidyl (2 mg three times a day) is recommended. Tardive dystonia may develop after months or years of treatment with antipsychotic medications and usually does not respond rapidly to anticholinergic medication.

<small>AKATHISIA.</small>

Akathisia is characterized by an internal sense of motor restlessness, often described as an inability to resist the urge to move. The most common forms of akathisia involve compulsive fidgeting, rocking back and forth, and pacing, and it most often occurs during the first few months of conventional antipsychotic use. At times, patients may have difficulty clearly describing the sensation of akathisia, which can lead to the misdiagnosis of agitation and an increase in antipsychotic medication—hence worsening the akathisia. Anticholinergics have limited utility in the treatment of akathisia; they should be considered second-line therapy and used when first-line agents, such as adrenergic receptor antagonists (propranolol), either fail or are contraindicated.

<small>PARKINSONISM.</small>

Parkinson disease and secondary parkinsonism arising from the use of antipsychotics can be indistinguishable. Secondary parkinsonism is more common with high-potency antipsychotics and is seen much less frequently with atypical antipsychotics. These symptoms may manifest at any time during antipsychotic treatment but usually begin within the first 3 months of treatment and can be managed by lowering the antipsychotic dose to the lowest effective dose. Symptoms include generalized slowing of movement (akinesia), rigidity, resting tremor, hypersalivation, cogwheeling, and hypomimia (masked facies), and are caused by a blockade of DA receptors of nigrostriatal neurons, which terminate in the caudate nucleus. In some cases, these symptoms may also be mistaken for the negative symptoms of schizophrenia.
