### A. Medical Treatment

Medical therapy may be general (focused on overall management of the pain) or disease-specific (focused on specific underlying etiologies of pain). The choice of medical therapy should be influenced by side-effect profiles as well as the patient's pregnancy intent.

General therapies include medications that are often used for the treatment of chronic pain syndromes. Initial treatment may begin with simple analgesics, such as [acetaminophen](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659831) or NSAIDs. Some women may also benefit from neuromodulators, including tricyclic antidepressants, duloxetine, [gabapentin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660534), and [pregabalin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=658955). Guidelines acknowledge the limited evidence for the effectiveness of these treatments for the management of chronic pelvic pain; the potential for side effects (such as GI and renal side effects with NSAIDs) may limit long-term use of some treatments. A large, randomized controlled trial of women with chronic pelvic pain demonstrated that side effects of gabapentin—dizziness, drowsiness, and visual disturbances—were common while pain control in the [gabapentin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660534) group was no better than in the placebo group. However, smaller trials have demonstrated benefit in some patients at 24 weeks of [gabapentin](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660534) treatment. Hormonal therapies are used primarily for treating gynecologic sources of chronic pelvic pain. Women with suspected endometriosis can be empirically treated with hormonal therapy before diagnostic laparoscopy is performed. Combined oral contraceptives, [progestins](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660152) (oral, injectable, and intrauterine), gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) analogs, and [danazol](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660179) are effective for treating the dysmenorrhea associated with endometriosis, although the side effects vary. The effects on BMD associated with GnRH analog therapy can be mitigated by “add-back” low-dose hormonal therapy ([norethindrone](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660222), low-dose estrogen, or a combination of estrogen and [progesterone](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660110)), which may also provide symptomatic relief for associated hot flashes and vaginal symptoms. [Danazol](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660179) may be associated with severe androgenic side effects, including hirsutism, acne, and weight changes; therefore, it is not used as first-line therapy. For women who have endometriosis and desire pregnancy, NSAIDs may provide symptomatic relief.

A Cochrane systematic review and meta-analysis investigated the effectiveness of noninvasive treatments for the management of chronic pelvic pain unrelated to endometriosis, primary dysmenorrhea, chronic pelvic inflammatory disease, or irritable bowel syndrome. Treatment with [medroxyprogesterone](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660109), compared to placebo, was associated with a reduction in pain that was maintained up to 9 months after therapy; side effects included weight gain and bloating.

Musculoskeletal sources of chronic pelvic pain include pelvic floor myalgia, which is related to spasm of the levator ani muscles, and myofascial trigger points. Treatments include referral to pelvic physical therapy and, if needed, trigger point injections with a local anesthetic or botulinum A toxin, although evidence supporting these therapies are mixed.



Treatment of irritable bowel syndrome and interstitial cystitis/bladder pain syndrome are discussed in [Parts 17-33](/content.aspx?legacysectionid=papacmdt26_ch17-33) and [26-04](/content.aspx?legacysectionid=papacmdt26_ch26-04).

## COMPLICATIONS

Perforation occurs in 20% of patients and should be suspected in patients with pain persisting for > 36 hours, high fever, diffuse abdominal tenderness or peritoneal findings, a palpable abdominal mass, or marked leukocytosis. #### 3. Anticonstipation agents

Treatment with oral osmotic laxatives such as polyethylene glycol 3350 (MiraLAX, 17–34 g/day) may increase stool frequency, improve stool consistency, and reduce straining but do not improve abdominal pain. [Lactulose](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660043) and [sorbitol](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660175) produce increased flatus and distention and should be avoided in patients with IBS. For patients with inadequate response to polyethylene glycol, several agents are available that may yield modest improvement in stool frequency and form, abdominal pain, and global IBS symptoms. The secretagogues [lubiprostone](/drug.aspx?gbosId=658964) (8 mcg orally twice daily), [linaclotide](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659164) (290 mcg orally once daily), [plecanatide](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659304) (3 mg orally once daily), and tenapanor (50 mg orally twice daily) are FDA approved for treatment of IBS with constipation based on modest demonstrated efficacy and are recommended in the 2021 ACG and 2022 AGA guidelines. Through different mechanisms, these agents stimulate increased fluid secretion, resulting in accelerated colonic transit. In clinical trials, [lubiprostone](/drug.aspx?gbosId=658964) led to global symptom improvement in 18% of patients compared with 10% of patients who received placebo (a therapeutic gain of 8%). Using different FDA-approved endpoints for significant clinical response (30% reduction in abdominal pain and more than three spontaneous bowel movements per week), phase 3 trials of [linaclotide](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659164) and [plecanatide](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659304) have demonstrated similar therapeutic gains: [linaclotide](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659164) 12.5% versus placebo 4% and [plecanatide](/drug.aspx?gbosId=659304) 26% versus placebo 16%. Tenapanor is FDA-approved for the treatment of IBS with constipation. It is a locally acting small molecule that inhibits the Na<sup>+</sup>/H<sup>+</sup> exchange pump on the apical surface of the small and large intestine, increasing fluid secretion into the intestinal lumen. In phase 3 clinical trials, 34.7% of patients treated with tenapanor met the FDA-approved endpoint versus 21.7% treated with placebo. Diarrhea led to tenapanor discontinuation in 6.5%. Tegaserod is a 5-HT<sub>4</sub>-receptor agonist that stimulates peristalsis; however, it was voluntarily withdrawn from the market in 2007 because of cardiovascular safety concerns. In 2019, after evaluation of clinical and safety data from 29 placebo-controlled trials and newer treatment outcome data, tegaserod (6 mg orally twice daily) was reapproved by the FDA for women under age 65 with IBS and constipation. It may be considered for treatment of women age younger than 65 years with one or fewer cardiovascular risk factors whose IBS with constipation has not improved with secretagogue therapy. Patients with intractable constipation should undergo further assessment for slow colonic transit and pelvic floor dysfunction (see [Constipation](/content.aspx?legacysectionid=papacmdt26_ch17-04), above).

#### 4. Psychotropic agents

Patients with predominant symptoms of pain or bloating may benefit from low doses of tricyclic antidepressants, which are believed to have effects on motility, visceral sensitivity, and central pain perception that are independent of their psychotropic effects. Because of their anticholinergic effects, these agents may be more useful in patients with diarrhea-predominant than constipation-predominant symptoms. Oral [nortriptyline](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660191), [desipramine](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660068), or [imipramine](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660070) may be started at a low dosage of 10 mg at bedtime and increased gradually to a maximum of 30–50 mg as tolerated. Response rates do not correlate with dosage, and many patients respond to doses of 50 mg or less daily. Higher doses used in treatment of depression (150–300 mg daily) are not needed for treatment of GI symptoms. Side effects are common, and lack of efficacy with one agent does not preclude benefit from another. Agents with higher anticholinergic activity may improve diarrhea but worsen constipation. Improvement should be evident within 4 weeks. The oral SSRIs ([sertraline](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660630), 25–100 mg daily; [citalopram](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660661), 10–20 mg; [paroxetine](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660655), 20–50 mg daily; or [fluoxetine](/drug.aspx?gbosId=660469), 10–40 mg daily) may be used to treat irritable bowel symptoms as well as treat mood disorders. SSRIs may accelerate GI transit and improve constipation. Anxiolytics should not be used chronically in IBS because of their habituation potential. Patients with major depression or anxiety disorders should be identified and treated with therapeutic doses of appropriate agents.
