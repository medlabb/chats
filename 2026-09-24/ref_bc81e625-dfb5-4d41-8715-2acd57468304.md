# Prevention



Numerous strategies have been tested for the prevention of preeclampsia, including protein or salt restriction, zinc,magnesium, fish oil, diuretics, antihypertensive medications, heparin, aspirin, calcium, and vitamins. Results from most of these trials showed little to no benefit for preeclampsia prevention. 1 Calcium, aspirin, and antioxidant vitamin therapy have been evaluated. A common theme has been promising results from small, single-center studies followed by larger, appropriately powered, randomized trials that failed to show a significant benefit in preventing preeclampsia or other adverse pregnancy outcomes.

Aspirin, an antiplatelet agent with anti-inflammatory properties, has been tested extensively for the prevention of preeclampsia in both low- and high-risk women. Evidence from randomized trials clearly shows that prophylactic administration of aspirin to pregnant women has modest ability to reduce the risk of developing preeclampsia and its sequelae. A 2019 meta-analysis of 74 trials including more than 40,000 women reported reduction in proteinuric preeclampsia, fetal or neonatal death, preterm birth less than 37 weeks, SGA infants, and a composite of serious adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes. 65 A 2021 meta-analysis by the US Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) reported similar data, including a lower risk of preeclampsia (RR 0.85), perinatal mortality (RR 0.79), preterm birth (RR 0.80), and intrauterine growth restriction (RR 0.82) with no increase in bleeding harms. The greatest benefit appears to be in women at moderate to high risk of developing preeclampsia. 66 The USPSTF and ACOG recommend aspirin prophylaxis for the following high-risk conditions: previous pregnancy with preeclampsia, pregestational diabetes, chronic hypertension, multifetal gestation, renal disease, and autoimmune disease with potential vascular complications (systemic lupus, antiphospholipid antibody syndrome). 2 , 66 Two or more moderate risk factors would also warrant aspirin for preeclampsia prevention such as nulliparity, obesity, family history of preeclampsia in mother or sister, age ≥35 years, as a few examples. Ideally, aspirin is initiated at 12 weeks and prior to 16 weeks and continued throughout the pregnancy. In general, aspirin 81 mg daily is used, with emerging evidence suggesting a benefit of doses of 100 to 150 mg, and possibly 162 mg. 67

Oxidative stress has been posited as a pathogenic factor in preeclampsia; thus antioxidant vitamins have been proposed for the primary prevention of preeclampsia. Despite initial, promising results from a small study in high-risk women, large, randomized, placebo-controlled trials of vitamins C and E did not reduce the risk of preeclampsia or improve maternal or fetal outcomes in various low- and high-risk populations. A Cochrane systematic review of vitamins C and E for the prevention of preeclampsia that included more than 20,000 women enrolled in 15 randomized clinical trials found no benefit (RR 0.94, 95% CI 0.82–1.07). 68 Therefore antioxidant therapies are not currently recommended for preeclampsia prevention.

Calcium supplementation for the prevention of preeclampsia has also been studied extensively. In a multicenter, randomized, placebo-controlled trial of healthy, nulliparous women conducted in the United States, calcium supplementation of 2 g/day did not reduce the incidence or severity of preeclampsia or delay its onset. However, a Cochrane meta-analysis of 13 trials, which included 15,730 women, demonstrated a significant reduction in preeclampsia risk with calcium supplementation (RR 0.45, 95% CI 0.31–0.65), with the greatest effect among women with low calcium intake at baseline (RR 0.36, 95% CI 0.20–0.65). 69 In aggregate, the evidence suggests a beneficial effect of calcium supplementation of 1.5 to 2 g/day for preeclampsia prevention in populations with low calcium intake at baseline.

Lifestyle modifications such as activity level have also been considered in the prevention of preeclampsia. Evidence to support bed rest is limited to small studies that have not adequately addressed other important adverse pregnancy outcomes and side effects of bed rest such as venous thromboembolism. 2 Physical activity has been hypothesized to reduce the risk of preeclampsia by improving vascular function as well as promoting placental angiogenesis. Although several small studies have evaluated modest exercise in the prevention of preeclampsia, results are inconclusive, and larger, well-controlled studies are needed to fully evaluate the effect of exercise on adverse pregnancy outcomes. 1

Further research is warranted to identify pathophysiologic subtypes of preeclampsia that may benefit from targeted preventive therapies.

# Prevention



There are no to very few preventive strategies for FGR. Counseling regarding modifiable risk factors (e.g., diet and cigarette smoking during pregnancy and malarial prophylaxis in high-risk areas) may be somewhat effective in mitigating FGR. Outside of these interventions, no effective preventive strategy has been identified. Nutritional and dietary supplementation regimens such as increased consumption of specific food groups; a low-salt diet; and iron, zinc, calcium, protein, or vitamin D supplementation have not been effective. Bed rest is not recommended for suspected FGR. 5 There is some preliminary evidence that aspirin may prevent severe FGR in the setting of abnormal umbilical artery Doppler studies. Studies have demonstrated that the aspirin-treated group was associated with a greater birth weight, prolonged gestation, and decreased need for admission to the neonatal intensive care unit when compared with the control group. However, aspirin is not routinely indicated at this time for the prevention of FGR and stillbirth given that studies are small and not conclusive. 43 Low-dose aspirin is recommended for women at high risk for preeclampsia, a common cause of FGR and stillbirth.
